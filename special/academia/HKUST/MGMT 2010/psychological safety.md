# psychological safety

Psychological safety is the shared belief that a person can speak up, ask a question, admit a mistake, or challenge an idea without being punished or humiliated. The belief belongs to the team, not to one member.

Google's Project Aristotle found psychological safety the single strongest predictor of effective teams.

Safety is not comfort. Speaking candidly can still be uncomfortable once it is safe.

- how psychological safety differs from comfort ::@:: The freedom to be candid, not comfort: a candid remark can still be uncomfortable to make.
- what speaking up means in a team ::@:: Raising a question, a doubt, a mistake, or an objection aloud rather than keeping it, without fear of punishment or humiliation.

## groupthink

Groupthink is what a group does when harmony and consensus matter so much that it stops testing its own ideas. Doubts go unspoken, and the loudest or most senior view wins.

Four warning signs mark it:

- __Illusion of unanimity__: no one objects, so the group reads silence as agreement.
- __Self-censorship__: a member with a doubt keeps it.
- __Pressure on dissenters__: whoever speaks against the group is pushed back.
- __Belief the group cannot be wrong__: the group treats its conclusion as beyond question.

## low and high safety

A team with low psychological safety reads a question as ignorance, hides its mistakes, and leaves bias unchallenged. A team with high psychological safety reads it as curiosity, surfaces errors early, and voices a difference of view.

- in a team with high psychological safety, how a question is read ::@:: As curiosity.
- what a team with high psychological safety does with its errors ::@:: Errors surface early.

## trust

Psychological safety rests on trust, the confidence that a teammate's words, ability, and intentions will hold. __Consistency__ means words and actions match, repeatedly and predictably. __Competence__ means people believe a person can do what they say they will. __Care__ means intentions are read as good, even when the feedback is hard.

Bias, a leaning for or against a person that their work does not justify, erodes trust. Unequal credit, uneven feedback, and invitations that never arrive add up, and none looks like an attack at the time.

## inclusion

Diversity is who is in the room. Inclusion is who gets heard in it, and it takes four practices:

- __Rotate the voice.__ Ask the quietest person first, then go round the table.
- __Slow the decision.__ Name one dissenting view before agreeing, and assign a devil's advocate.
- __Structure judgment.__ Put the same questions and criteria to everyone, written down first.
- __Credit precisely.__ Name who had the idea, and interrupt the interruption.

A devil's advocate argues the case against the option the group is about to accept, so its weaknesses surface while the group can still change course.

## chain from trust to inclusion

Each step rests on the one before it: trust is the foundation, psychological safety lets people speak, dissent breaks groupthink, and inclusion improves decisions. Dissent is a view spoken against what the group leans toward.
