# lecture

- HKUST MGMT 2010 week 3 lecture 1
- parent: [week 3 lecture 1](index.md)

Four exercises each name a bias and ask for a one-minute pitch or a rating, so the bias does its work before anyone names it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what each of the four exercise situations asks the reader to produce ::@:: A one-minute sales pitch or a rating of the person involved.
- why each exercise's bias has already done its work before it is named ::@:: The pitch or rating is settled first, and the bias is seen afterwards.
- the four biases the exercise situations are built on ::@:: Anchoring, confirmation bias, the halo effect, and the false consensus effect.

## anchoring bias

> __Prompt.__ You are selling an iPad mini, second hand. You bought it for $\$3{,}799$, had it for a month, used it twice, and want to sell it for $\$3{,}699$. What is your one minute sales pitch?

The price paid anchors the offer. It says nothing about what a used unit fetches today, so a pitch that runs "only used twice, so almost new, so barely below the purchase price" charges the buyer for the seller's decision. Arguing from the market for the same device in the same condition treats that price as sunk.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- anchoring bias, in judging what a used item is worth ::@:: Letting an early number pull every later estimate toward it.
- what anchors the offer on the iPad mini bought for 3,799 dollars ::@:: The 3,799 dollar purchase price, not the market for a used unit.
- what the 3,799 dollar purchase price says about a used unit's value today ::@:: Nothing, since it records the seller's own past payment.
- the pitch that runs used twice, so almost new, so barely below the purchase price ::@:: It charges the buyer for the seller's decision.
- what a resale pitch should argue from instead of the purchase price ::@:: The market for the same device in the same condition.
- what the 3,799 dollars becomes once the iPad mini has been bought ::@:: A sunk cost, spent already and not recoverable.

## confirmation bias

> __Prompt.__ You are a dietitian who believes strongly in an all vegetarian (plant-based) diet: fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds, and grains, possibly with dairy and eggs. Its benefits are a high fibre, vitamin, and antioxidant content and a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, and certain cancers. Its drawbacks are potential deficiencies in vitamins, iron, zinc, and calcium if the diet is not balanced. The goal is long-term health, sustainability, and ethical eating. What is your one minute sales pitch?

The brief lists the drawbacks. A pitch that runs through the benefits and ethics while dropping the deficiencies is confirmation bias at work. Every claim in it is true, and a client who later develops an iron deficiency was sold half the brief.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- confirmation bias, in a pitch for an all vegetarian diet ::@:: Keeping the points that support a belief one holds and dropping the rest.
- the drawbacks a plant-based diet brief names where the diet is unbalanced ::@:: Deficiencies in vitamins, iron, zinc and calcium.
- the nutrient benefits a plant-based diet brief claims ::@:: High fibre, vitamins and antioxidants.
- the diseases a plant-based diet brief says the diet lowers the risk of ::@:: Cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension and some cancers.
- the aims a plant-based diet brief sets besides health ::@:: Sustainability and ethical eating.
- which parts of the plant-based brief a benefits-only pitch keeps ::@:: The health benefits and the ethical case.
- what a benefits-only plant-based pitch does with the deficiencies ::@:: It drops them from the pitch.
- why every claim in a benefits-only plant-based pitch can still be true ::@:: The drawbacks it drops are true too, so nothing in it is false.
- what the client got from a pitch that ran only the plant-based benefits ::@:: Half of the brief the dietitian was given.

## halo effect

> __Prompt.__ A professor in the Division of Environment and Sustainability has been awarded HK$5.6 million from a collaborative research fund for a project on a sustainable AI datacenter with immersion cooling and generalised carbon estimation. How would you rate this professor for kindness: high, medium, or low?

The grant measures a proposal, not a person's conduct, so it says nothing about kindness. Rating kindness from it is the halo effect. Medium already claims more than the item holds, and the honest answer is no rating.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the halo effect, in rating a professor's kindness from a research grant ::@:: Letting one admired quality spill into judgements about unrelated ones.
- what the 5.6 million Hong Kong dollar collaborative research fund went to ::@:: A proposal for a sustainable artificial intelligence datacenter with immersion cooling.
- what the research grant says about how the professor treats people ::@:: Nothing, since it measures a proposal and not a person's conduct.
- why a kindness rating of medium is already too generous here ::@:: Even the middle rating asserts what the grant cannot support.
- the honest kindness rating for the professor holding the research grant ::@:: No rating at all.
- the kindness rating the halo effect talks a reader into ::@:: High, because the grant reads as general goodness.

## false consensus effect

> __Prompt.__ Social media is more helpful than harmful. Explain why this is the case in one minute.

The claim arrives as settled and the task is to defend it. That is the trap, since the premise is contested. False consensus makes a person's own view feel like the room's view, so the speaker argues the obvious side and skips the harm.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the false consensus effect, in carrying a view into a room ::@:: Overestimating how widely others share one's own view.
- how the social media claim is presented to the speaker ::@:: As settled ground that the one-minute answer has to defend.
- why the social media claim is a trap rather than a free point ::@:: The premise is contested, so defending it means arguing one side.
- what false consensus makes a speaker's own view feel like ::@:: The room's view.
- how false consensus shapes the one-minute social media answer ::@:: The speaker argues the obvious side and skips the harm.
- how to answer the claim that social media is more helpful than harmful ::@:: Name both sides, then say which weighs more and why.
