# lecture

- HKUST MGMT 2010 week 5 lecture 1
- parent: [week 5 lecture 1](index.md)

Eight cases in deontological reasoning, each written so that following the rule costs a great deal.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- how a deontologist answers a case whose every option breaks a duty ::@:: Name the duties in conflict, then ask which of them weighs more.
- what a prima facie duty is, before a case weighs it against others ::@:: A duty that binds unless a weightier duty overrides it.
- whether one prima facie duty always outranks the rest, whatever the case ::@:: No: any of them can be overridden by a heavier one.
- what makes a deontological case hard, where keeping the rule costs 500 families their jobs ::@:: The consequences do not decide it, so the rule still holds.
- the prima facie duty of fidelity, and what it covers besides a promise ::@:: Telling the truth, since a hearer relies on the words given.
- the prima facie duty of reparation, owed once a wrong has been done ::@:: Making good the wrong one has done.
- the prima facie duty of gratitude, owed to someone who has helped you ::@:: Repaying a benefit that one has received.
- the prima facie duty of justice, and what it gives to each person ::@:: Giving each person what is due to them.
- the prima facie duty of beneficence, and what it asks of the agent ::@:: Improving the condition of other people.
- the prima facie duty of non-maleficence, and the harm it forbids ::@:: Doing harm to other people.
- the humanity formulation, and the rule it puts on how a person may be treated ::@:: Never treat a person merely as a means, always also as an end.

## cases

> __Prompt.__ The whispering whistleblower. You discover your Singaporean company's beloved founder, who funded your education, has been illegally diverting minor environmental fees to keep the firm solvent. Reporting him will bankrupt 500 families and bring intense shame upon your family. Your parents beg you to destroy the evidence. What would a deontologist do?

Report the diversion and refuse to destroy the evidence. Gratitude to a benefactor is a prima facie duty, and it loses to the duty not to deceive the regulator and the public. Destroying the records is a second breach.

> __Prompt.__ The ancestral land grab. A developer offers a fortune for your family's ancestral farmland to build a medical research facility that will save millions. Your dying grandfather refuses to sign, citing ancestral duty and filial piety. The local government pressures you to forge their signature while semi-conscious in the hospital. What would a deontologist do?

Refuse to forge the signature. Forging treats the grandfather as a means to the facility, which the humanity formulation forbids. The lives it would save cannot buy the lie.

> __Prompt.__ The high-tech clan favour. Your cousin desperately needs a loan for a life-saving transplant but was rejected by an unfair, automated credit algorithm. He begs you, the chief security officer, to bypass the firewall and manually approve the loan. Refusing him violates deep clan obligations, but agreeing violates your signed employment contract. What would a deontologist do?

Refuse the bypass, and take the unfair algorithm to the people who can fix it. A signed contract is a promise, and fidelity to it outranks the clan obligation. The loan can still be approved another way, but a broken firewall stays broken.

> __Prompt.__ The exam proctor. You catch the daughter of the university's primary donor cheating on a final exam. If you report her, she is expelled, and her furious father will pull his funding, forcing a tuition spike that will cause dozens of low-income students to drop out. If you stay silent, you preserve their education. What would a deontologist do?

Report the cheating. Impartiality is the point of an invigilator, and silence exempts one student from a rule every other student keeps. The students who stay enrolled are a consequence the duty does not depend on.

> __Prompt.__ The poisonous factory spill. An equipment failure causes your chemical plant to slowly leak into a river, slightly lowering water quality for a nearby village. Fixing it immediately requires a plant shutdown, causing a massive financial hit that will result in the immediate layoff of 50 local factory workers who have no other employment. What would a deontologist do?

Shut the plant down and fix the leak. Non-maleficence toward the village outweighs beneficence toward the workers. The duty not to poison people does not shrink because the alternative is expensive.

> __Prompt.__ The plagiarized patent. You realise your Shenzhen biomedical colleague plagiarised key schematics from a deceased scientist to invent a revolutionary medical device. Exposing this will ruin the startup and halt a device destined to save thousands of lives. Staying silent brings your team massive acclaim and saves patients. What would a deontologist do?

Expose the plagiarism. The dead scientist's claim to the work is a matter of justice and reparation, and honesty about its origin does not wait on how many patients the device would treat.

> __Prompt.__ The toxic tech recycler. You manage a massive electronic waste recycling facility in an industrial zone in Vietnam. The local government offers your firm a highly lucrative contract to recycle toxic lithium batteries. You know your facility lacks the advanced ventilation systems required to protect your 30 immigrant workers from chemical fumes. If you refuse the contract, your firm faces bankruptcy, leaving these vulnerable workers unemployed and unable to send remittances home. What would a deontologist do?

Refuse the contract. Taking it uses the workers' lungs to win the fee, the clearest case of treating people as means. Beneficence toward their wages ranks below the duty not to poison them.

> __Prompt.__ The traditional medicine trial. You are a clinical researcher testing a traditional herbal remedy for chronic pain at a university in Hanoi. You realise the lead researcher, your respected academic mentor, has subtly omitted several instances of mild liver irritation from the initial data to ensure the drug passes to human trials. If you report them, the research grant is revoked, ending your academic career and bringing public disgrace to your department. What would a deontologist do?

Report the omissions. Trial participants are owed the information that would let them refuse, and honesty to them outweighs loyalty to a mentor and the career the report costs.
