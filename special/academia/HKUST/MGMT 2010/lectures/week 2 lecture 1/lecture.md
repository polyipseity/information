# lecture

- HKUST MGMT 2010 week 2 lecture 1
- parent: [week 2 lecture 1](index.md)

Ten hypothetical ethical dilemmas for a leader, each answered with one of six response styles, and a scoring sheet that turns the choices into a profile.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what the questionnaire measures about the person answering it ::@:: Their preference among six response styles, counted across ten dilemmas.
- how a leader answers an item on the questionnaire ::@:: By choosing the one of six reasons that best describes why they would act.

## response alternatives

The same six reasons answer every situation, word for word:

- __A. I would do what is right.__ Meeting responsibilities, fulfilling obligations, and following organisation policy, explicit or implicit.
- __B. I would do what benefits the most people.__ What helps the most people overall: the greatest good for the greatest number.
- __C. I would do what a good person would do.__ Excellence of character: integrity, faithfulness to one's principles, and virtues such as courage, honesty, and loyalty.
- __D. I would do what shows that I care about my close relationships.__ Maintaining caring relationships, meeting the needs of others, and giving special consideration to those one shares a bond with.
- __E. I would do what benefits me the most.__ Achieving one's goals, succeeding at the assigned task, advancing one's career, and other self-interest.
- __F. I would do what is fair.__ Distributing benefits and burdens to everyone equally, and treating others fairly.

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the six response styles of the questionnaire, and the letter each carries ::@:: Duty (A), utilitarian (B), virtue (C), caring (D), egoism (E), and justice (F).
- the duty response "I would do what is right" ::@:: Meeting responsibilities, fulfilling obligations, and following organisation policy, explicit or implicit.
- the utilitarian response "I would do what benefits the most people" ::@:: What helps the most people overall, and the greatest good for the greatest number.
- the virtue response "I would do what a good person would do" ::@:: Excellence of character: integrity, faithfulness to one's principles, and virtues such as courage, honesty, and loyalty.
- the caring response "I would do what shows that I care about my close relationships" ::@:: Maintaining caring relationships, meeting the needs of others, and giving special consideration to those one shares a bond with.
- the egoism response "I would do what benefits me the most" ::@:: Achieving one's goals, succeeding at the assigned task, advancing one's career, and other self-interest.
- the justice response "I would do what is fair" ::@:: Distributing benefits and burdens to everyone equally, and treating others fairly.

## situations

> __Prompt.__ 1. You are the manager of a manufacturing team and learn that your employees are falsifying product quality results to sell more products. If you report the matter, most of them will lose their jobs, you may lose yours, and your company will take a significant hit to its reputation. What would you do in this situation?

Reporting is the duty, since the rule against falsified results holds whatever follows. Silence is the utilitarian move, keeping several jobs and a reputation.

> __Prompt.__ 2. You have an employee who has been having performance problems, which is making it hard for your group to meet its work quota. This person was recommended to you as a solid performer. You now believe the person's former manager had problems with the employee and just wanted to get rid of the person. If you give the underperforming employee a good recommendation, leaving out the performance problems, you will have an opportunity to pass the employee off to another group. What would you do in this situation?

Passing the employee on is egoism, since the group's quota and one's own standing both improve. Duty and justice point the other way, because the next manager is owed an accurate reference.

> __Prompt.__ 3. Your team is struggling to complete a critical project. You hear about a job opening that would be much better for one of your key employees' careers. If this individual leaves the team, it would put the project in danger. What would you do in this situation?

Telling the employee is care, since the bond with a person outweighs the group's convenience. Withholding it is the utilitarian move, putting the project's delivery first.

> __Prompt.__ 4. An employee of yours has a child with a serious illness and is having trouble fulfilling obligations at work. You learn from your administrative assistant that this employee claimed 40 hours on a time sheet for a week when the employee actually only worked 30 hours. What would you do in this situation?

Justice applies one rule to every employee who claims unworked hours. Care asks what a single parent with a sick child needed.

> __Prompt.__ 5. You are a manager, and some of your employees can finish their quotas in much less than the allotted time to do so. If upper management becomes aware of this, they will want you to increase the quotas. Some of your employees are unable to meet their current quotas. What would you do in this situation?

Raising the quota is the utilitarian gain in output, and keeping quiet protects the few slow workers at the group's expense. Justice asks whether one quota is fair to people of different capacities.

> __Prompt.__ 6. You are an organisation's chief financial officer, and you are aware that the chief executive officer and other members of the senior leadership team want to provide exaggerated financial information to keep the company's stock price high. The entire senior management team holds significant stock positions. What would you do in this situation?

Accurate reporting is the duty: the rule against misleading investors does not bend to the share price. Egoism pulls the other way, since the senior team holds the stock and sets one's pay.

> __Prompt.__ 7. Two new employees have joined your accounting team right out of school. They are regularly found watching YouTube or gaming on their phones. Your accounting work regularly requires overtime at the end of the month to get the financial reports completed. These employees refuse to do any overtime, which shifts work to other team members. The other team members are getting resentful and upset. What would you do in this situation?

The resentment is a justice complaint, because the workload has stopped being shared fairly. Requiring the overtime is also the utilitarian fix, since the reports have a fixed deadline.

> __Prompt.__ 8. You are the manager of a neighbourhood food support shop for under privileged families (selling cheap or past use by date foods). A member, a single parent with three children, is caught shoplifting $200 in groceries from the shop. You suspect this person has been stealing for years. You consider pressing charges. What would you do in this situation?

Care points to the four people the groceries were for, and to a member the shop exists to help. Justice points to the other members, whose food the loss comes out of, and to the rule that keeps the shop open.

> __Prompt.__ 9. You have been accused of discriminating against a particular gender in your hiring practices. A new position opens, and you could hire a candidate of the gender you've been accused of discriminating against over a candidate of another gender, even though the latter candidate has slightly better qualifications. Hiring the former candidate would let you address this accusation and improve your reputation in the company. What would you do in this situation?

Hiring the better-qualified candidate is justice: the post is owed the best applicant. Hiring to answer the accusation is egoism, since the decision buys reputation rather than serving the post.

> __Prompt.__ 10. You are a professor. One of your best students buys an essay online and turns it in for a grade. Later in the term, the student begins to feel guilty and confesses to you that the paper was purchased. It is the norm at the university to fail a student guilty of plagiarism. You must decide if you will fail the student. What would you do in this situation?

Duty applies the university's rule to every case of plagiarism; the confession changes nothing about the act. Care and utilitarianism see a student who came forward, and ask whether a rule everyone else was held to can be waived for the one who confessed.

## scoring

Each letter is counted across the ten situations: A duty, B utilitarianism, C virtue, D care, E egoism, F justice. The six counts form a profile, not a total.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- how the questionnaire turns the ten answers into a score ::@:: Count the answers letter by letter, and read each count as the preference for that style.
- why the score is a profile rather than a total ::@:: The six counts measure six different preferences, so no single number ranks them.
