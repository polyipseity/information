# normative ethics

Normative ethics is the branch of ethics that asks how one ought to act. It sets a standard for right and wrong action, then applies that standard to a case. Because it tells a person what to do rather than reporting what people believe, it is sometimes called prescriptive ethics. Ethics and morals both name such a standard, and [ethics and morals](ethics%20and%20morals.md) part on where it comes from: ethics from an external source or social system, morals from a person's own compass.

## branches of ethics

Ethics divides into four lines of inquiry, and only one sets a standard. Metaethics studies the meaning of moral language and the metaphysics of moral facts. Applied ethics takes one particular issue, such as whether or when abortion is acceptable, or how a business should treat the people its decisions reach. Business conduct is the subject of [business ethics](business%20ethics.md). Descriptive ethics studies moral beliefs as an empirical matter, asking what people in fact hold. Normative ethics keeps to the standard itself.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what metaethics studies ::@:: The meaning of moral language and the metaphysics of moral facts.
- what applied ethics takes up ::@:: One particular issue, such as whether or when abortion is acceptable, rather than standards for action in general.
- what descriptive ethics studies ::@:: People's moral beliefs, as an empirical matter, asking what they in fact hold.
- how normative ethics differs from descriptive ethics ::@:: Normative ethics sets a standard for how one ought to act, and descriptive ethics reports the beliefs people actually hold.

## families of normative theory

A normative theory says what makes an act right. The three families divide by what each treats as the bearer of moral worth: the character of the person acting, the act or the rule the person follows, or the outcome the act brings about.

---

### virtue ethics

[Virtue ethics](virtue%20ethics.md) centres on the character of the person acting, and calls an act right when it is what a virtuous person would do in the same circumstances. Aristotle set out the classical form, and the family returned to notice after the 1950s through G. E. M. Anscombe, Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre, and Rosalind Hursthouse.

---

- the ancient philosopher who set out the classical form of virtue ethics ::@:: Aristotle.

### deontological ethics

[Deontology](deontology.md) centres on duties and rights, so on the act or the rule, not on what follows from it. Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative gives its best-known form, testing an act against a law that could hold for everyone. John Rawls builds the rules of a society from an agreement made behind a veil of ignorance, where nobody knows what place they will hold. Natural rights theories, such as those of John Locke and Robert Nozick, start from rights a person holds before any law is made.

### consequentialism

Consequentialism centres on the outcome, and calls an act right when it brings the best result. [Utilitarianism](utilitarianism.md) is its best-known form. Preference utilitarianism counts satisfied preferences rather than happiness, state consequentialism measures the good of a state, situational ethics judges each situation on its own, and welfarism judges an outcome by the welfare it holds. G. E. M. Anscombe coined the word in 1958, and utilitarianism had been the family's generic name before that.

## comparison of the families

Each family asks a different question about the same case: what kind of person should I be, what rule binds me, or what result should I bring about. The unit carrying moral worth follows the question: a character trait, a duty or a right, or a state of affairs. They part where the answers collide: a deontologist keeps the rule and accepts the worse outcome, a consequentialist takes the better outcome and breaks the rule, and a virtue ethicist asks what a person of good character would do here, which may match neither.

- what each family does with a case where the answers collide ::@:: The deontologist keeps the rule and accepts the worse outcome, the consequentialist takes the better outcome and breaks the rule, and the virtue ethicist asks what a person of good character would do here.

## common ground

Each family is normative. It sets a standard to argue for and then apply, and it does not report what people believe. Reading one case through all three shows where they part, since the three readings can disagree.

---

- what the three families of normative theory share ::@:: Each is normative: none merely describes what people believe, and each sets a standard that can be argued for and applied to the same case.
- what reading one case through all three families shows ::@:: Where the families part, since the readings can disagree.

## hybrid and overlapping theories

Few theories sit inside one family. Rule utilitarianism applies the happiness standard to rules rather than to single acts, asking which rule produces the most good when it is widely followed. Rossian pluralism, named after William David Ross, keeps several _prima facie_ duties with no ranking among them, so it is deontological in shape while refusing one master rule. [Ethics of care](ethics%20of%20care.md) grows out of feminist theory as a relational fourth family, through Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings, and overlaps virtue ethics on character and deontology on the obligation to particular people. Role ethics builds on family roles and stays close to [Confucian ethics](Confucian%20ethics.md). Contractarianism, contractualism, natural rights theories, pragmatic ethics, and situational ethics each sit partly inside or beside the three families.

## using the frameworks

The frameworks are lenses on one case rather than rivals. None of them is the one true theory. Each makes visible what the others pass over: a character to build, a duty that holds whatever follows, an outcome that can be weighed. Competence lies in switching between them.
