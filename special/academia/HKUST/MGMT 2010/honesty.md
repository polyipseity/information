# honesty

Honesty is truthfulness in conduct. It rules out lying, cheating, stealing, and keeping what belongs to someone else. Dishonesty is any of those acts chosen against someone. For both, the usual setting is a relationship, where each side can see what the other does.

The expectation reaches past relationships. Public services, business transactions, and government policy rest on strangers acting honestly, and almost all of them are people one never meets. Trade and administration cost more when a buyer cannot trust a supplier or a tax office cannot trust a return.

## honesty without a relationship

A dishonest act among people who know each other carries three costs: the other person finds out, the relationship takes the damage, and a reputation follows whoever did it. A lost wallet removes all three: the owner is a stranger, the wallet was found away from the owner's home, and nobody is likely to learn who kept it.

Nothing in the setting punishes dishonesty or rewards a relationship, so only the finder's own standard is left. A returned wallet measures that standard, not caution.

## lost-wallet field experiment

A field experiment runs in the setting the behaviour happens in rather than in a laboratory, and the people taking part are not told that they are being studied. Behavioural economist Alain Cohn of the University of Michigan led the study, which appeared in Science. The team handed wallets to front-desk staff in banks, post offices, museums, hotels, and public offices. The work ran in 355 cities across 40 countries between 2013 and 2016, and covered more than 17,000 wallets. A member of the team played the finder, said the wallet had been found around the corner, and left it with the desk. A separate round of fieldwork covered three countries and tested a much larger sum.

### wallet design

Every wallet was the same on the outside: a small clear case, so the contents could be read without opening it. Inside were three identical business cards, a grocery list in the local language, and a key.

Money was the variable: half the wallets held the local-currency equivalent of about 13 United States dollars, 13.45 exactly, and half held nothing.

### return measurement

The business cards carried a name and an email address, and a message to that address counted as a return. Every message got a reply saying the owner had left town and no longer needed the wallet, so nothing was ever handed back.

---

- how a lost wallet's return was recorded ::@:: Whether the front-desk staff emailed the address on the wallet's business cards.
- what the owner's reply to a front-desk message said ::@:: That the owner had left town and no longer needed the wallet, so nothing was ever handed back.

### controls against detection

Fear of being caught is the obvious rival explanation, so the design recorded who else was present when the desk took the wallet, whether security cameras covered the spot, and whether local law forbade keeping lost property. None explained the higher return rate on wallets with money, so fear of punishment was not the cause.

---

- what the detection checks showed about wallets holding money ::@:: Presence of others, cameras, and local law ruled out fear of punishment as the explanation.

## effect of money on returning a wallet

Empty wallets were reported 46% of the time, against 61% for wallets holding about 13 United States dollars. The direction held in 38 of the 40 countries. A three-country follow-up raised the stake to 94.15 United States dollars and lifted average returns by 18%. Wallets holding nearly 100 United States dollars were reported 72% of the time.

The result held across the age and sex of the front-desk worker and whether anyone observed the handover. Asked to predict the effect, 279 economists said the opposite: money would make a wallet less likely to come back.

## explanations for the money effect

Three explanations fit the pattern and none excludes the others: care for the owner's loss, the picture the finder holds of themselves, and the harm the finder believes the owner suffers. A fourth treats honesty as a response to being seen. None is a cost-benefit calculation: what feels comfortable and what can be rationalised afterwards matters more than the sum in the wallet.

---

### altruism

The first explanation is care for the person who lost the wallet, a stranger whose loss still counts. The key tested this: it is precious to the owner and worthless to anyone else, so a finder moved by the owner's loss returns it and a finder moved only by value keeps it. Wallets holding a key were about 10% more likely to be reported than wallets without one.

### self-image

The second explanation is the picture a person holds of themselves. Most people want to think of themselves as honest, and a small sum can be explained away. Finders said the more money a wallet held, the more keeping it would feel like stealing.

### perceived harm to the owner

The third explanation is the harm the finder believes the owner suffers, and a larger amount is a larger loss. Anonymity should pull the other way: an owner known only through a business card is easy to set aside, yet returns rose anyway.

### sense of being watched

Being seen still matters where no punishment can follow. A picture of eyes on a wall raises the sense of being watched, and people behave more honestly even when nobody is watching. The object carries the same signal: a wallet showing signs of a person, such as a photograph, is more likely to come back.

## beliefs that raise returning

Simon Moss, a senior lecturer in psychology at Charles Darwin University, was less surprised by the money effect and placed it alongside earlier findings on what moves a person to hand something back. People return anything more readily, he said, when the object carries more evidence of humanness, such as photographs. Some return more when they see life as unpredictable but broadly fair, in the way that what goes around comes around, and others because they feel watched with nobody present.

Alain Cohn of the University of Michigan said the team had not expected money to raise returns, and put the pattern down to two factors: altruism toward a stranger, and the difficulty of still seeing oneself as honest. Altruism, he held, does not explain the whole effect.

## variation between countries

Ranked by the share of empty wallets returned, Switzerland came first at 74%. Among wallets holding money, Australia returned 69% and the United States returned 57%.

The ranking tracks education and wealth: trust supports trade, so honesty may help an economy grow, and wealth makes honesty easier to afford. Either direction can run, so the causal order stays open.

### parochialism

The grocery list and the business cards were written in the local language, which told the finder that the owner was a local. Honest and helpful behaviour is often parochial: it favours a person recognised as part of one's own group. Economist Shaul Shalvi of the University of Amsterdam called the design "a new way to assess human honesty" and doubted the result would hold in a diverse globalised society. He found the result surprising, so his doubt was about whom it covers rather than whether it holds. He called the honesty the experiment measured civic honesty: truthfulness toward a stranger rather than someone one knows. A rate measured for a local owner can fall for a tourist or an immigrant.

## implications for incentives and modelling

If people weighed every dishonest act as a cost and a benefit, monitoring and penalties would do most of the work of keeping them honest. The evidence puts that calculation below the picture a person holds of themselves and below what a setting treats as normal, so what a workplace makes normal matters alongside the size of the penalty.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what the lost-wallet results imply for designing incentives ::@:: Monitoring and penalties address a weaker motive than assumed, so what a setting makes normal and visible matters as much as the penalty.
- what a social norm is, in deciding whether to act honestly ::@:: What a setting treats as normal, which the lost-wallet results put above a cost-and-benefit calculation.

### incentives built on pessimism

Economists and laypeople expected money to reduce returns, and monitoring and penalties aimed at that expectation are not wasted. Some people do act narrowly selfish: 31% of the Australian wallets holding money were never returned. Sizing a whole system for that minority is the mistake.

---

- what over-pessimism about other people's honesty costs ::@:: Effort goes to a motive weaker than assumed, and a system sized for the narrowly selfish minority fits the rest badly.

### modelling and celebrating honesty

Honesty can be grown rather than policed. Anyone who sees themselves as honest has a reason to keep acting honestly, and a setting supplies that reason by letting others see honest conduct and thanking the person who chose it.

- what a visible example of honesty adds to a penalty ::@:: A penalty restrains a calculation, while a visible example supplies a picture of oneself that the next person wants to keep.
