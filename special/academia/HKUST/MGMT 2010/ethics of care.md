# ethics of care

Care ethics is the view that we should have a caring character, one that helps others in need and avoids harming them. It grounds ethical concern in our emotional responses to people we have a personal relationship with, and those responses weigh most when the other person is vulnerable or dependent on us. A special relationship adds a responsibility to consider how an action affects that person.

## universalist ethics and partiality

Care ethics began as an argument about the dominant theories of the Western ethical tradition. Rights-based and utilitarian theories rest, on that argument, on moral responses more typical of men and boys than of women and girls. Both are universalist: a system of ethics should favour no one's interests or needs over anyone else's. Partiality runs the other way and favours a person one is close to, whose interests then count for more than a stranger's.

Universalist impartiality counts the pain of a dog we do not know for as much as that of a dog we love. Some care ethicists hold that this gives too little weight to our feelings for those we are close to, and stress instead our relationships, the feelings they carry, and our natural wish to care for the vulnerable. Interdependence, or mutual dependency, means each person's wellbeing depends on other people. A care ethicist may then put a loved dog's needs first without ignoring another dog's.

## care for sentient beings

Care ethics rests on attentiveness to the needs of others, so it reaches animals whose needs have to be inferred. A sentient being is one that can feel suffering and wellbeing.

An anthropocentric reading of care ethics excludes nonhuman animals, or gives their interests less weight: we usually have stronger ties with humans, even strangers, than with other animals, so the interests of all humans come first. Care ethicists answer that a caring agent cannot ignore a being it knows to be suffering, so concern reaches all who can feel suffering and wellbeing. Some also take on duties toward exploited animals and toward animals living in the wild, which supports veganism and helping wild animals to prevent suffering from natural causes.

## four ethical qualities of care

Care ethics can also join a universalising ethic of equal moral consideration for all sentient beings. One such account names four ethical qualities of care: attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the four ethical qualities of care ::@:: Attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness.
- the ethic the four qualities of care are combined with ::@:: A universalising ethic that gives equal moral consideration to all sentient beings, regardless of how close the carer's relationship with them is.

### attentiveness

Attentiveness is paying attention to the needs of others, which means learning what a being's needs are. Care is impossible without it. It matters most with animals, which cannot put their needs into words the way most humans can.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what attentiveness asks of a carer ::@:: Paying attention to the needs of others, which means learning what a being's needs are.
- why attentiveness is necessary for care ::@:: We cannot care well for others without first learning what their needs are.
- where attentiveness matters most in care ::@:: With animals, which cannot communicate their needs to us verbally the way most humans can.

### responsibility

Responsibility is taking it upon oneself to look after the wellbeing of others, and care is impossible without it. It differs from obligation in universalist ethics, which is treatment owed to someone, as in a legal contract: responsibility is looser, taken on with no moral or legal duty. Someone who believes humans owe wild animals no help can still take responsibility for their suffering and enter a caring relationship with them.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what responsibility asks of a carer ::@:: Taking it upon oneself to look after the wellbeing of others, even with no moral or legal duty to do so.
- why responsibility is needed for care ::@:: We cannot care for others unless we take it upon ourselves to be responsible for their wellbeing.
- responsibility against obligation in care ethics ::@:: Obligation is treatment owed to someone, as in a legal contract; responsibility is looser, taken on with no moral or legal duty.
- taking responsibility for wild animals without any duty to help them ::@:: Possible: a person who believes humans owe wild animals no help can still take responsibility for their suffering.

### competence

Recognising a need and taking on responsibility for helping are not enough. The carer must also be, or become, competent to help. Competence matters in helping nonhuman animals, which differ from us, and most of all in natural ecosystems, where intervening without it is useless or dangerous.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what competence asks of a carer ::@:: Being, or becoming, able to help the being whose need one has recognised and taken responsibility for.
- why competence is required in care ::@:: Recognising a need and taking on the responsibility to help are not enough; the carer must also be, or become, competent to do so.
- why competence matters most when intervening in natural ecosystems ::@:: Taking on that responsibility would be useless or dangerous unless the carer becomes competent to intervene safely.

### responsiveness

The carer must be responsive to what the dependent being is trying to communicate, and stay responsive for as long as the relationship lasts. A carer who misreads the being in their care can harm it by accident. Responsiveness matters most with nonhuman animals, whose means of communication differ from ours.

---

- why responsiveness must be ongoing across the caring relationship ::@:: A carer who is not responsive risks accidentally harming the being in their care.
- where responsiveness is especially important in care ::@:: With nonhuman animals, whose means of communication differ from ours.

## relational repair

Relational repair, also called moral repair, is the work of restoring a relationship after one person has harmed the other. Care ethics treats it as an ethical goal in its own right, not as a way of settling who was right. An ethic of rules can end a dispute by assigning fault; repair keeps the bond the harm put at risk, so walking away looks like a failure rather than a resolution.

The apology carries the repair. It acknowledges the harm to the person who suffered it and restores the standing the harm took away, so the relationship continues on its earlier terms.

Attentiveness comes first: the apology starts from the harm the other person suffered, not from a defence of the one who caused it. Responsiveness carries it forward, since the carer acts on what the harmed person is trying to communicate about that harm. Responsibility enters in its care-ethics form, taken on rather than owed: no contract compels an apology, so the repair has to be taken up by the one who caused the harm.

---

- why care ethics treats walking away from the person as a failure ::@:: Repair keeps the bond that the harm put at risk, so ending the relationship resolves the dispute by giving up what care ethics values.
- what the apology accomplishes in relational repair ::@:: It acknowledges the harm to the person who suffered it and restores the standing the harm took away.
- what the apology begins from in relational repair ::@:: The harm the other person suffered, reached through an effort to become aware of their needs, rather than a defence of the person who caused it.

## limits of relationship-based care

Care for those we are close to covers very few people: we have no relationship with the overwhelming majority of humans. A person with a lifelong bond to a dog might stay indifferent to starving children elsewhere. If relationships were the only basis of caring, that indifference would count as ethical, and some nonhuman animals would deserve more consideration than many humans.

One reply rejects the relevance of relationships to moral consideration, at the cost of part of what care ethicists defend. The other keeps care ethics and joins it to a universalising ethic of equal moral consideration for all sentient beings.

---

- the objection to making relationships the only basis of caring ::@:: It would leave us caring about very few individuals, since we have no relationship with the overwhelming majority of humans.
- the dog against starving children case ::@:: A lifelong bond with a dog joined to complete indifference to starving children elsewhere would have to count as ethical, with the dog deserving more consideration than many humans.
- the reply that rejects relationships as a basis of moral consideration ::@:: Relationships are irrelevant to moral consideration, even though this rejects part of what care ethicists defend.
- the reply that combines care ethics with a universalising ethic ::@:: Care ethics is joined to an ethic that gives equal moral consideration to all sentient beings, regardless of how close the carer's relationship with them is.

## speciesism

Peter Singer, an Australian moral philosopher and emeritus professor of bioethics at Princeton University, popularises the term speciesism in _Animal Liberation_ (1975). Speciesism is a bias in favour of one's own species, analogous to racism or sexism. Any being that can suffer has interests that matter morally, so animal suffering cannot be discounted because animals are less intelligent. He adds the argument from marginal cases: if infants and severely cognitively disabled humans get equal moral weight, many animals must get serious weight too, since they can suffer and enjoy as much.
