# deontology

Deontology holds that an action is right or wrong in itself, whatever it brings about. The name joins the Greek _deon_, duty or obligation, to _logos_, the study of. It is also called duty-based, obligation-based, or rule-based ethics.

Immanuel Kant (1724-1804), a German philosopher of Königsberg, gave the theory its best-known form in the categorical imperative. He wanted an objective, rational basis for morality, one that did not rest on changing emotions, cultural tradition, or unpredictable consequences. Ethical action then follows universal moral laws: do not kill, do not lie, do not steal.

Lying, promise-breaking, murder, and torture stay wrong even where they would leave the world better off. A utilitarian tests consequences instead: an action is right when it produces the greatest net happiness.

## duty and the good will

Immanuel Kant holds that only a good will, a will that acts from duty, is good without qualification. Talents of the mind such as intelligence and wit, qualities of temperament such as courage and perseverance, and gifts of fortune such as wealth and power are desirable. A corrupt will turns any of them to an evil end.

An action has moral worth only when it is done from duty, not when it merely conforms to duty or follows from inclination. Morality is recognising what reason commands and acting on it, not how one feels.

### acting from duty versus in conformity with duty

One shopkeeper charges every customer a fair price to keep a good reputation and maximise profit. The motive is self-interest, so the fair dealing would stop if cheating paid better and carried no risk. The action conforms to duty but has no moral worth.

A second shopkeeper charges the same fair price because honesty is an obligation, even where cheating would bring greater profit and no risk of detection. That action is done from duty, so it has moral worth. The rule each acts on separates the two.

## hypothetical and categorical imperatives

An imperative is a command reason gives to action. Immanuel Kant divides imperatives by whether the command depends on a desire the agent holds.

---

- an imperative of reason ::@:: A command that reason gives to action.
- what separates a hypothetical from a categorical imperative ::@:: Whether the command depends on a desire the agent happens to hold.

### hypothetical imperative

A hypothetical imperative is conditional. It tells a person what to do to reach a personal desire, as in "If you want to pass the exam, you ought to study." Someone who does not care about passing is not bound by it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- hypothetical imperative ::@:: A conditional command telling a person what to do to reach a personal desire.
- the exam example of a hypothetical imperative ::@:: "If you want to pass the exam, you ought to study."
- who is bound by a hypothetical imperative ::@:: Only a person who holds the desire it serves.

### categorical imperative

A categorical imperative is unconditional. It binds every rational being at all times, whatever that being wants, as in "You ought not to lie." Immanuel Kant holds it to be the fundamental principle of morality.

---

- categorical imperative ::@:: An unconditional command that applies to every rational being regardless of personal desires.
- the lying example of a categorical imperative ::@:: "You ought not to lie." It binds everyone at all times.

## formulations of the categorical imperative

Immanuel Kant stated the categorical imperative in more than one formulation.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the universal law formulation and the humanity formulation ::@:: The universal law formulation tests the rule the agent acts on; the humanity formulation tests what the action does to the people it touches.

### universal law formulation

The formulation reads: "Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law." A maxim is the rule the agent acts on. The test asks whether that rule could bind every rational being.

Suppose you promise a colleague to work with them on a business project, then learn the partnership may damage your reputation. If everyone broke a promise whenever keeping it cost something, promising would lose its meaning and assure nobody of anything. The broken promise is wrong because its maxim cannot be universalised, not because breaking it produces worse results.

The test also forbids special exceptions: a rule that one person may follow and another may not has already failed it.

- maxim ::@:: The rule that an agent acts on.

- why breaking a promise fails the universal law test ::@:: If everyone broke promises when keeping them cost something, promising would lose its meaning and assure nobody of anything.
- what makes a broken promise wrong under the universal law test ::@:: The maxim cannot be universalised. Worse results are not the ground.
- keeping a promise that damages one's reputation ::@:: The promise still binds, because the personal cost is no reason to make an exception of oneself.

### humanity as an end in itself

The second formulation, humanity as an end in itself, forbids treating a person merely as a means and requires treating every person at the same time as an end. What makes a person an end in itself is a will, agency, and a dignity equal to one's own. A person is used as a tool when their rights, needs, and dignity count for nothing beside the purpose they serve.

A surgeon cannot take the organs of one healthy patient to save five dying ones, because killing the one to save the five uses that patient as a mere means.

- an end in itself in Immanuel Kant's ethics ::@:: A being with value in its own right, so it may never be used only as a means to someone else's purpose.
- what every person possesses that grounds the humanity formulation ::@:: A will, agency, and a dignity equal to one's own.

## duties without exceptions

Five duties bind whatever the cost. Never lie, even when the truth causes harm. Do not kill an innocent person. Keep a promise, however ruinous keeping it is. Do not steal, not even to prevent greater suffering. Tell the truth in professional contexts such as medicine and law.

A promise binds because the obligation was created voluntarily, so a borrower who promised to repay a loan on time still owes it when repayment brings hardship or bankruptcy.

---

- the rule against killing ::@:: Do not kill an innocent person.
- the rule on promise-keeping ::@:: Keep a promise, no matter the cost.
- the rule against stealing ::@:: Do not steal, even to prevent greater suffering.
- truth-telling in professional contexts ::@:: Tell the truth in medical and legal ethics.
- why a promised loan repayment still binds under hardship ::@:: The obligation was created voluntarily, so hardship and bankruptcy do not release it.

## strengths

The theory sets an absolute barrier against exploitation, discrimination, and tyranny, and rules out sacrificing one person for the greater good. Universalizability, the property of a maxim that every rational being could act on, rules out double standards, where a rule binds others but not the person who states it. Deontology fits human rights codes, professional oaths such as "first, do no harm", informed medical consent, and codes of conduct.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what deontology rules out absolutely ::@:: Exploitation, discrimination, and tyranny.
- the double standard that universalizability rules out ::@:: A rule that binds other people but not the person who states it.
- universalizability ::@:: The property a maxim, the rule an agent acts on, has when every rational being could act on it.
- codes and practices deontology supports ::@:: Human rights codes, professional oaths such as "first, do no harm", informed medical consent, and professional codes of conduct.

## criticisms

Rigidity is the standing objection: Immanuel Kant held lying impermissible in any circumstance. In the inquiring murderer case, a killer asks where a friend is hiding, and strict Kantianism forbids the lie that would save the friend.

A second objection is that the theory ignores outcomes and the empathy and care they call for, so the framework is austere and over-intellectual.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the rigidity objection to deontology ::@:: Immanuel Kant held lying impermissible in any circumstance, so the theory cannot allow the lie that saves a life.
- the inquiring murderer case ::@:: A killer asks where a friend is hiding, and strict Kantianism forbids the lie that would save the friend.
- the objection about consequences and emotions ::@:: Leaving out real outcomes and the capacity for empathy and care produces an austere, over-intellectual framework.
- how W. D. Ross answered the rigidity objection ::@:: He gave duties prima facie weight instead of absolute priority, so conflicting duties can be weighed against one another.

## prima facie duties

W. D. Ross (1877-1971), Sir William David Ross, was a Scottish philosopher in the Aristotelian tradition. His best-known work, _The Right and the Good_ (1930), sets out a pluralist, intuitionist ethics of prima facie duties. Pluralism rejects monism, which grounds every obligation in one ultimate principle, and holds that several fundamental principles do the work instead.

He thought the monistic theories oversimplify, since each states its demand without exception: a utilitarian must always maximise overall happiness, even by torturing or killing innocent people, and a Kantian must always respect people, even when that demands someone be lied to or harmed to prevent a greater disrespect. Monism also cuts against moral intuition, because ordinary judgement weighs several considerations at once.

- Sir William David Ross ::@:: A Scottish philosopher in the Aristotelian tradition (1877-1971), whose _The Right and the Good_ (1930) sets out a pluralist, intuitionist ethics of prima facie duties.
- monism versus pluralism about moral obligation ::@:: Monism roots every obligation in one ultimate principle; pluralism holds that there are several fundamental principles.
- the absolute demand each monistic theory makes ::@:: A utilitarian must always maximise overall happiness, and a Kantian must always respect people and never harm them for a good end.
- W. D. Ross's objection to utilitarianism ::@:: Maximising overall happiness can be reached by torturing or killing innocent people.
- W. D. Ross's objection to Kantian ethics ::@:: It can demand that someone be lied to or harmed to prevent a greater disrespect.
- why W. D. Ross thought monism cuts against moral intuition ::@:: Ordinary judgement weighs several considerations at once, so a single ultimate principle oversimplifies.

### prima facie duty

_Prima facie_ is Latin for "at first glance". A prima facie duty is an action that one has some moral reason to perform or avoid. Each carries moral weight, and none always outranks the rest.

W. D. Ross lists seven prima facie duties:

| Duty | What it requires |
| --- | --- |
| Fidelity | Keeping promises |
| Reparation | Making amends for wrongful actions |
| Gratitude | Repaying the good actions of people from whom one has benefited |
| Justice | Apportioning pleasure or happiness to merit |
| Beneficence | Promoting the good of others |
| Self-improvement | Improving one's own moral character |
| Non-maleficence | Not harming or injuring others |

Three of the seven answer something already done: fidelity a promise made, reparation a wrong done, gratitude a benefit received. Justice fits treatment to what a person deserves. The remaining three look forward to good and harm. Beneficence and self-improvement are positive duties to bring good about, non-maleficence is the negative duty to refrain, and only self-improvement is owed to oneself.

They are self-evident: any mature, rational person who reflects on them sees that each names a feature that tends to make an action right. Keeping a promise is one such feature. Treating someone unfairly or injuring them works the other way, so one action can carry both features, and the two duties pull against each other.

### conflicts of duty

A conflict of duty is a case where prima facie duties point in different directions. The actual duty is what one ought to do once they are weighed. You have promised a friend lunch. On the way out your mother, who lives nearby, calls to say she has had an accident and needs a lift to the hospital. The promise gives way, because the emergency carries more weight. Breaking it leaves a duty of reparation, an apology to the friend.

A harder case leaves the balance unclear. Your mother's car has broken down and she needs a lift to a food bank, where she serves lunch to people in need. Driving her may do more good than meeting the friend, and the promise still stands. The good is indirect, since the lift only lets her serve that lunch. Whether fidelity or beneficence weighs more is the question, and no rule settles it.

W. D. Ross advises studying the situation carefully and judging by what one really thinks is right. The same judgement settles the hospital case, where the urgency makes the right answer seem obvious. A considered opinion can still be mistaken, so acting on it carries a risk. That judgement remains the best a person can do.

### intuitionism

W. D. Ross's theory is called intuitionism. Intuition, in his sense, is not a moral sixth sense. It is the ordinary ability to think rationally and make judgements, so it amounts to one's considered opinions. Opinions about promises, justice, and beneficence are deep and settled, while others stay open to revision.

Views about right and wrong have changed before. The best information still comes from the considered opinions of people who are well-informed, perceptive, sensitive, and rational. The prima facie duties pick out values that reflection and experience have not displaced.

The objection is that intuition cannot be trusted: intuitions may be biased or unethical, thoughtful people often disagree, and settling conflicts by intuition looks mysterious and unprincipled. W. D. Ross accepts that people disagree and recommends humility about one's own opinion along with attention to the views of others.
