# Confucian ethics

Confucian ethics is the ethical teaching built on Confucius (孔子). Confucius, born Kong Qiu (孔丘), was a Chinese philosopher of the Spring and Autumn period, traditionally taken as the paragon of Chinese sages. His teaching, Confucianism, rests on personal and governmental morality, correctness of social relationships, justice, and sincerity. Confucian ethics asks what kind of person to become rather than which act a rule permits, and answers with a set of virtues, a body of ritual practice, and an ideal person to imitate.

A person is defined by relationships, as child, parent, friend, and citizen, so a good life brings harmony to family and society rather than perfecting one individual.

## relational person

A ruler and a subject, a superior and a subordinate, a parent and a child, and two friends each carry different obligations, because duty follows the role rather than a rule that covers everyone alike.

- how a role assigns duty in Confucian ethics ::@:: Duty follows the relation, so a ruler and a subject, a superior and a subordinate, a parent and a child, and two friends each owe different obligations.

### role relations

A role's name is a claim about how the parties will act. Rectification of names (_zhengming_, 正名) requires the name to match the conduct it announces, so a leader who does not lead, or a firm that calls its workers partners while treating them as subordinates, states something false about the relation. The failure is moral rather than verbal, because the name is what the other party relies on.

A person can hold two roles whose duties clash. A purchasing officer who is also a devoted niece owes professional and family duty at once, and gratitude to the uncle who paid for her education pulls against the rule governing a tender.

## five constant virtues

The five constant virtues (五常, _wuchang_) are humaneness (_ren_, 仁), rightness (_yi_, 義), ritual propriety (_li_, 禮), wisdom (_zhi_, 智), and trustworthiness (_xin_, 信). They work as a set, and each is a trait of character rather than a rule about acts. Chen Lai calls this focus virtuous conduct (_dexing_, 德行), the outward expression of a person's inner moral quality.

### humaneness

Humaneness (_ren_, 仁) is the virtue of caring for other people, and it is the foundation of the other virtues, rightness, ritual propriety, and wisdom among them. It shows in ordinary kindness, such as inviting a classmate sitting alone at lunch into the group.

- the three virtues that rest on humaneness (_ren_, 仁) ::@:: Rightness (_yi_, 義), ritual propriety (_li_, 禮), and wisdom (_zhi_, 智).

### rightness

Rightness (_yi_, 義) is doing what is right because it is right, rather than to look good or to gain, and it covers moral duty and justice together. Where one member of a group did the work and everyone collects the same high grade, rightness is telling the truth about who did what. The same act can come from rightness or from personal profit.

### ritual propriety

Ritual propriety (_li_, 禮) is the set of customs, ceremonies, and rules of conduct governing how a person greets someone, eats with their parents, mourns a parent, and marks an occasion. It reads as empty tradition from outside, but it is the structure that shapes and carries moral behaviour.

Putting a phone away at a family dinner and supporting a friend who asks for help are both _li_.

### face

Face (_mianzi_, 面子) is the standing a person holds in the eyes of others, and ritual protects it. A junior who corrects a senior in public wins the argument and costs the senior face, so the same truth is raised in private or obliquely. At a company dinner, an intern who has checked the figures knows the manager's speech carries a false market statistic. Speaking up before the chief executive protects the numbers and costs the manager face. The intern is in the first year and researched the market figures personally, so the error is known rather than suspected.

Three answers are open. Correcting at once holds that the true figures outrank the manager's standing. Correcting in private sends a message that evening and the correct figures the next morning, so the manager hears of the error first and the room never hears it contradicted. A toast turns the talk away from numbers, so the manager is spared and the false statistic stays.

A public correction costs more than a wrong figure. A figure can be corrected afterwards, while face lost before colleagues and a chief executive cannot be restored by the junior who took it.

The same tension runs through a defective design in Shenzhen, where an apprentice holds test data showing that a product may catch fire. Humaneness (_ren_, 仁) asks whether an elder's dignity outranks protecting thousands of consumers from that fire hazard. Rightness (_yi_, 義) asks whether concealing a known flaw fits a professional's duty of integrity and public safety. Ritual propriety (_li_, 禮) asks how hierarchical respect and professional deference are kept without giving up honest reporting and accountability for the role. Trustworthiness (_xin_, 信) with filial duty (_xiao_, 孝) asks whether the apprentice owes absolute loyalty to a mentor's vanity, or a real loyalty to that mentor's technical honour. The apprentice meets all four at once by presenting the test data to the mentor in private, so the flaw is remedied without public humiliation.

### wisdom

Wisdom (_zhi_, 智) is knowing the difference between right and wrong and thinking before acting. It rests on an understanding of human nature and on deep study.

In daily life it is critical judgement: checking a claim before passing it on, or seeing a friend press someone into a bad decision and stepping away.

### trustworthiness

Trustworthiness (_xin_, 信) is keeping a promise, matching words to actions, and being someone others can rely on. It is treated as a foundation of society, because a ruler or a citizen who cannot be trusted brings the whole arrangement down. A deck promised for eight in the evening arrives by eight, and a secret told in confidence stays with the person told.

## filial piety

Filial piety (_xiao_, 孝) is the duty a child owes a parent, and it sets the pattern every other role duty follows. It also creates open conflicts: a student who wants to play sport faces parents who spent years paying for a finance degree.

An uncle who paid for an education and a mentor who taught a trade both hold a claim on the person, carried as a debt of gratitude rather than a contract.

## reciprocity

Reciprocity (_shu_, 恕), also translated as understanding, is putting yourself in another person's place. Confucius states it in the negative: do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire.

The rule links Confucianism to care ethics, where feeling for another person grounds the obligation.

### innate sprouts of virtue

Mencius grounded reciprocity in human nature, holding that people are naturally good, because each carries innate sprouts of virtue, among them the heart of compassion. Anyone who sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion at once, before any thought of gain. That reflex is his evidence that care for others is native rather than taught. Mencius (孟子), born Meng Ke (孟軻), was a Confucian philosopher of the Warring States period, called the Second Sage (亞聖) for his standing next to Confucius (孔子), whose teaching he inherited and developed.

## exemplary person

The ethical ideal is the _junzi_ (君子), the gentleman or exemplary person, and at the furthest reach the sage (_shengren_, 聖人). The resemblance to the practically wise person (_phronimos_) of Aristotle, the ancient Greek philosopher, is close: neither merely follows rules, and each sees how to act rightly in the situation in front of them.

### solitary vigilance

Solitary vigilance (_shen-du_, 慎獨) is the conduct a person keeps when nobody is watching. A public figure who posts about mindfulness and community service and is rude to service staff when unobserved contradicts the posts, because no audience shapes that conduct.

## moral and non-moral virtues

Wong Wai-ying draws a line between two kinds of virtue. Virtue ethics in the West concentrates almost entirely on moral virtues such as honesty and justice. Confucianism also counts non-moral virtues, qualities that make a life good without being strictly moral: aesthetic satisfaction, wisdom, courage in facing obstacles, and physical grace. The aim is a well-rounded person, not only a morally correct one.

## likeness to virtue ethics

Confucian ethics and virtue ethics both ask what kind of person to become, where a rule theory asks which rule governs the case and a result theory asks which outcome the available actions produce. Both cultivate character, and both treat a good person as someone with a feel for the right act in an unfamiliar case.

---

- what Confucian ethics and virtue ethics both centre on ::@:: Cultivating character, so that the aim is to become a certain kind of person rather than only to perform correct actions.
- the question Confucian ethics asks where a rule theory asks which rule applies ::@:: What kind of person to become, rather than which rule governs the case or which outcome is best.

## divergence from virtue ethics

The likeness stops at three points. Confucianism carries a structured practice for building virtue that virtue ethics leaves vague, uses duty alongside character, and gives special weight to the people closest to a person.

### ritual as directed empathy

Mencius tells of a king who felt pity for an ox being led to slaughter and did nothing for his own starving people. Marion Hourdequin takes the story to show that empathy is not self-directing: the feeling arrives and needs somewhere to go. _Li_ aims a person's care at family, community, and the duties they hold, and repetition settles the aim.

### rule-based reading

Lee Ming-huei argues that Confucian ethics sits closer to a rule-based or duty-based ethics than to Aristotelian virtue ethics. The early Confucians, Confucius and Mencius among them, set acting from rightness (_yi_, 義) against acting for personal profit, and a focus on duty over consequences looks Kantian.

The majority reading keeps the focus on the inner self: the rules of ritual propriety (_li_, 禮) are tools for internalising the spirit of humaneness (_ren_, 仁), not a list to obey blindly.

### partiality toward the close

Affection for the close (_qin-qin_, 親親) puts a person's duty to a friend or a parent ahead of an impersonal rule, and universal justice puts the rule first. A student whose closest friend cheated on a graded examination owes something to the friend, and the class that loses marks until the culprit is named holds a claim too.
