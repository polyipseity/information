# ethical decision-making

A decision becomes an ethical problem when every option open to the person carries a real cost. A gut reaction then leaves no reason the person can give afterwards.

An ethical process supplies that reason. It names its checks in advance, applies each to every alternative, and records why an alternative was dropped. The PLUS model is one such process: four filters named by Policies, Legal, Universal, and Self, applied in a fixed order that runs from the rules written inside the organization, through the law, to shared values, and ends at the decision maker's own conscience. Each step moves further from what can be looked up and closer to what only the person deciding can answer.

- the kind of decision that calls for an ethical process ::@:: One where every option carries a real cost, so a gut reaction leaves no reason the decision maker can still give.
- what a decision process supplies that intuition does not ::@:: Checks named in advance and applied to every alternative, plus a record of why an alternative was dropped.
- the four words inside the acronym PLUS ::@:: Policies, Legal, Universal, and Self, the four filters applied to every alternative.
- the stages the fixed filter order passes through ::@:: The organization's written rules, the law, shared values, and the decision maker's own conscience.
- what ethical decision-making is ::@:: Settling a choice by checks that can be stated to others, so the reason for it survives being questioned.

## PLUS model

The model is a checklist for a decision that will have to be explained. Written out in advance, each test forces a conclusion reached on instinct to survive four questions in turn. The same work is due diligence before a deal, where risks and assumptions are examined while the commitment can still be avoided.

Applied to a decision, a filter asks one question of every alternative and lets through the ones that answer it well enough. The four run in a fixed order because each tests something the earlier ones cannot. A written rule can still break the law. A lawful act can still be unfair, and a fair act can still be one the person who chose it cannot live with.

### policies

Policies asks whether an alternative aligns with the organization's policies and guidelines. It covers the written rules, such as a code of conduct, a human resources policy, or an industry standard, and the unwritten norms that guide how people behave. An alternative that fails is usually dropped at once, so the three later filters never reach it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what the Policies filter asks of an alternative ::@:: Whether it aligns with the organization's policies and guidelines.
- the written and unwritten rules the Policies filter considers ::@:: Written rules such as a code of conduct, a human resources policy, or an industry standard, and the unwritten norms that guide behaviour.
- what usually happens to an alternative that fails the Policies filter ::@:: It is dropped at once, so the Legal, Universal, and Self filters never reach it.

### legal

Legal asks whether the alternative complies with every relevant law and regulation. Company policy can allow what the law forbids, so this check does not follow from the policy filter. The law applied is the one that governs where the decision lands, such as local labour law, anti-corruption statutes, or data privacy rules.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what the Legal filter asks of an alternative ::@:: Whether it complies with every relevant law and regulation.
- why a company policy that permits an act does not settle the Legal filter ::@:: Policy can allow what the law forbids, so the act is tested against the law that governs where the decision lands.
- the kinds of law that govern where the decision lands ::@:: Local labour law, anti-corruption statutes, and data privacy rules.

### universal

Universal asks whether the alternative upholds universal ethical principles such as fairness, honesty, and respect, and whether broader societal standards would call it ethical. A second reading tests the values the organization adopted in its mission and core values, where sustainability may sit beside those same principles. Either reading needs a shared understanding of those values, so two employees answer alike. One agreed set is empathy, patience, integrity, and courage, abbreviated EPIC.

---

- what the Universal filter asks of an alternative ::@:: Whether it upholds universal ethical principles such as fairness, honesty, and respect.
- the standard beyond ethical principles that the Universal filter applies ::@:: Whether broader societal standards would call the decision ethical.
- the organizational-values reading of the Universal filter ::@:: Whether the alternative conforms to the values the organization adopted in its mission and core values.
- the agreed value set abbreviated EPIC ::@:: Empathy, Patience, Integrity, and Courage, a shared set two employees can apply alike.

### self

Self asks whether the alternative aligns with the decision maker's own values and beliefs. Two questions carry the test: would the decision maker be comfortable if the decision were made public, and could they sleep at night having made it. The filter comes last because it asks what nobody else can settle: the reasoning can be given to colleagues, but only the decision maker can accept it.

- why the Self filter comes last of the four ::@:: It asks what nobody else can settle, since the reasoning can be given to colleagues and only the decision maker can accept it.

## decision-making steps

Step one defines the problem: why a decision is called for, the outcome wanted, and the facts and stakeholders that belong to it. Step two lists the courses of action, wide enough to include the options that are not immediately obvious. Step three puts each alternative through the four filters. Step four selects the alternative that best holds up and records the choice with its reasoning, so the decision can be explained later. Step five puts the choice into effect through a visible process, telling every stakeholder affected what was decided. Step six assesses the result against the ethical outcome the decision was meant to reach, and keeps what it taught for the next decision.

A stakeholder is anyone the decision reaches. A problem stated as the gap between the current situation and the wanted outcome decides where alternatives are looked for. Step one also gathers help from a mentor, colleagues, professional guidelines, and organizational policies.

The filters reach beyond step three: they are applied when the problem is defined, when the decision is made, and when the outcome is assessed.

## limits

A written policy can be outdated or incomplete, so an alternative that clears it can still be ethically weak. Universal principles are not shared across every region: what one place treats as a principle another does not, which matters to an organization working in several regions. The Self filter rests on a personal definition of right, fair, and good, and those definitions vary, so individual judgement needs balancing against organizational standards.

The model also depends on conditions the organization has to supply. One is a shared understanding across employees of the policies as they apply to a situation, the laws and regulations that govern it, the agreed universal values, and each person's own sense of right. The other is a formal way to obtain a definitive reading of any of them when an employee's knowledge is too thin to decide with confidence.
