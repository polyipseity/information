# utilitarianism

Utilitarianism judges an action by the happiness it produces. It belongs to consequentialism, which looks only at results, and adds a claim about which results are good: happiness and pleasure are good, suffering and pain are bad, and every person's welfare counts the same. The right act leaves the most net happiness behind, and no kind of act is ruled out in advance. That net balance is utility.

## consequentialism

Consequentialism judges the rightness or wrongness of an action by its results alone. It says nothing about which results are good. Actions and failures to act face the same test: failing to act when acting would have done more good is as wrong as a harmful act.

Deontology holds the opposite: agents have duties, expressed as rules, and an act is right when it follows them, whatever the outcome. A deontologist forbids lying to a patient because lying is wrong, while a consequentialist asks what the lie will do. A rule utilitarian forbids the same lie for a different reason: the rule produces the most good when widely followed.

## principle of utility

The principle of utility states that the morally right action is the one that produces the most good, and that a wrong action is one that reduces the maximum good available. It is also called the greatest happiness principle.

Benefit and harm admit more than one characterisation. Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill take them as happiness and unhappiness, or as pleasure and pain, and treat pleasure and happiness as synonyms.

The sum is net, taken over everyone the act touches: benefit to some set against harm to others, and the act is right when the balance is positive.

A bank robbery shows how far the sum reaches. If the robbers share the money widely enough that more people gain than lose, the theory counts the heist as right. Most people reject the verdict.

### proof of the principle

John Stuart Mill (1806-1873), the English philosopher, political economist, and politician, argued in _Utilitarianism_ (1861) that the principle of utility follows from three claims about happiness. Desirability: happiness is desired as an end in itself, and the only evidence that something is desirable is that people desire it, as the only evidence that something is visible is that people see it. Exhaustiveness: whatever else a person desires, they desire it because getting it would make them happy, so happiness is the only thing desired for its own sake. Impartiality: equal amounts of happiness are equally desirable whether the same person feels them or different people do, so the general happiness is the good of the aggregate. Mill's own words are that "each person's happiness is a good to that person, and the general happiness, therefore, a good to the aggregate of all persons".

The third step is the weak one: Mill asserts that one person's happiness is as desirable as another's without showing why, so whether his argument establishes the principle or only states it is disputed.

## ends and means

A man in New York has no income, no savings, and nobody who will lend to him. His wife is ill and needs 500 dollars of treatment, and his two children have been sent home from school over 500 dollars of tuition. A wealthy friend refuses to help, so he steals 1,000 dollars from the friend and pays both bills. A deontologist calls the theft wrong because stealing is wrong. A utilitarian calls it right: the friend loses money he has less need of, and three people gain what they need more. The theft is the means, the family's welfare the end.

- what counts as the means and what as the end in a utilitarian theft of 1,000 dollars from a wealthy friend ::@:: The theft is the means, and the medical treatment and the schooling are the end.

## classical formulations

Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832), the English philosopher, jurist, and social reformer regarded as the founder of modern utilitarianism, treats every pleasure as a quantity on one scale. John Stuart Mill (1806-1873) holds that pleasures also differ in kind, so a smaller amount of a higher pleasure can outweigh a larger amount of a lower one.

### felicific calculus

Jeremy Bentham proposed a felicific calculus, a single scale for comparing any two pleasures and pains objectively. It has seven dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, proximity, fecundity, purity, and extent. Intensity is how strong the feeling is, duration how long it lasts, certainty how likely it is, and proximity how near at hand. Fecundity is how likely the pleasure is to lead to further pleasure, purity how free of pain it is, and extent how many people it reaches.

His own comparison sets a pint of beer against reading Shakespeare's _Hamlet_. The beer is more intense, lasts longer, is more certain, is closer at hand, is more likely to lead to further pleasure, is purer, and is shared with friends in the bar. It wins on every dimension, so the calculus says to drink the beer. The person choosing runs the comparison. Jeremy Bentham claimed only that it can be made.

Jeremy Bentham meant the calculus for legislation, so a policy maker could weigh the pleasure an act would produce across society. He held that, a quantity of pleasure being equal, pushpin, a popular children's game of the time, is as good as poetry. Critics called the doctrine a pig philosophy, and read the logic as a prediction that people would sit around eating or chasing base pleasures all day.

### higher and lower pleasures

John Stuart Mill accepted the calculus as far as quantity goes and denied that quantity is all there is. A pleasure is higher when people competent to judge, having experienced both, prefer it even at the cost of discomfort and would not trade it for any quantity of the lower one.

On that test, reading _Hamlet_ gives less pleasure than a pint of beer but a higher kind, so a person choosing between them should read the play. The verdict holds whether the choice is made once or stands as a rule. John Stuart Mill's summary is that it is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied, and better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.

Roger Crisp, the British philosopher and Professor of Moral Philosophy at the University of Oxford, frames the choice as a soul in heaven waiting to be allocated a life. The soul is offered either the composer Joseph Haydn, who dies at 77, or an oyster that feels mild pleasure and lives as long as it likes. The oyster's mild pleasure is like floating in a warm bath. However much pleasure Haydn's life holds, an endless oyster life passes it in total, and almost everyone still chooses Haydn. The charge of elitism is that someone might prefer a life of endless chocolate cake to being Haydn, and Mill answers that only a person acquainted with both pleasures can judge between them.

John Stuart Mill's reply rests on two claims: some pleasures are incomparable, and a person aware of the higher ones will not be content to leave them uncultivated. No intelligent human being would consent to be a fool, no instructed person to be an ignoramus, and no person of feeling and conscience to be selfish and base, even if persuaded that the fool, the dunce, or the rascal is better satisfied with their lot. Where people give up a life of happiness, John Stuart Mill says they do it for a further end, the happiness of others or God.

## varieties of utilitarianism

The principle leaves two questions open. The first asks what is judged: a single act, or a general rule. The second asks which consequence counts: the result that actually followed, or the result a reasonable person could have foreseen. The two are independent, so act and rule utilitarianism each come in an actual and a foreseeable form.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the two questions that divide utilitarian theories ::@:: What is judged, a single act or a general rule, and which consequence counts, the actual result or the foreseeable one.
- whether the act and rule division and the actual and foreseeable division are the same question ::@:: No. One asks what is judged and the other asks which consequence counts, so the two pair freely.

### act utilitarianism

Act utilitarianism applies the principle to each act on its own. An act is right when it produces more happiness than the alternatives open in that situation. Nothing is settled beforehand, so the same kind of act can be right in one case and wrong in another.

A doctor lies to a patient to keep them calm during surgery, which helps save the patient's life, so act utilitarianism approves it. A hospital short of a life-saving drug has a harder case. Five patients need a small dose each to survive, and one critically ill patient needs the whole supply. Dividing the supply saves five and lets the one die, so act utilitarianism requires the division.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what act utilitarianism judges an act against ::@:: The happiness its own consequences produce in that situation, compared with the alternatives open at the time.
- whether act utilitarianism can approve one lie and condemn another of the same kind ::@:: Yes. Each act is judged on its own, so nothing about lying is settled in advance.
- why act utilitarianism approves a doctor's lie during surgery ::@:: The lie prevents panic and helps save the patient's life, so in that case it produces the best outcome.
- how act utilitarianism divides a drug supply that can save five patients or one ::@:: It divides the supply among the five, since five survivals outweigh one.

### rule utilitarianism

Rule utilitarianism judges general rules instead of isolated acts. An act is right when it follows a rule whose general practice maximises happiness. Moral rules are summaries of the results an act of that kind typically brings, and stealing is wrong on that ground.

The doctor's lie fails the test. A rule allowing doctors to lie would spread distrust of medical care, so the rule against lying produces more happiness overall than the permission does. The shortage case is unsettled: a rule that gives patients equal treatment, or respects their right to treatment, can forbid sacrificing the one patient even to save five.

- why rule utilitarianism rejects a doctor's lie to a patient ::@:: A rule allowing doctors to lie would spread distrust of medical care, so the rule against lying produces more happiness overall.
- how rule utilitarianism treats a drug supply that can save five patients or one ::@:: A rule of equal treatment or of patients' rights can forbid sacrificing the one, even to save five.

### actual consequence utilitarianism

Actual consequence utilitarianism judges an act by what actually happened: the act is right if its real results produced the greatest happiness overall, whatever the agent believed, could have predicted, or intended. A decision can be right in foresight and wrong in hindsight.

A doctor gives a medicine expected to cure, and the patient dies of a rare allergic reaction. The act is wrong on this view, because the result was harmful.

J. J. C. Smart (1920-2012), the British-Australian philosopher and emeritus professor at the Australian National University, uses a rescue to divide the two views. Someone pulls a drowning person from the water, and that person later becomes a mass murderer. The rescue is wrong in hindsight, and it would have counted as right had the person become Nelson Mandela or Martin Luther King Jr. The rescuer acted in good faith, so the verdict rests on an outcome nobody at the water could have known. The foreseeable verdict is that the rescue was right.

### foreseeable consequence utilitarianism

Foreseeable consequence utilitarianism judges the results a reasonable person could have expected when the decision was made. The quality of the decision is what counts, so an outcome nobody could foresee does not make a sound decision wrong.

A doctor who gives a medicine the evidence supports acts rightly. The patient's rare allergic reaction was not foreseeable.

## strengths

- The test is the same for every case. Two people who disagree can each state the happiness they expect from an option, so the argument turns on comparable estimates rather than on competing duties.
- Each person's welfare counts the same, so the theory gives no extra weight to the agent's own interests, to rank, or to distance.
- The theory asks what a decision will do, which suits a policy that burdens a few and improves health, safety, or income for many. It also reaches rights: a rule that protects rights generally raises welfare.

---

- why clarity counts as a utilitarian strength ::@:: One test applies to every case, so a disagreement turns on comparable estimates of happiness.
- why impartiality counts as a utilitarian strength ::@:: Each person's welfare counts equally, and the agent's own interests get no extra weight.
- why a focus on outcomes counts as a utilitarian strength ::@:: The theory asks what a decision will do, so a policy that burdens a few and improves life for many can be defended.
- how a rule utilitarian explains why rights matter ::@:: Rights are rules that generally raise welfare, so protecting them follows from the principle of utility.

## weaknesses

Three objections stand: the calculation cannot be made, the individual can be sacrificed, and the agent is asked to calculate too much. The first is a technical difficulty the theory could settle. The second is the graver problem, because it conflicts with the intuition that people have a right not to be used.

### measurement of utility

Utility has to be measured, and no agreed scale exists, so two comparisons are needed. Within one person, different pleasures and pains have to be ranked against each other. Between people, one person's benefit has to be set against another's on a neutral scale, because the theory adds every benefit into a single total.

Jeremy Bentham claimed his seven dimensions settled the first comparison, and John Stuart Mill replied that pleasure differs in kind. Neither answer settles the second, so the total rests on a comparison no agent can make with confidence.

### sacrifice of rights

Act utilitarianism can approve the sacrifice of one person for a larger group. The trolley problem is a thought experiment in which a runaway trolley will kill several people unless the agent intervenes. Peter Singer, the Australian moral philosopher and emeritus professor of bioethics at Princeton University, gives the footbridge version: five people cannot leave the track, and the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a footbridge in front of it. Pushing the stranger kills one and saves five, so act utilitarianism appears to require the push, and the stranger's right to life carries no weight of its own. Killing innocent people generally does not produce the best results, so a rule utilitarian is wary of the push, and the uncertainty in the calculation strengthens the warning.

If expelling an innocent person would end a riot that threatens dozens, the calculation weighs the riot against the expulsion and gives the person's innocence no weight. Jeremy Bentham dismissed natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts".

John Stuart Mill's answer is that a society whose members could be killed by the government for another's happiness is less happy than one where everyone is protected, so protection enters the sum. Ursula K. Le Guin, the American author of speculative fiction, puts the trade at the centre of a city in _The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas_. Everyone in Omelas is happy, healthy, and rich, and that happiness rests on one child kept in a dungeon in perpetual filth, darkness, and misery. The city is presented as a fairy tale, and each reader is invited to imagine their own perfect city in its place. Every citizen, on coming of age, sees the child, shows it no love, and consents to its continued misery; some then walk away from the city, towards a place even less imaginable that may not exist.

The story is read as an attack on utilitarianism. John Stuart Mill would answer that it misreads him, since _On Liberty_ belongs with _Utilitarianism_.

### demandingness

Act utilitarianism asks the agent to weigh the consequences of everything they might do and pick the best option, which nobody can do for every choice.

The agent can estimate from past experience of similar acts instead of calculating afresh each time. Rule utilitarianism takes the estimate further by judging whole classes of act, so ordinary moral rules carry the work and the agent rarely calculates.

## utility and rights

John Stuart Mill's _Utilitarianism_ and _On Liberty_ are two halves of one argument: the first principle of utility is the protection of the rights of all. Mill held that rights can be accounted for on purely utilitarian terms, so a right stands on the welfare its protection produces rather than on a separate foundation.
