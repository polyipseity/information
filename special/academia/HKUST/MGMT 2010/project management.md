# project management

Project management is the work of getting a team to a fixed deadline with a finished product. It turns a brief into tasks, names an owner and a date for each, and orders them by what waits on what. What it tests is coordination: a team can hold every ability the product needs and still miss the deadline.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the work of turning a brief into owned, dated, and ordered tasks ::@:: Project management, which gets a team to a fixed deadline with a finished product.
- what project management coordinates ::@:: Tasks rather than the work itself: who owns each one, when it is due, and what waits on it.

## coordination

Ability answers whether the work can be done. Coordination answers who does each task and by when.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what coordination answers that ability does not ::@:: Who does each task, and by when.

### shared work and missing owners

Eight people can share one document, each assuming the rest will handle what they leave, and reach the deadline with the word count over the limit, three references instead of five, and nobody cast in the role play. Nobody lacked ability. The group lacked an owner: a task nobody is named to do has nobody to notice it going undone.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- why an able group can still miss a deadline ::@:: No task had an owner, so nobody was named to format the document, check the references, rehearse the role play, or submit.
- what sinks a task shared by the whole group ::@:: It is left to everyone, so no one notices it is not being done.

### ownership, timing, and dependency

Every task answers three questions: who owns it, when it is due, and who depends on it. A task that cannot answer them has not been planned.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- coordination ::@:: Everyone knowing what they own, when it is due, and who depends on it, rather than everyone doing everything.
- the three answers every task must carry ::@:: What one owns, when it is due, and who depends on it.

## planning the work

A plan starts from the brief, which states what must be delivered and in what form, not from what the team would like to do. Each limit the brief states becomes a row: the word count, the number of references, the case the argument rests on, the length of the performance, the number of people taking part, and the file to submit.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- where a project plan starts ::@:: From the brief, because it states what must be delivered and in what form.
- what a stated limit in the brief becomes in a plan ::@:: A row of its own, such as the word count, the reference minimum, the performance length, or the file to submit.

### requirements

Each row of a requirements table carries the item, the person who writes it, the person who checks it, and the date. Writing and checking are two jobs, since an author is a poor judge of their own word count or reference list. A requirement counts as planned once both people are named and a date sits against it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a requirements table holds per row ::@:: The item to produce, the person who writes it, the person who checks it, and the date.
- when a requirement counts as planned ::@:: When a writer and a checker are both named and a date sits against it.
- why the writer does not check their own work ::@:: An author is a poor judge of their own word count, references, or formatting.

### team roles

A role names a responsibility rather than a person. The work divides into eight of them:

- project coordinator: chairs meetings, tracks actions, keeps the team on schedule, and submits
- ethics lead: keeps the ethical principle clear and tied to the simulation
- scenario designer: builds the setting, conflict, characters, and outcomes
- research lead: finds and checks the real case and the references
- writing lead: drafts and edits the written document
- rehearsal director: runs rehearsals, timing, transitions, and audience engagement
- role-play lead: casts the performers and writes the dialogue
- quality checker: tests formatting, word count, references, plagiarism and AI rules, and submission requirements

No role may be left blank: an unfilled role fails as an unowned task does.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- project coordinator ::@:: Keeps the team on schedule, chairs meetings, tracks actions, and submits the final document.
- ethics lead ::@:: Keeps the ethical principle stated plainly and tied to the simulation.
- scenario designer ::@:: Develops the setting, conflict, characters, and possible outcomes.
- research lead ::@:: Finds and checks the real-world case and the academic references.
- writing lead ::@:: Drafts and edits the written document.
- rehearsal director ::@:: Organises rehearsals, timing, transitions, and audience engagement.
- role-play lead ::@:: Casts the performers, writes the dialogue, and checks enough people take part.
- quality checker ::@:: Checks formatting, word count, references, plagiarism and AI rules, and submission requirements.
- what a role names ::@:: A responsibility rather than a person, so one person can hold two roles on a small team.

### team charter

A charter fixes the channel the team talks on, the response time it expects, the weekly meeting day and time, where files live, who calls an emergency meeting, and what happens to a member who misses a deadline.

A charter's second half fixes who decides: routine decisions, important creative decisions, the tie-break once the team cannot agree, who approves the final version, and who presses submit. Settling those answers costs a few minutes and prevents a deadline argument.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a team charter fixes ::@:: The channel, the expected response time, the meeting day and time, where files live, who calls an emergency meeting, and what happens when a member misses a deadline.
- what the decision rules of a charter assign ::@:: Who makes routine decisions, who makes creative ones, how a tie is broken, who approves the final version, and who submits.
- why the rules are settled in advance ::@:: The argument they prevent would otherwise happen at the deadline.

## ordering and risk

Tasks are not independent. The references constrain the writing, the writing constrains the rehearsal, and the rehearsal constrains the performance. A list that never states these orders is a to-do list. It fails wherever one part finishes after the part that needs it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- why a list of tasks is not a schedule ::@:: The tasks depend on each other, and a list never states which one waits on which.

### dependencies and schedule

The performance date is fixed. Working backwards from it sets every other date, and a dependency belongs on the plan wherever a task would otherwise start out of order.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a schedule states that a to-do list does not ::@:: Which task cannot start until another one finishes.
- the chain that runs through a project ::@:: The references constrain the writing, the writing constrains the rehearsal, and the rehearsal constrains the performance.
- how the dates of a project are set ::@:: Backwards from the fixed deadline, so each stage sits before the stage that needs it.

### risk register

A risk register records what could go wrong, how likely and costly it would be, who watches it, and what the team does if it happens. The risks that end a student project are an unequal workload, roles nobody filled, an ethical analysis too thin to teach, and a rehearsal that never took place.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a risk register records ::@:: What could go wrong, how likely and how costly it would be, who watches it, and what the team does if it happens.
- the risks that most often end a team project ::@:: An unequal workload, roles nobody filled, a thin ethical analysis, and a rehearsal that never took place.

### testing a plan

A plan is tested by the questions it answers before the week it matters. What gets cut first if the rehearsal runs long names the trade while there is still time to choose. A simulation is simple enough when an employee could follow it in two minutes. A document and a performance are one project when the role play demonstrates the principle the written analysis explains. Submission has an owner once the team can name who presses the button, and by when.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- how to test a plan against a fixed limit ::@:: Name what gets cut first if the rehearsal runs long, before the week it matters.
- the question that tests whether a simulation is too complex ::@:: Whether an employee could understand it in two minutes.
- the question that tests whether the document and the performance agree ::@:: How the role play demonstrates the principle the written analysis explains.
- how to test whether submission has an owner ::@:: Name who presses submit, and by when.

## documentation and accountability

A written plan is also a record of who agreed to what, and that record makes an owner answerable afterwards. Accountability is answering for what a decision causes, to the people it falls on.

Documentation also restrains conduct. A person with something to hide would rather leave no trail, so a bank robber carries no list of the jobs. A team that writes down its decisions, and who made them, hands the next person something to check against.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- why a written plan restrains conduct ::@:: It records who agreed to what, so a decision taken badly has a person to answer for it.
- the comparison that shows what a written record does to conduct ::@:: A person with something to hide leaves no trail, so a bank robber carries no list of the jobs.
- accountability ::@:: Answering for what a decision causes, to the people it falls on.
