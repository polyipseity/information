# cognitive bias

A cognitive bias is a systematic error in thinking. It draws on what a person has perceived and remembered, and on what their society holds, so it shapes how they process information, read people, and decide. Its sources are heuristics, the mental shortcuts a mind uses to reach an answer quickly, plus social pressure and emotion. Those shortcuts are automatic, which makes decisions quicker.

Bias also names a tendency to lean for or against a person, group, idea, or thing, usually unfairly. It comes from human nature rather than one faulty mind, and it shapes what a person does as well as what they think.

- what a cognitive bias is based on ::@:: A person's perceptions, memories, and the beliefs that person and their society hold.
- what a cognitive bias can lead to ::@:: Irrational thoughts or judgments.

## conscious and unconscious bias

Conscious bias, also called explicit bias, is one a person knows they hold, in attitude and behaviour. It supplies a sense of identity and can lead to good decisions. It turns dangerous when it hardens into conscious stereotyping: a fixed, oversimplified picture of a group, applied to every member.

Unconscious bias is the narrower sense of cognitive bias: unintentional bias in attitude and behaviour that the person does not notice. It starts in the brain's attempt to simplify processing, so the errors sit in memory and attention, outside deliberate reasoning.

- where conscious bias can help a person ::@:: It supplies a sense of identity and can lead to good decisions.
- when conscious bias becomes dangerous ::@:: When it takes the form of conscious stereotyping.

- unconscious bias ::@:: Unintentional biases in attitude and behaviour that the person does not notice.

## information bottleneck

The brain takes in roughly 11 million bits each second and handles about 40 of them consciously. The gap is the information bottleneck. It does not close by trying harder, so the mind leans on heuristics. A heuristic's answer then arrives looking like perception rather than a guess.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- how much information the brain takes in and handles each second ::@:: Roughly 11 million bits taken in, against about 40 handled consciously.
- what the information bottleneck is ::@:: The mismatch between the roughly 11 million bits the brain takes in each second and the 40 it handles consciously.
- heuristic ::@:: A mental shortcut that reaches an answer quickly instead of examining every piece of information.
- why an unexamined heuristic is the problem ::@:: Its answer arrives looking like perception rather than a guess, so nobody checks it.

## implicit bias

Implicit bias covers the unconscious and automatic features of prejudiced judgment and social behaviour. Its associations can be favourable or unfavourable, and they activate without the person's awareness or control. That separates them from a known bias, which a person may hide for social or political reasons.

Everyone holds these associations, including judges who declare a commitment to fairness, and most people do not know their own. They need not match a declared belief. They usually favour the person's own ingroup, the group a person belongs to and identifies with, though research also finds bias against it. They can be changed.

A father and his son are badly hurt in a car accident and taken to different hospitals. The boy reaches surgery, and the surgeon says the boy is the surgeon's son. Nothing says the surgeon is a man, and a reader who assumes it gets stuck. The assumption rests on no stated belief, which is what makes it implicit.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- implicit bias ::@:: The unconscious, automatic features of prejudiced judgment and social behaviour, favourable or unfavourable, activated without awareness or control.
- how implicit bias differs from a known bias the person hides ::@:: Implicit bias sits below awareness, while a known bias is held knowingly and concealed for social or political reasons.
- how widespread implicit bias is ::@:: Everyone holds it, including people with avowed commitments to fairness such as judges.
- whether implicit bias matches a person's declared beliefs ::@:: Not necessarily: the associations need not match the belief the person states.
- which group implicit bias usually favours ::@:: The person's own ingroup, though research also finds bias against it.
- what an ingroup is ::@:: The group a person belongs to and identifies with.
- in the father and son puzzle, which assumption about the surgeon makes the puzzle hard ::@:: That the surgeon is a man. The surgeon is the boy's mother.

## belief formation

### confirmation bias

People seek out, notice, interpret, and remember information that supports what they already believe, and discount what challenges it. They do this outside awareness, so a person can feel objective while reading one side only.

Biased search keeps to sources that agree, such as news matching one's politics. Biased interpretation treats supportive evidence as strong and conflicting evidence as flawed. Biased memory keeps the facts that fit the view and loses the rest. Beyond one judgment, the bias hardens stereotypes, weakens critical thinking, and polarises opinion.

A question can carry the bias before any evidence is read: asking why a carnivore diet works puts the answer inside the question, while asking whether it works leaves it open.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- confirmation bias ::@:: Seeking out, noticing, interpreting, and remembering information that supports an existing belief while ignoring or discounting what challenges it.
- biased search ::@:: Looking only at sources that agree with the belief, such as news that matches one's politics.
- biased interpretation ::@:: Treating supportive evidence as strong and conflicting evidence as flawed or unimportant.
- biased memory ::@:: Remembering the facts and examples that fit the belief and forgetting the ones that do not.
- how a question can carry confirmation bias ::@:: Asking why a carnivore diet works presumes that it works; asking whether it works leaves the answer open.
- how confirmation bias weighs the evidence it meets ::@:: Unevenly: what proves the person right gains weight, and what suggests they might be wrong loses it.
- which is easier, finding confirming evidence or challenging one's own belief ::@:: Finding confirming evidence is easy; challenging one's own belief is not.
- what confirmation bias does beyond a single judgment ::@:: It hardens stereotypes, weakens critical thinking, and polarises opinion.

### availability heuristic

An event feels likely when instances of it come easily to mind. Plane crashes are widely reported and easy to recall, so they feel common although they are rare. Judged risk follows the news rather than the data.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- availability heuristic ::@:: Judging an event as more likely because instances of it come easily to mind.
- why plane crashes feel more common than they are ::@:: Crashes are widely reported and easy to recall, so their judged likelihood rises above their statistical frequency.
- what the availability heuristic distorts ::@:: Risk perception, because the judged likelihood follows the news rather than the data.
- what the availability heuristic distorts beyond the judged risk ::@:: Decisions taken under uncertainty.

### anchoring bias

Anchoring bias is relying too heavily on the first piece of information received, the anchor, when deciding or estimating. The anchor can be arbitrary and still pull the answer: a team shown a high anchor sets a higher value than a team shown a low one. The pull holds in negotiations, pricing, and everyday choices.

A car displayed beside a private jet sells as a small purchase, because the jet sets the reference. The pairing also carries brand alignment: a luxury car and a private aircraft both sit at the peak of engineering, so the aircraft's setting carries over.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- anchoring bias ::@:: Relying too heavily on the first piece of information received, the anchor, when making a decision, judgment, or estimate.
- why an arbitrary anchor still changes an estimate ::@:: The first reference point pulls later reasoning toward itself, even when the anchor means nothing.
- how displaying a car beside a private jet uses anchoring ::@:: The jet's price becomes the reference, so the car looks like a small purchase beside it.
- what a car displayed beside a private jet borrows besides a reference price ::@:: Brand alignment: a luxury car and a private aircraft are both the peak of engineering, and the aircraft's setting carries over.

### misinformation effect

The misinformation effect is memory altered by information met after the event: a witness who hears a misleading account later recalls a detail the account supplied, and that detail feels like memory rather than suggestion.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- misinformation effect ::@:: Memory altered by information encountered after the event.
- how a misleading account reaches a witness's memory ::@:: The witness later reports a detail the account supplied, and the detail feels like their own recollection.
- what the misinformation effect weakens ::@:: The reliability of eyewitness testimony and of personal recollection.
- what eyewitness testimony is ::@:: An account of an event given by a person who saw it.

## biases about the self

### self-serving bias

Self-serving bias attributes success to the person and failure to the situation. Athletes credit a win to hard work and mental toughness, and blame a loss on unfair calls or bad weather. In a workplace, people point to internal factors for being hired and to external factors for being fired.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- self-serving bias ::@:: Attributing one's successes to personal ability and one's failures to external factors.
- how athletes show self-serving bias ::@:: A win goes to hard work and mental toughness, a loss to unfair calls or bad weather.
- how the workplace shows self-serving bias ::@:: Internal factors explain being hired, and external factors explain being fired.

### Dunning-Kruger effect

People with low ability at a task overestimate their competence at it: a novice may believe they understand a subject better than the experts do. That overestimate brings overconfidence, poor decisions, and resistance to feedback.

Confidence does not follow competence in step. Someone who knows nothing begins with little confidence. The first stretch of learning lifts it to its highest point, before much of the subject is understood. Seeing how much remains unknown brings it down, and a long climb settles it on a high plateau once the competence is real. The stages are named Mount Stupid for the first peak, the Valley of Despair for the trough, the Slope of Enlightenment for the climb, and the Plateau of Sustainability for the settled top. <p> ![A green curve of confidence against competence that starts low at know nothing, peaks early, falls into a valley, then climbs to a high plateau at guru competence](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger%20Effect%2001.svg)

- Dunning-Kruger effect ::@:: The tendency of people with low ability at a task to overestimate their competence at it.
- how the Dunning-Kruger effect appears in a novice student ::@:: The student believes they understand a subject better than the experts do.
- what overestimating one's competence brings ::@:: Overconfidence, poor decisions, and resistance to feedback.
- how the curve of confidence against competence in the Dunning-Kruger effect reads: ![A green curve of confidence against competence that starts low at know nothing, peaks early, falls into a valley, then climbs to a high plateau at guru competence](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger%20Effect%2001.svg) ::@:: Confidence peaks early, before much of the subject is understood, falls once the learner sees how much is unknown, then climbs slowly to a high plateau.
- what the curve of confidence against competence in the Dunning-Kruger effect shows ::@:: A beginner's confidence at its highest early, a fall once the unknown becomes visible, and a slow climb to a settled plateau. <p> ![A green curve of confidence against competence that starts low at know nothing, peaks early, falls into a valley, then climbs to a high plateau at guru competence](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger%20Effect%2001.svg)

### optimism bias

Optimism bias makes a person believe bad events will find other people first. Someone who assumes they are less likely than their peers to fall ill underestimates their own risk, and the low estimate encourages risk-taking.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- optimism bias ::@:: Believing that one is less likely than other people to experience a negative event.
- what optimism bias does to a decision about risk ::@:: The estimated risk falls, so risk-taking rises.

## biases about other people

### halo and horns effect

The halo effect is an overall positive impression built from one trait, which then colours assumptions about a person's unrelated traits. The horns effect is the same mechanism with a negative trait: one bad quality colours the rest.

A charming employee gets a better performance review than their competence supports. The same pull appears elsewhere:

- a brand's whole range trusted after one good product
- an attractive person read as more intelligent or kinder
- an attractive defendant given a more lenient sentence in a mock trial

People want consistency, so a good impression and a bad one of the same person clash. Missing information needs filling, so the known trait stands in for the rest.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- halo effect ::@:: An overall positive impression built from one trait that then colours the assumptions about a person's unrelated traits.
- horns effect ::@:: The same mechanism as the halo effect with a negative trait, where one bad quality colours judgment of everything else.
- how the halo effect appears in a workplace ::@:: A charming employee receives a better performance review than their competence supports.
- what causes the halo effect ::@:: A wish for consistency, since conflicting views of one person are uncomfortable, and a need to fill gaps where information is missing.

### actor-observer bias

A person attributes their own actions to external causes and other people's actions to internal ones. One's own late arrival is traffic, and a colleague's is laziness. The workplace name for the pattern is attribution bias. Misunderstandings and unfair judgments follow, because each side explains the same event differently.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- actor-observer bias ::@:: Attributing one's own actions to external causes and other people's actions to internal causes.
- how the actor-observer bias explains two late arrivals ::@:: One's own lateness is put down to traffic, a colleague's lateness to laziness.
- attribution bias in a workplace ::@:: A colleague's failure is read as character, while one's own failure is read as circumstance.
- what the actor-observer asymmetry produces ::@:: Misunderstandings and unfair judgments, because each side explains the same event differently.
- what attribution bias is ::@:: The workplace name for the actor-observer pattern.

### false consensus bias

False consensus bias overestimates how far other people share one's beliefs, attitudes, and behaviour. A person takes their own opinion as ordinary, which hides how far others differ and makes compromise harder.

Projection does part of the work: a person reads their own attitudes and values onto others and treats them as normal. Belief that others agree validates the view and lifts self-esteem. Group settings strengthen the error: a room with no opposing view makes its own position look universal.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- false consensus bias ::@:: Overestimating the extent to which other people share one's beliefs, attitudes, and behaviour.
- what false consensus bias does to a decision ::@:: It hides the diversity of opinion, so compromise gets harder and a group hears only its own view.
- what projection contributes to false consensus bias ::@:: A person projects their own attitudes, values, and behaviour onto others and treats them as normal.
- why group settings strengthen false consensus bias ::@:: With no opposing view in the room, the group's own position looks universal.

## cost of bias

In talent, strong candidates are screened out and promotion goes to the familiar rather than the capable. In decisions, narrow input leaves blind spots, because a risk nobody raised stays unflagged. In engagement, people who feel unseen contribute less, speak less, and leave sooner.

The cost adds up as many small decisions about who gets the interesting work, each one reasonable alone.

---

- what bias costs an organization in talent ::@:: Strong candidates are screened out, and promotion goes to the familiar rather than the capable.
- what bias costs an organization in decisions ::@:: Narrow input leaves blind spots, because risks nobody raised stay unflagged.
- what bias costs an organization in engagement ::@:: People who feel unseen contribute less, speak less, and leave sooner.
- how bias usually shows up in an organization ::@:: As many small decisions about who gets the interesting work, each one reasonable on its own.

## preventing bias

Recognising a bias is the first step against it; the rest are strategies that train the unconscious mind to think differently. Metacognition is the examination of a person's own thinking. Slowing a decision down and improving its reasoning works against the shortcuts, as do strengthening memory and minimising distractions.

The four-step process is to acknowledge the limits of memory, seek another perspective while deciding, self-critique, and choose a strategy that prevents the error. A checklist puts that strategy into practice: each written item is verified instead of remembered, and the list is a safety protocol, built to prevent errors and ensure every necessary action is taken.

In a cockpit, a cross-check has one pilot verify the other's actions before takeoff, as when cabin crew confirm the doors are armed. Situational awareness keeps both pilots on one picture of the aircraft.

Point and call takes the fourth step furthest, by having a worker point at an object and say aloud what they are about to do: track clear, gauge in order, timetable confirmed. Naming the action raises attention, so a lapse is caught as it happens. In Tokyo, employees in white gloves point at anything that could be a hazard, on every platform and train. The network carries upwards of 13 billion passenger trips a year, with hundreds of trains entering and leaving each day.

A study by the Japan Railway Technical Research Institute found pointing and calling cut errors by 85 per cent. On a safety simulator with a grid of 140 buttons, a coordinate appears and the worker presses the matching button; a correct press earns a chime, a wrong or slow one earns none. Pressing without confirmation produced a 2.5 per cent mistake rate, and the procedure lowered it. Airports in Okinawa use it too.

On one sector from Narita to Taipei, a Boeing 747 crew spoke the word _check_ 74 times between the pre-start and secure checklists, covering taxi, takeoff, cruise, descent, approach, and landing. A third crew member ticked a mark on paper for each one and watched the two flying pilots throughout.
