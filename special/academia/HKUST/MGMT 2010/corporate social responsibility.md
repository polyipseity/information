# corporate social responsibility

<!-- check: ignore-next-line[header_style]: proper noun -->
## Carroll's pyramid (Carroll, 1991)

Corporate social responsibility is the obligation a firm owes the stakeholders it affects and depends on.

A. B. Carroll arranged them as a four-level pyramid in 1991. Each level rests on the one below:

| Level | Responsibility | Expectation | Obligation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base | Economic | Be profitable | Required by capitalism |
| Second | Legal | Obey the law | Required by stakeholders |
| Third | Ethical | Be ethical | Expected by stakeholders |
| Top | Philanthropic | Be a good global corporate citizen | Desired by stakeholders |

A firm that cannot sustain itself cannot meet a higher duty. Profit sits at the base. <p> ![Four bands narrowing upward, labelled economic responsibility, legal responsibility, ethical responsibility, and philanthropic responsibility](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/The%20pyramid%20of%20corporate%20social%20responsibility.png)

> Source: Carroll, A. B. (1991). The Pyramid of Corporate Social Responsibility: Toward the Moral Management of Organizational Stakeholders. _Business Horizons_, 34(4), 39-48.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- Carroll's corporate social responsibility pyramid ::@:: Base to apex: economic (profit), legal (law), ethical (what stakeholders expect), philanthropic (voluntary good). Each rests on the one below.
- Economic responsibility ::@:: Capitalism requires profit, and every higher duty rests on it.
- Legal responsibility ::@:: Obey the law; stakeholders require it.
- Ethical responsibility ::@:: Meet what stakeholders expect beyond the law.
- Philanthropic responsibility ::@:: Voluntary. Stakeholders want it but do not require it.
- Carroll's pyramid drawn as four bands <p> ![Four bands narrowing upward, labelled economic, legal, ethical, and philanthropic responsibility](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/The%20pyramid%20of%20corporate%20social%20responsibility.png) ::@:: Economic, legal, ethical, and philanthropic responsibility, in that order from base to apex.
- which band of Carroll's pyramid is widest and which narrowest ::@:: The economic band at the base is widest; the philanthropic band at the apex narrowest. <p> ![Four bands narrowing upward](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/The%20pyramid%20of%20corporate%20social%20responsibility.png)
- what corporate social responsibility obliges a firm to do ::@:: What a firm owes the stakeholders it affects and depends on, at the levels of profit, law, ethics, and voluntary good works.
- who A. B. Carroll was and what model he set out ::@:: A. B. Carroll is the business ethicist whose 1991 article in Business Horizons, The Pyramid of Corporate Social Responsibility, set out the four-level model.

## stakeholder perspective

Internal stakeholders sit inside the firm: employees, managers, and owners. External stakeholders sit outside it: suppliers, society, government, creditors, shareholders, and customers. <p> ![A company at the centre with arrows to internal stakeholders on the left and external stakeholders on the right](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Stakeholder%20%28en%29.svg)

"Doing well" means profit, the economic layer at the base. "Doing good" means the ethical and philanthropic layers above it. When the two pull apart, managers weigh results against ethical duty.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- Doing well versus doing good ::@:: Doing well means profit. Doing good means ethical and philanthropic duties beyond the law.
- The central tension of corporate social responsibility ::@:: Profit can pull against what stakeholders expect. Managers then weigh results against ethical duty.
- the stakeholder map of a company <p> ![A company at the centre with its internal and external stakeholders](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Stakeholder%20%28en%29.svg) ::@:: A company sits at the centre, with its internal stakeholders on one side and its external stakeholders on the other.
- what a company's stakeholder map shows ::@:: Internal stakeholders (employees, managers, owners) on one side, external stakeholders (suppliers, society, government, creditors, shareholders, customers) on the other, arrows both ways between each group and the company. <p> ![A company at the centre, internal stakeholders on the left and external stakeholders on the right](../../../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Stakeholder%20%28en%29.svg)
- a stakeholder of a company ::@:: A party the company affects and depends on. Employees, managers, and owners sit inside it; suppliers, society, government, creditors, shareholders, and customers sit outside.

## competitive advantage

A competitive advantage lets a firm take customers, employees, or partners a rival would otherwise get. An ethical framework earns a firm five advantages. It brings top talent and customer trust, stronger partnerships, sustainability leadership, and resilience in a crisis. Sustainability means meeting present needs without spending what later users depend on. All five work through reputation. Employees, customers, and partners judge a firm on how it acts when nothing forces it to.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the five ways an ethical framework becomes a business advantage ::@:: Attracting top talent, building customer trust, strengthening partnerships, sustainability leadership, and resilience in crises.
- why an ethical framework attracts top talent ::@:: Candidates prefer an employer whose conduct they respect, so a firm known for its principles hires from a wider pool.
- why an ethical framework builds customer trust ::@:: Customers who see a firm keep its stated standards buy again and recommend it to others.
- why an ethical framework strengthens partnerships ::@:: Partners can predict how a firm with stated principles will act, so suppliers commit to closer, longer ties.
- what sustainability leadership gives a firm ::@:: A firm that meets environmental and social standards early sets terms rivals must later match, and draws customers who care about them.
- why an ethical framework makes a firm resilient in a crisis ::@:: Consistent conduct shields a reputation when a scandal hits the industry, so customers and employees stay.
- what a competitive advantage is ::@:: An edge that lets a firm take customers, employees, or partners a rival would otherwise get.
- what sustainability means for a firm ::@:: Meeting present needs without spending what later users depend on.

## frameworks in corporations

Patagonia's ethical framework rests on transparency, fair labour practices, and environmental stewardship. Johnson & Johnson's Credo guides ethical decisions by putting customers, employees, and communities first. Unilever's Sustainable Living Plan writes sustainability, diversity, and social impact into strategy. Each states its standard, so outsiders can hold the firm to it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- Patagonia's ethical framework ::@:: Transparency, fair labour practices, and environmental stewardship.
- Johnson & Johnson's Credo ::@:: A guiding document for ethical decisions that puts customers, employees, and communities first.
- Unilever's Sustainable Living Plan ::@:: A plan that writes sustainability, diversity, and social impact into business strategy.
- what Patagonia, Johnson & Johnson, and Unilever have in common ::@:: Each states its ethical framework and lets it steer strategy, so outsiders can check what the company does against what it says.
- an ethical framework of a firm ::@:: The principles the firm states explicitly, so outsiders can hold its conduct to them.
