# classical physics

_Classical physics_ is the body of physical theory that predates quantum mechanics and relativity; in historical discussions it means physics before 1900. By the 1890s it had three parts: mechanics, electromagnetism, and thermodynamics. Albert A. Michelson's remark in 1894 captures the confidence of the period. He said the more important fundamental laws and facts of physical science had all been discovered, and that future discoveries "must be looked for in the sixth place of decimals". As Auguste Comte (1798–1857) put it, to understand a science it is necessary to know its history.

## mechanics

Mechanics was given its experimental foundations by Galileo (1564–1642) and its laws by Isaac Newton (1642–1727). Newton unified the heavens with the Earth: the planets' motions about the Sun follow the same laws as apples falling from trees or a ball kicked toward a goal.

### laws of motion

Newton's three laws of motion describe how forces change motion. The first law, also called the law of inertia, holds that an object keeps a constant velocity unless a net external force acts on it, a body at rest being the case of zero velocity. It is also what defines inertial reference frames. In an [inertial frame](inertial%20frame.md) the first law holds without correction for acceleration. The second law states that the acceleration $a$ of a body is proportional to the net external force $F$ and inversely proportional to the mass $m$, $F = ma$, and in general force equals the time rate of change of momentum, $F = dp/dt$. The third law pairs the two forces: the force of body 1 on body 2 equals in magnitude and reverses in direction the force of body 2 on body 1, $F_{21} = -F_{12}$, the law of action and reaction.

### Maxwell's equations

Maxwell's equations state the theory: $\nabla\cdot D = \rho_v$, $\nabla\cdot B = 0$, $\nabla\times E = -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}$, and $\nabla\times H = \frac{\partial D}{\partial t} + J$. Together with the Lorentz force law $\vec F = q\vec E + q\vec v \times \vec B$ on a charge $q$, they explain much of electromagnetism: charges and currents create fields, and those fields in turn create further electric and magnetic fields.

### first and second laws

Thermodynamics rests on two fundamental laws, the first $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$ and the second $\frac{dS}{dt} \geq 0$. The first law in its working form gives the change in internal energy of a system as $\Delta U = Q + W$, the heat $Q$ added to the system plus the work $W$ done on it. It is energy conservation with heat included. The second law governs which processes can happen at all: heat cannot be converted completely into work with nothing else changing, heat does not flow spontaneously from a colder body to a hotter one, and no perfect engine, perfect refrigerator, or perpetual motion machine can be built. Work converts completely into heat; the conversion back never happens without some other change taking place.

### zeroth and third laws

The zeroth law states that two systems each in thermodynamic equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other: two systems at the same temperature as a third have the same temperature as one another. It underpins the concept of temperature, and it was not stated explicitly until the twentieth century. The third law states that it is not possible to achieve an absolute zero temperature.

## technology of the period

The period's technology was built on classical physics. Watt installed the first steam engines in 1776, Morse invented the telegraph in the 1830s, Sturgeon the electric motor in 1832, Bell patented the telephone in 1876, Edison the light bulb in the 1870s, Benz developed a gasoline-powered automobile in 1886, and Marconi established wireless communication in 1901. The Wright brothers made the first flight on 17 December 1903.
