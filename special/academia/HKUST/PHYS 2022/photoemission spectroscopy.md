# photoemission spectroscopy

Photoemission spectroscopy (PES) shines light on a material and measures the kinetic energy of each electron the light frees. The photon energy is known from the source, so subtracting the measured kinetic energy from it gives the binding energy that held the electron in the material. The technique is the [photoelectric effect](photoelectric%20effect.md) turned into a measurement, and photoemission is one family of [spectroscopy](spectroscopy.md).

## binding energies

The balance behind one point of the spectrum is $E_b = hf - K$: the binding energy of the level is the photon energy minus the kinetic energy the electron arrives with. The work-function offset between sample and spectrometer is calibrated out against a known line, so the axis comes out in binding energies. Each level of each element sits at its own binding energy, so counts plotted against binding energy come out as a peak per level. The peaks name the elements present.

The electron has to escape to be counted. One born deeper than a few nanometres scatters on its way out and arrives with less kinetic energy than its binding energy predicts, so its peak moves or it never arrives at all. Only the near-surface layers of a solid contribute clean peaks, so its photoemission spectrum is a surface reading.

## photon sources

The source decides which levels are reachable. Ultraviolet light frees valence electrons, and counting them maps the valence bands that take part in bonding: ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy. X-ray photons reach the tightly bound core levels, where each element has its own set. That type is [X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy](X-ray%20photoelectron%20spectroscopy.md).
