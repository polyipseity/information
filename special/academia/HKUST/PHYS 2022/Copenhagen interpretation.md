# Copenhagen interpretation

The Copenhagen interpretation is the standard taught account of what quantum mechanics means. It grew from the 1925–1927 work of Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, and Max Born in Copenhagen, and Heisenberg later coined the name. It is not a single formal statement but a shared stance: quantum mechanics is complete, probabilities are fundamental rather than a sign of hidden ignorance, and a quantum system carries no definite values for complementary quantities between measurements.

## three principles

Three ideas carry the interpretation. The uncertainty principle fixes a lower bound on the product of spreads of complementary variables, $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \geq \hbar/2$, so sharp position and sharp momentum cannot coexist in one state. Complementarity, Bohr's principle, holds that wave and particle aspects are mutually exclusive experimental arrangements rather than simultaneous properties: an apparatus set up to reveal diffraction will not reveal which-path information, and vice versa. The Born rule supplies the third: $|\Psi|^2$ is not a disguised density but a probability measure for the outcomes of many identical preparations.

## role of measurement

In the Copenhagen view a measurement is irreversible and special. Before it, the system evolves by the Schrödinger equation as a superposition; the measurement outcome is one value drawn with the Born probabilities, and afterwards the system is described by the updated wave function for that outcome. Bohr insisted on describing instruments and apparatus in classical terms, since every report of a quantum result is ultimately a classical record, a needle position, a spot on a screen. Einstein never accepted this division. He argued that the probabilities express ignorance of pre-existing detail, God does not play dice, and with Podolsky and Rosen he constructed a scheme in which correlated distant particles seemed to demand elements of reality beyond quantum mechanics. The Copenhagen reply has always been that no experiment reveals what the framework refuses to assign.

Schrödinger pressed the same worry in 1935 with a cat in a closed box, alive or dead according to whether a quantum event occurs. Nothing fixes which until an observer opens the box, against the intuition that the cat is one or the other at every moment.

## alternatives and standing

Later schools reject one or more pillars. De Broglie–Bohm theory keeps definite particle positions guided by a pilot wave, accepting nonlocality to recover determinism. Many-worlds denies collapse and keeps every branch of the superposition. Objective-collapse theories modify the Schrödinger equation so superpositions genuinely decay. None has displaced the Copenhagen reading in teaching: it remains the default vocabulary for states, measurement, and probability, and the alternatives are usually introduced only after that vocabulary is secure.
