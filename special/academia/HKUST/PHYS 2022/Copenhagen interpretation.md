# Copenhagen interpretation

The Copenhagen interpretation is the standard taught account of what quantum mechanics means. It grew from work Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, and Max Born did in Copenhagen between 1925 and 1927, and Heisenberg later coined the name. It is not one formal statement but a shared stance. Quantum mechanics is complete. Its probabilities are fundamental, not a sign of hidden ignorance. A quantum system also carries no definite values for complementary quantities between measurements.

## three principles

Three ideas carry the interpretation. The uncertainty principle sets a lower bound on the product of spreads of complementary variables, $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \geq \hbar/2$. Sharp position and sharp momentum therefore cannot coexist in one state. Complementarity is Bohr's principle. Wave and particle aspects are mutually exclusive experimental arrangements, not simultaneous properties. An apparatus set up to reveal diffraction will not reveal which-path information, and the reverse arrangement behaves the same way. The Born rule supplies the third. $|\Psi|^2$ is a probability measure for the outcomes of many identical preparations, not a disguised density.

- what complementarity means in an experiment ::@:: The apparatus decides which aspect appears. A diffraction setup shows interference and no path; a which-path setup shows path and no interference.

## role of measurement

In the Copenhagen view a measurement is irreversible and special. Before it, the system evolves by the Schrödinger equation as a superposition. The outcome is one value drawn with the Born probabilities, and afterwards the updated wave function describes the system in that outcome. Bohr insisted on describing instruments and apparatus in classical terms. Every report of a quantum result is ultimately a classical record, a needle position or a spot on a screen. Einstein never accepted this division. He argued that the probabilities express ignorance of pre-existing detail, and that God does not play dice. With Podolsky and Rosen he built a scheme in which correlated distant particles seemed to demand elements of reality beyond quantum mechanics. The Copenhagen reply has always been that no experiment reveals what the framework refuses to assign.

Schrödinger pressed the same worry in 1935 with a cat in a closed box, alive or dead according to whether a quantum event occurs. Nothing fixes which until an observer opens the box. Intuition says the cat is one or the other at every moment.

## alternatives and standing

Later schools reject one or more pillars. De Broglie–Bohm theory keeps definite particle positions guided by a pilot wave, accepting nonlocality to recover determinism. Many-worlds denies collapse and keeps every branch of the superposition. Objective-collapse theories modify the Schrödinger equation so superpositions genuinely decay. None has displaced the Copenhagen reading in teaching. It remains the default vocabulary for states, measurement, and probability, and the alternatives usually arrive only after that vocabulary is secure.
