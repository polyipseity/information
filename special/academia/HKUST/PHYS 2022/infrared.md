# infrared

_Infrared_ is the band of [electromagnetic radiation](electromagnetic%20radiation.md) just beyond the red edge of the visible spectrum, at wavelengths longer than any visible colour. Its wavelengths run from roughly $700\ \text{nm}$ up to about $1\ \text{mm}$. Its wave properties are those of the rest of the [spectrum](wave.md), and it is [thermal radiation](thermal%20radiation.md) like any other. Only the name and the range set it apart, and the name comes from the red, the last colour the eye can see.

A body at ordinary room temperature radiates almost nothing in the visible and most of its energy in the infrared. A black-body radiator below about $800\ \text{K}$ does the same, which is why it looks black while hot.

## the prism and the thermometer

In 1880, Sir William Herschel split sunlight with a prism and placed a thermometer in each colour of the resulting spectrum, reading the temperature each colour produced. He wanted to compare the heating power of the colours and expected nothing remarkable.

The thermometer read highest in the red region and lowest in the blue, both as expected. The reading that did not fit the expectation came from past the red, where no light is visible. A thermometer there should have read lower than in any visible colour.

## past the red edge

Past the red, the thermometer read higher than in the red itself, and rose further as he moved it along the spectrum.

A thermometer responds to radiant energy at any wavelength, not only to visible light. A reading that rises where nothing is visible means radiation the eye cannot detect is arriving. Herschel concluded that there must be an invisible form of light beyond the visible spectrum.

The eye responds only where light is visible. Bolometers and semiconductor detectors respond to something else, so they reach bands the eye misses. The ultraviolet was found the same way, from a detector reading outside the visible.
