# light

_Light_ is electromagnetic radiation, the part of the electromagnetic spectrum that the eye can see. Whether it travels as a wave or as a shower of particles was argued for about 150 years. Newton's particle view gave way after Young's double-slit experiment of 1801, announced in 1802, produced behaviour the particle model cannot account for. Most physicists held the wave view by 1830. Maxwell later identified light with the [electromagnetic wave](electromagnetic%20radiation.md) of his theory.

## wave and particle views

The two views differ over what light does at an obstruction. They were not judged equal at first.

### wave view

The wave view, associated with Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695), has light travelling through space like ripples spreading across a still pond after a dropped rock. Waves bend into a shadow, so light spreads past an obstacle instead of leaving a hard edge behind it. Shadows bear this out: light falling on a razor blade's edge gives alternating bright and dark lines all around it, and a magnified sharp edge shows the same bright and dark regions. The straight-line ray picture still holds where the wavelength is much smaller than the obstacles and apertures the light meets.

### particle view

The particle view, associated with Isaac Newton (1642–1727), has light travelling as a shower of particles going in a straight line until an obstacle deflects, reflects, or absorbs them. Particles produce straight shadows.

## Young's double-slit experiment

Thomas Young passed light through two narrow slits in 1801, and the Bakerian lecture on the experiment was published in 1802. A shower of particles would leave two bright lines, but the light formed a pattern of bright bands. That pattern is interference between waves arriving from the two slits. The wave view won out over the following decades, as Fresnel's diffraction work confirmed it. Maxwell later showed that the wave is the electromagnetic wave predicted by his equations.

Bright bands sit where the waves from the two slits arrive in step. For slits separated by $d$ and light of wavelength $\lambda$ the bands sit at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ for integer $m$, so the first bright band away from the central maximum obeys $\sin\theta_1 = \lambda/d$: spreading the slits apart shrinks that angle, and a longer wavelength opens it. Each slit's own width $a$ casts a single-slit envelope over the whole pattern, dark where $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$ for nonzero integer $m$, so a narrower slit spreads the envelope wider and lights more of the bands it covers.

## speed of light in water

The two views demanded opposite speeds of light on entering denser material. Let $c$ be the speed of light in vacuum and $v$ its speed in another medium: the corpuscular view explains refraction only if light speeds up, $v > c$, while Huygens's wave construction predicts the opposite, $v < c$. In 1850 Foucault measured the speed of light in water and found it slower there than in air. The corpuscular theory seemed incorrect, and the wave view stood.
