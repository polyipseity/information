# electromagnetic radiation

_Electromagnetic radiation_ is a wave of oscillating electric and magnetic fields, both perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. Maxwell's equations require such a wave to exist, and fix its speed in vacuum at $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}$. Here $\mu_0$ and $\varepsilon_0$ are the permeability and the permittivity of free space, and $c = 3 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Speed, wavelength $\lambda$, and frequency $f$ satisfy $c = \lambda f$.

## the electromagnetic spectrum

Waves are classified by wavelength. The spectrum runs from $10\ \text{m}$ down to $10^{-13}\ \text{m}$, and its frequencies from $10^{8}\ \text{Hz}$ to $10^{22}\ \text{Hz}$. The bands, in order of decreasing wavelength: radio and TV, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. Nearly all the [thermal radiation](thermal%20radiation.md) from an ordinary object lands in the [infrared band](infrared.md), immediately beyond red.

### visible light

Visible light is the narrow band the human eye sees. It runs from $700\ \text{nm}$ to $400\ \text{nm}$, red at the long-wavelength end and violet at the short-wavelength end. Orange, yellow, green, and blue fall in between.

## the medium and the aether

A wave was assumed to need a medium to travel in. $c$ is fixed by the properties of free space, so the equations called for a space-filling "ether". Its existence became one of the three unresolved problems of 1895, which [modern physics](modern%20physics.md) took up.

## generation of electromagnetic waves

Electromagnetic waves come from accelerating electric charges. A stationary charge creates a static Coulomb field falling off as $1/r^2$, and does not radiate. A charge moving at constant velocity produces a steady magnetic field and no propagating wave. Only acceleration ($\vec{a} \neq 0$) detaches the field from the charge. The changed field propagates outward at speed $c$, at the charge's oscillation frequency, with wavelength $\lambda = c/f$.

## detection of electromagnetic waves

A receiving antenna detects the wave: its electric field exerts a force $\vec{F} = -e\vec{E}$ on free electrons, driving a current at the same frequency. Heinrich Hertz (1857–1894) confirmed Maxwell's prediction in 1885, detecting electromagnetic waves $15\ \text{m}$ away. Asked about their applications, he called them "of no use whatsoever" and answered "Nothing, I guess."

## wireless communication

Guglielmo Marconi (1874–1937) turned the waves Hertz dismissed into a radiotelegraph system. On 17 December 1902 the first radio message crossed the Atlantic, from Glace Bay in Nova Scotia to Poldhu in Cornwall. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1909.
