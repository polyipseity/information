# X-ray

X-rays are electromagnetic radiation produced when fast-moving electrons interact with matter. Wilhelm Röntgen discovered them on 8 November 1895. Laboratories produce them mainly through bremsstrahlung and characteristic X-ray emission.

## discovery

Röntgen found them while studying cathode rays passing through various materials. He noticed that a phosphorescent screen near the tube glowed, and deduced that X-rays were being produced when cathode rays struck the glass walls. The new rays were unaffected by magnetic fields, so they carried no charge. Röntgen named them X rays and received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901. He established three properties of the rays: they penetrated materials further than cathode rays, they made many materials fluoresce, and they darkened photographic film. The same rays damaged living tissue, which made them dangerous as well as useful.

## bremsstrahlung

In an X-ray tube, thermionic emission electrons are accelerated by thousands of volts and impinge on a metal target. When an energetic electron passes near a nucleus in the target, it decelerates and radiates a photon. This braking radiation is called bremsstrahlung (German for "braking radiation"). The photon energy equals the kinetic energy lost by the electron: $hf = E_i - E_f$, where $E_i$ and $E_f$ are the initial and final kinetic energies of the electron. The maximum photon energy corresponds to the electron losing all its kinetic energy in a single encounter.

The glass envelope of the tube is evacuated to a high vacuum. A filament (cathode) is heated by a separate current, causing thermionic emission of electrons. A high voltage (typically 30 to 150 kV) accelerates these electrons across the gap toward a rotating anode target, usually made of tungsten. The rotating target spreads the heat over a larger area to prevent melting. About 99% of the electron energy is converted to heat; only about 1% emerges as X-rays. The voltage determines the maximum photon energy ($hf_{\max} = eV$). The tube current, set by the filament temperature, determines the number of electrons and so the X-ray intensity.

The same voltage sets the short-wavelength edge of the spectrum. An electron that gives up all its kinetic energy in one encounter makes one photon of that energy, $hf_{\max} = eV_0 = hc/\lambda_{\min}$, so the cutoff sits at $\lambda_{\min} = hc/(eV_0) = 1.240 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{V}\cdot\text{m}/V_0$. This is the Duane-Hunt rule, found from the measurements before quantum theory explained it in 1915. It depends on the accelerating voltage alone: at a fixed voltage every target shows the same cutoff wavelength. X-ray wavelengths typically run from about $0.01$ to $1\ \text{nm}$. The continuous spectrum spans that band from the long-wavelength end down to this cutoff.

How bright the beam is follows the target and the voltage. The intensity rises with the square of the atomic number $Z$, since a heavier nucleus gives the electron a stronger field to radiate in, and roughly with the square of the voltage. Tungsten carries that load: its high melting point lets it withstand high electron-beam currents.

## characteristic X-rays

When a high-energy electron collides with an inner-shell electron, the inner-shell electron is ejected and leaves a hole. An outer-shell electron drops to fill the hole, emitting an X-ray photon whose energy equals the difference between the two shell levels. The energy levels are quantised and characteristic of the target element, which is why these are called characteristic X-rays. The shells carry letter names: the $K$ shell is $n = 1$, the $L$ shell is $n = 2$, and higher letters follow. The atom is most stable in its ground state, so a vacancy in a low shell draws an electron down from a higher one. Each drop is named by the shells it connects: from the $L$ shell to the $K$ shell is $K\alpha$, from the $M$ shell to the $K$ shell is $K\beta$, from the $M$ shell to the $L$ shell is $L\alpha$, and from the $N$ shell to the $L$ shell is $L\beta$. The same lines identify elements directly. In [energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy](energy-dispersive%20X-ray%20spectroscopy.md) an electron beam is driven into a sample and the characteristic X-rays emitted back identify the element, which makes the technique the standard compositional tool of scanning electron microscopy.

## Moseley's law

Henry Moseley measured the frequencies of the characteristic X-rays across the elements and found them tied to the atomic number $Z$, the number of protons in the nucleus. The $K$ series follows from a drop into the $K$ shell out of level $n$: $$\frac{1}{\lambda_K} = R(Z-1)^2\left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{n^2}\right),$$ with $R$ the Rydberg constant and $n = 2, 3, \ldots$ The $K\alpha$ line, the $n = 2$ to $n = 1$ drop, then has frequency $f_{K\alpha} = \tfrac{3}{4}cR(Z-1)^2$. The $(Z-1)^2$ carries a Bohr-like reading. In the $K\alpha$ line the falling electron comes from the $L$ shell. The single electron remaining in the $K$ shell screens the nucleus from it, so the falling electron feels an effective charge of $(Z-1)e$ and moves like a hydrogen-like ion. Bohr energies scale with the square of the charge an electron sees, so the drop into the $K$ shell radiates a frequency $f \propto (Z-1)^2$. In the general form $\sqrt{f} = A(Z-b)$, $A$ collects the level difference of the series line and the screening constant $b$ is about $1$ for the $K$ series, so Moseley's plot of $\sqrt{f}$ against $Z$ came out straight. The $L$ series screens harder. The $L$ shell lies outside the $K$ shell, so an $L$-shell electron has the two $K$ electrons between it and the nucleus, screened further by its fellow $L$ electrons. Fitting the $L$ lines gives $b \approx 7.4$. Moseley's law fixed every element's place on a numerical scale: the electron shell picture holds for all of them, not for hydrogen alone.

The plot also fixed what the periodic table is ordered by. That order is atomic number $Z$, the number of protons in the nucleus, not atomic weight, which is why potassium ($Z = 19$, $A = 39.10$) follows argon ($Z = 18$, $A = 39.95$) instead of preceding it. Moseley's own table ran from aluminium ($Z = 13$) to gold ($Z = 79$) and left three gaps at $Z = 43$, $61$, and $75$, elements nobody had yet found. The $Z = 61$ element, promethium, turned up around 1940.

## wave nature

Röntgen's discovery left the nature of the new rays open, and the effort to establish it ran through the early 1900s. Charles Barkla found that each element emits X-rays of its own characteristic wavelengths and that X-rays show polarisation, evidence that they are electromagnetic waves. By 1912 that reading was widely accepted, yet X-rays had never been diffracted the way visible light is. A grating diffracts a wave strongly only when its spacing is near the wavelength, and the suspicion was that X-ray wavelengths lie far below those of visible light.

The wavelength estimates soon put numbers on the suspicion: Wilhelm Wien and Arnold Sommerfeld placed X-ray wavelengths between $10^{-10}$ and $10^{-11}\ \text{m}$. Max von Laue's 1912 proposal answered with the crystal itself: the atoms sit in a regular lattice about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ apart, so a crystal acts as a three-dimensional grating at exactly the right spacing. X-rays passed through crystals produced diffraction patterns with maxima only along specific directions, and Laue's analysis of the patterns matched the measurements. X-rays are electromagnetic waves of very short wavelength. The reflection analysis of the same effect, and the structure determination it made possible, are [Bragg's law](Bragg%27s%20law.md).

Scattering from matter is elastic at these energies. An incident X-ray photon makes the electrons of an atom oscillate, and the atom re-emits a photon of the same energy in a different direction, with no energy transfer, no ionisation, and no electron ejection.

## synchrotron radiation

A synchrotron is a particle accelerator where electrons travel a circular path many times. Electric fields accelerate them and magnetic fields bend the orbit. When electrons traverse a periodic magnet structure called an undulator, they oscillate and emit electromagnetic radiation including X-rays. Synchrotron light sources produce bright, tunable X-ray beams for scientific research.
