# modern physics

_Modern physics_ is the physics that grew out of the discoveries of the 1890s and the questions they left open.

---

## unresolved questions of 1895

The controversy over [atomic theory](history%20of%20atomic%20theory.md) was still open in 1895: nobody knew whether atoms had constituents at all, and the structure of matter stayed uncertain.

Classical physics carried three problems into the 1890s. It needed a medium to carry electromagnetic waves, the "ether". It could not say why the electric and magnetic field differ between a stationary and a moving reference system, the problem of the speed of light. The Galilean transformation relates [inertial frames](inertial%20frame.md) at constant relative velocity. Maxwell's equations do not keep their form under a Galilean transformation. It could not account for [blackbody radiation](black-body%20radiation.md), a gap [Planck's law](Planck%27s%20law.md) later closed.

Lorentz and FitzGerald proposed in 1892 that space contracts along the direction of motion of the body, an idea that anticipated Einstein's theory of 1905.

## discoveries of 1895 to 1898

Wilhelm Röntgen, a German physicist, discovered X-rays on 8 November 1895. Pieter Zeeman, a Dutch physicist, found the Zeeman effect in 1896: a single spectral line splits into two or three lines when the sample is placed in a magnetic field. Lorentz explained the effect classically, as light emitted by electrons moving inside the atom with their frequency affected by the field. J. J. Thomson, a British physicist, found the electron in 1897. The Nobel Prize in Physics went to Röntgen in 1901, to Zeeman in 1902, and to Thomson in 1906. By 1899 Thomson had shown that radioactive, heated, and illuminated materials all yield the same negatively charged particles.

The electron came out of a Crookes tube, a glass envelope evacuated to low pressure with a cathode at one end and an anode at the other. A high voltage across the terminals produced cathode rays, a visible glow. Thomson placed a Maltese cross in the path of the rays. It cast a sharp shadow on the phosphorescent screen behind it, a sign the rays travel in straight lines.

He applied electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to the ray direction. The electric field deflected the rays toward the positive plate, showing that they carry negative charge. The magnetic field deflected them the other way. An earlier attempt by Heinrich Hertz had seen no deflection at all: the tube's poor vacuum left gas for the rays to ionise, and that masked the effect. Only a better evacuated tube showed it. He adjusted the two fields until the net deflection was zero, giving the velocity $v = E/B$.

The electric deflection alone gave the charge-to-mass ratio $q/m$. The ratio held for any cathode material and any gas in the tube, so the electron is a universal constituent of atoms. The accepted value is $e/m = 1.76 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C/kg}$, about a thousand times larger than any charge-to-mass ratio then measured for the hydrogen atom. Either the electron's charge is large or its mass is small, and either way the carriers of electricity are much smaller than atoms or molecules.

The charge on the electron came next. Robert Millikan measured it in the oil drop experiment around 1909, balancing the electric and gravitational forces on a charged oil droplet. His measurements did more than fix the size: they came in whole multiples of one basic charge, so electric charge is quantised, every observed charge is $q = ne$ for an integer $n$, and the electron carries $-e$ where $e$ is quoted positive. His apparatus produced $e = 1.592 \times 10^{-19}$ C, and later, more precise measurements give the accepted value $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C. Millikan's figure is low by about $0.6\%$, a gap set by the precision available at the time.

Radioactivity ran alongside this work. Henri Becquerel discovered it by accident in February 1896: he placed uranium salt beside a carefully wrapped photographic plate, and the developed plate carried the salt's silhouette, showing a very penetrating ray from the uranium. Marie and Pierre Curie took it further in 1898, and Becquerel and the two Curies shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903.

## revision of the classical foundations

The discoveries forced a revision of the assumptions behind the success of [classical physics](classical%20physics.md), and two theories drive it: relativity and quantum mechanics.

## open problems

Physics divides into three bodies of theory: classical physics, relativity, and quantum physics. Beyond them, quantum gravity, dark matter, and dark energy remain unexplained. The proposed routes to quantum gravity are string theory and loop quantum gravity.
