# modern physics

_Modern physics_ is the physics that grew out of the discoveries of the 1890s and the questions they left open.

## unresolved questions of 1895

The controversy over [atomic theory](history%20of%20atomic%20theory.md) raised questions of its own: it was not universally accepted, the constituents (if any) of atoms became a significant question, and the structure of matter remained unknown with certainty.

Three further problems were left open. The first was the existence of an electromagnetic medium, the "ether". The second was the observed difference in the electric and magnetic field between stationary and moving reference systems, which is the problem of the speed of light. The third was the failure of classical physics to explain blackbody radiation.

## discoveries of 1895 to 1898

Wilhelm Röntgen, a German physicist, discovered X-rays on 8 November 1895, and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901. Pieter Zeeman, a Dutch physicist, found the Zeeman effect in 1896 and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1902. J. J. Thomson, a British physicist, established the electron in 1897 and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906. Henri Becquerel, a French physicist, discovered radioactivity in 1896, and Marie and Pierre Curie took it further in 1898; the three shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903.

## revision of the classical foundations

The discoveries of the decade and the complications that followed required a revision of the fundamental physical assumptions that had made [classical physics](classical%20physics.md) succeed. The revision starts with two theories, the theory of relativity and quantum mechanics.

## open problems

The map of physics divides into classical physics, relativity, and quantum physics. Beyond them the questions are still open: quantum gravity, dark matter, and dark energy are unexplained, and the proposed routes to quantum gravity include string theory and loop quantum gravity.
