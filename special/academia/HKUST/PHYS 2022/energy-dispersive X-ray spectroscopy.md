# energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

_Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy_ (EDS) excites a sample with a focused beam and measures the energies of the characteristic X-rays the sample emits back, returning its elemental composition. It takes its place in X-ray spectroscopy, the branch that reads elements off their characteristic lines; [Moseley's law](X-ray.md#Moseley%27s%20law) ties those lines' frequencies to the atomic number $Z$. The physics is the [characteristic X-ray production](X-ray.md#characteristic%20x-rays) of an X-ray tube, run on the sample itself.

The technique is the standard compositional tool of scanning electron microscopy. The same focused beam that forms the image can be parked on one point of it, and the X-rays from that point name the elements sitting there.

## how it works

The chain runs as it does in an X-ray tube. The incident beam ejects an inner-shell electron and leaves a hole; an outer-shell electron drops to fill it; the energy difference between the two shells leaves as an X-ray photon. Each element's shell energies are quantised and its own, so the photon energies it emits are unique, and [Moseley's law](X-ray.md#Moseley%27s%20law) predicts where they sit.

The spectrometer sorts the arriving X-rays by energy and counts them. One spectrum therefore carries a peak per element present, at the position that element's shell gaps fix, over the background the sample's bremsstrahlung adds.

## against wavelength dispersion

The sibling technique is wavelength-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, which sorts the X-rays by wavelength through crystal diffraction and resolves the peaks far more finely. The energy-dispersive detector instead sorts each X-ray by the energy it deposits and collects the whole spectrum at once, so a measurement is fast and needs no crystal scan, at the cost of that resolution.
