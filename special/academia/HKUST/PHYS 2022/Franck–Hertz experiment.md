# Franck–Hertz experiment

The _Franck–Hertz experiment_ fires electrons through mercury vapor and records the current that reaches the collector as the accelerating voltage rises. The current does not climb smoothly: it falls sharply at regular voltage steps, and the excited mercury atoms give off ultraviolet light at a single wavelength. The experiment was the first electrical measurement to show that atoms take energy only in fixed amounts. James Franck and Gustav Hertz published it in 1914 and received the 1925 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

## what the current shows

The accelerating voltage sets the kinetic energy of the electrons as they cross the vapor. Raising that voltage from zero, the collector current climbs, then drops sharply at $4.9\ \text{V}$. It climbs again and drops at $9.8\ \text{V}$, and again at $14.7\ \text{V}$: each drop sits at a multiple of $4.9\ \text{V}$. The regular spacing is the point of the measurement. The drops sit at the same voltage increment each time because the electrons surrender the same energy increment each time.

A hot filament boils the electrons off, an accelerating field between filament and grid sets their energy as they cross the mercury vapor, and a decelerating voltage of about $1.5\ \text{V}$ sits between grid and collector. Only an electron arriving with more than $1.5\ \text{eV}$ counts, so one that has just lost $4.9\ \text{eV}$ in a collision falls short and drops out of the measured current. <p> ![a mercury vapor tube labelled in Catalan: a V-shaped filament at the left end, a wire-mesh grid across the middle and an anode plate at the right, two electrons drawn in flight from filament to grid, a voltmeter and a 0 to 40 volt accelerating supply between filament and grid, a 6 volt filament supply, and a 1.5 volt retarding supply with an ammeter between grid and anode](attachments/Aparell%20Franck-Hertz.png)

The first peak itself carries the tube's metals rather than the vapor. The filament and the grid are dissimilar metals with different work functions, so the first peak sits away from $4.9\ \text{V}$ even though the peaks stay $4.9\ \text{V}$ apart.

## how the collisions transfer energy

Mercury's first excited state lies $4.9\ \text{eV}$ above the ground state, and that number decides everything. An electron crossing the vapor with less than $4.9\ \text{eV}$ of kinetic energy cannot excite a mercury atom: no level lies within reach, so the collision is elastic and the electron keeps its energy and keeps moving towards the collector. An electron carrying $4.9\ \text{eV}$ or more can excite the atom, which absorbs exactly $4.9\ \text{eV}$, and the electron flies on with whatever energy remains. An electron that surrenders $4.9\ \text{eV}$ late in its flight no longer has the energy to reach the collector, so the measured current drops. Accelerated again after such a loss, the electron accrues another $4.9\ \text{eV}$, and the pattern repeats.

## light from the excited atoms

The excited mercury atoms do not stay excited. An atom sits in the excited state for about $10^{-8}\ \text{s}$, then decays back to the ground state emitting a single prominent ultraviolet line at $254\ \text{nm}$. The photon energy is $hc/\lambda = 1240\ \text{eV nm}/254\ \text{nm} \approx 4.9\ \text{eV}$, exactly the energy the colliding electron gave up. The current drops and the emitted wavelength carry the same number through two different kinds of measurement. The pair also measures Planck's constant: taking the step energy as $E = hc/\lambda$ at the observed $254\ \text{nm}$ returns $h$ in agreement with determinations by other routes.

## what it established

Combining the two measurements gives the clue the experiment was after: a mercury atom accepts energy only in fixed amounts, $4.9\ \text{eV}$ at a time, and gives the same fixed amount back as light. That is the quantisation the [Bohr model](Bohr%20model.md) had postulated the year before, now measured electrically rather than inferred from spectral lines. Franck and Hertz first read the drops as ionisation of the mercury atom. The drops in fact mark excitation to the first excited state. Atoms have discrete excited states, and the Franck–Hertz data are the direct electrical evidence for them. The observation outlived its tube: [electron energy loss spectroscopy](electron%20energy%20loss%20spectroscopy.md) sends electrons of known energy into a material and reads the discrete energies they lose.
