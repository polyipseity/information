# neutron diffraction

_Neutron diffraction_ aims a beam of thermal or cold neutrons at a sample and reads the sample's atomic structure from the diffraction pattern the beam leaves. The neutrons arrive carrying the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ of their matter wave, so the pattern forms where Bragg's law $n\lambda = 2d \sin\theta$ is met. The technique takes its place beside [X-ray crystallography](X-ray%20crystallography.md) and [low-energy electron diffraction](low-energy%20electron%20diffraction.md) in the diffraction family.

## beam wavelength

The beam's wavelength has to reach the plane spacings it will measure. A neutron in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings carries kinetic energy $3kT/2$, so $p = \sqrt{3mkT}$ and $\lambda = h/\sqrt{3mkT} = 2.52\ \text{nm}\cdot\text{K}^{1/2}/\sqrt{T}$: $0.145\ \text{nm}$ at $300\ \text{K}$ and $0.287\ \text{nm}$ at $77\ \text{K}$, both within the range of crystal plane spacings. The derivation sits with the [de Broglie hypothesis](matter%20wave.md#de%20broglie%20hypothesis). Cooling lengthens the wavelength, so cold and supercold neutron beams are prepared when a longer one is wanted. A neutron carries no charge, so stray electric and magnetic fields bend its path far less than an electron's.

## nuclear scattering

X-rays scatter from an atom's electrons, while a neutron scatters from the nucleus. The two beams carry complementary information about the same crystal. A neutron also carries a magnetic moment, so magnetic order inside the sample shifts the pattern and the technique maps magnetic structure along with atomic structure. The beam penetrates deeply, so a bulk sample can be measured rather than only a thin one.
