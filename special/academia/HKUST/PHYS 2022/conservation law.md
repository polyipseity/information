# conservation law

A _conservation law_ states that some measurable property of an isolated physical system does not change as the system evolves. Classical physics rests on four of them. Energy in all its forms is conserved in all interactions, and so is electric charge. Linear momentum is conserved in the absence of external forces, and angular momentum in the absence of external torque. Relativity makes mass part of the conservation of energy, and although the pair is sometimes called conservation of mass-energy, conservation of energy is still the term used. Elementary particles add two more: baryon number and lepton number are conserved too.

## invariances behind the laws

Each conservation law comes with an invariance: a way in which the laws of physics themselves do not change. Energy is conserved because the laws are the same at every time (time invariance). Linear momentum is conserved because they are the same at every place (space invariance). Angular momentum is conserved because they are the same in every direction (rotation invariance). Charge is conserved because of gauge invariance. Other parameters are invariant in some interactions and not others, time reversal, parity, and distance among them. Newtonian, or Galilean, invariance turns out to fail in relativity, where a new invariance principle takes its place. Galilean invariance itself holds between [inertial frames](inertial%20frame.md).

## status of the laws

The conservation laws cannot be proven. Physicists accept them as correct and use them as a foundation for explaining nature, and as long as an explanation works they do not ask why the laws hold.
