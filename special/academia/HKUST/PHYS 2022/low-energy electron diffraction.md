# low-energy electron diffraction

_Low-energy electron diffraction_, LEED for short, aims a collimated beam of slow electrons at a crystal surface and records where the electrons come back. The diffracted beams land as spots on a fluorescent screen. The beam runs at 30 to 200 eV, which gives the electrons the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = 1.226\ \text{nm}/\sqrt{V_0}$: about $0.22\ \text{nm}$ at $30\ \text{V}$ down to $0.09\ \text{nm}$ at $200\ \text{V}$, the spacing scale of surface atoms. The technique is the surface branch of the electron diffraction family. It descends from the 1927 [Davisson and Germer experiment](matter%20wave.md#davisson%20and%20germer), which found the scattering maximum off nickel where $\lambda = h/p$ predicted it.

## diffraction condition

The rows of atoms on the surface act as a grating of row spacing $D$. A maximum appears at the scattering angle $\varphi$ when $n\lambda = D \sin\varphi$, with $n$ an integer. The screen shows only those maxima, so the spot pattern reproduces the periodicity of the surface itself. Each spot's position is fixed by the periodicity that produced it.

## spot motion

Raising the accelerating voltage $V$ shortens $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2meV}$, and $\sin\varphi = n\lambda/D$ shrinks with it. Every spot then moves inward toward the central beam. The Cu(100) pattern recorded at $120\ \text{eV}$ contracts when the energy rises to $130\ \text{eV}$. Molecules adsorbed on the surface add their own periodicity, so the pattern grows the extra spots of that larger unit cell.

## reading the pattern

The spot positions give the first reading. Their arrangement shows the symmetry of the surface's periodicity, and an adsorbate layer's spots give the size and rotational alignment of its unit cell against the substrate beneath. The intensities hold the second reading. Plotting an intensity against the beam energy gives an I–V curve. Comparing such curves with calculated ones pins down the atomic positions on the surface.
