# Rutherford backscattering spectrometry

Rutherford backscattering spectrometry aims an alpha beam into a sample and counts the particles that bounce back out. It takes its place in the ion-beam analysis family, running the large-angle alpha backscattering of the gold-foil experiment as a compositional tool. Each run reads two independent pieces of information: how many alphas come back, and with what energy each one comes back. The count tells how much of what material sits in the beam's path. The energy tells which element scattered each alpha and how deep the scattering happened.

The count grows with $Z_2^2$ through the [scattering equation](Rutherford%20scattering%20experiments.md#rutherford%20scattering%20equation), so heavy-element layers stand out sharply against light ones. The count also grows with the number of atoms the beam crosses, so a thicker layer of the same material returns more particles. Turning counts into amounts runs through calibration against standards of known composition. The beam never damages the sample, so the measurement is non-destructive.

Each backscattered alpha keeps a fixed fraction of its incident energy. That fraction is the kinematic factor $K = E_{\text{out}}/E_{\text{in}}$. The kinematic factor is set by the masses of the alpha and the target nucleus and by the scattering angle. A heavier target nucleus gives a larger $K$, so it bounces the alpha back with more energy. Alphas scattered at the very surface arrive with the largest energy each element allows, so the high-energy edge of the spectrum identifies the element at the surface. An alpha scattered deeper travels further out through the material and loses energy along the way through the stopping power $dE/dx$. It arrives with less energy than a surface scatter of the same element. The energy axis below the high-energy edge doubles as a depth scale.
