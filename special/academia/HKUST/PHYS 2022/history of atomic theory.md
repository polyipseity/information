# history of atomic theory

_Atomic theory_ holds that matter is composed of particles called atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of matter that can be identified with a particular element. A molecule is either a single atom or a combination of two or more atoms, like or dissimilar, and it may consist of thousands of atoms.

## atomic models

Six models span two thousand years, and only the first two treat the atom as indivisible.

| year   | model        | who               | the picture                                                                                          |
| ------ | ------------ | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 400 BC | atomos       | Democritus        | Atoms are the building blocks of matter. The shape of an atom explains the behaviour of its element. |
| 1803   | solid sphere | John Dalton       | The atom is a solid, indivisible sphere.                                                             |
| 1897   | plum pudding | J. J. Thomson     | Negative electrons are embedded in a sea of positive charge.                                         |
| 1911   | nuclear      | Ernest Rutherford | The positive charges are located within a central nucleus.                                           |
| 1913   | planetary    | Niels Bohr        | Electrons are restricted to circular orbits with different energy levels.                            |
| 1926   | quantum      | Erwin Schrödinger | Electrons are clouds surrounding the nucleus, less dense away from it.                               |

The modern theory grew from a chemical law. Proust showed in 1799 that when elements combine into a compound their proportions by weight are always the same, water for instance always one part hydrogen to eight parts oxygen by mass. Dalton's 1803 answer was that each element is made of atoms with characteristic atomic weights, and those weights fix the proportions.

Two of the six rows hide more than the picture suggests. Thomson's plum pudding spread the positive charge uniformly through a sphere the size of the atom, with the newly discovered electrons embedded in it, and an early version had the electrons carrying all the mass. By 1907 the count of electrons in an atom was known to track atomic weight, which put the mass in the positive sphere instead. In 1910 the model gave the positive charge discrete units of equal size, each carrying $+e$, spread evenly with empty space between. The model also had to produce its own light. Heating the atom sets the embedded electrons vibrating about their equilibrium positions. Those oscillations radiate at visible frequencies when the positive sphere is about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across. The model never produced hydrogen's spectrum. Thomson tried for years and could not calculate it from his own model. Rutherford's nuclear row was drawn from [scattering data](Rutherford%20scattering%20experiments.md): the atom is mostly empty space, with the positive charge and nearly all the mass in a tiny central nucleus. The Bohr row carries a hidden failure too, since an orbiting electron radiates and would collapse the atom in $1.6 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{s}$ under classical electrodynamics; the [Bohr model](Bohr%20model.md) quantises the orbits to escape it. Thomson's electron work brought the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded for his research on the conduction of electricity by gases.

## evidence for atoms

No atom had been seen directly, and the first evidence came from bulk behaviour. Avogadro proposed that equal volumes of gas at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules; that number, $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ per mole, is now Avogadro's constant. Maxwell derived the speed distribution of atoms in a gas, and kinetic theory as a whole, built on atoms, accounted for specific heats and transport phenomena as well. Robert Brown observed the microscopic "random" motion of grains of pollen suspended in water, and Einstein explained that motion from atomic theory in the 20th century.

## evidence for atomic structure

By 1900 four clues said the atom was not indivisible; Dalton's 1803 picture of a solid sphere could not account for them. There seemed to be too many kinds of atoms, each belonging to a distinct chemical element, and a thing with that many kinds hints that it is not fundamental. Atoms and electromagnetic phenomena were intimately related, since atoms carry charge. The problem of valence showed internal structure at work: certain elements combine with some elements but not with others. And the discoveries of radioactivity, of X-rays, and of the electron proved that something smaller than an atom exists.

Radioactivity itself became the probe. Alpha, beta, and gamma particles smash atoms open to reveal the structure inside, the same strategy that later particle colliders run at far larger scales.

The probe found the structure. Alpha particles fired at gold foils mostly passed straight through, a few deflected through large angles, and about 1 in 8,000 came straight back. The atom this describes is mostly empty space, with the positive charge and nearly all the mass concentrated in a tiny central nucleus. [Rutherford scattering experiments](Rutherford%20scattering%20experiments.md) works through how that reading is drawn from the data.

## opposition to atomism

The atom was not universally accepted in the 1890s, and whether it had constituents was an open question. Ernst Mach (1838–1916) opposed atomism from logical positivism, on the ground that atoms are "unseen" and so their reality is in question. Wilhelm Ostwald (1853–1932) accepted that premise and called atoms hypothetical structures for bookkeeping rather than real objects. Measurement answered both men: radioactivity, discrete spectral lines, and the formation of molecular structures. The spectral lines were the hardest of the three to explain away. Each element emits at a fixed set of wavelengths and at no others, and no continuous model of a charge cloud produces such a set. The [emission spectrum](emission%20spectrum.md) records those fixed wavelengths.

The measurements grew sharper. Einstein's 1905 explanation of Brownian motion treated a grain's random walk through its many molecular collisions and yielded approximate masses and sizes for atoms and molecules. Perrin's 1908 experiment, built on kinetic theory, made four kinds of measurement that all agreed with Einstein's predictions and gave the first accurate values of Avogadro's number. Since 1908 the atomic theory has been accepted by practically everyone.
