# history of atomic theory

_Atomic theory_ holds that matter is composed of particles called atoms.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what atomic theory holds ::@:: Matter is composed of particles called atoms.

## atomic models

Six models span two thousand years, and only the first two treat the atom as indivisible.

| year   | model        | who               | the picture                                                                                          |
| ------ | ------------ | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 400 BC | atomos       | Democritus        | Atoms are the building blocks of matter. The shape of an atom explains the behaviour of its element. |
| 1803   | solid sphere | John Dalton       | The atom is a solid, indivisible sphere.                                                             |
| 1897   | plum pudding | J. J. Thomson     | Negative electrons are embedded in a sea of positive charge.                                         |
| 1911   | nuclear      | Ernest Rutherford | The positive charges are located within a central nucleus.                                           |
| 1913   | planetary    | Niels Bohr        | Electrons are restricted to circular orbits with different energy levels.                            |
| 1926   | quantum      | Erwin Schrödinger | Electrons are clouds surrounding the nucleus, less dense away from it.                               |

## evidence for atoms

No atom had been seen directly, and the first evidence came from bulk behaviour. Avogadro proposed that equal volumes of gas at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number $N$ of molecules (atoms), $6.023 \times 10^{23}$. Maxwell derived the speed distribution of atoms in a gas. Robert Brown observed the microscopic "random" motion of grains of pollen suspended in water, and Einstein explained that motion from atomic theory in the 20th century.

## opposition to atomism

The atom was not universally accepted in the 1890s, and whether it had constituents was an open question. Ernst Mach (1838–1916) opposed atomism from logical positivism, on the ground that atoms are "unseen" and so their reality is in question. Wilhelm Ostwald (1853–1932) accepted that premise and called atoms hypothetical structures for bookkeeping rather than real objects. Measurement answered both men: radioactivity, discrete spectral lines, and the formation of molecular structures. The spectral lines were the hardest of the three to explain away. Each element emits at a fixed set of wavelengths and at no others, and no continuous model of a charge cloud produces such a set. The [emission spectrum](emission%20spectrum.md) records those fixed wavelengths.
