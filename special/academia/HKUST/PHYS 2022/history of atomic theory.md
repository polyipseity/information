# history of atomic theory

_Atomic theory_ holds that matter is composed of particles called atoms. The word has meant different things at different times: an indivisible building block, a solid sphere, a sea of charge with electrons in it, a nucleus with orbiting electrons, and finally an electron cloud around a nucleus. By the 1890s the theory was still not universally accepted, and the constituents of the atom, if any, were an open question.

## atomic models

The models succeeded one another over two thousand years, and the atom was indivisible in them until 1897.

| year   | model        | who               | the picture                                                                                              |
| ------ | ------------ | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 400 BC | atomos       | Democritus        | Atoms are the building blocks of matter, and the shape of an atom explains the behaviour of its element. |
| 1803   | solid sphere | John Dalton       | The atom is a solid, indivisible sphere.                                                                 |
| 1897   | plum pudding | J. J. Thomson     | Negative electrons are embedded in a sea of positive charge.                                             |
| 1911   | nuclear      | Ernest Rutherford | The positive charges are located within a central nucleus.                                               |
| 1913   | planetary    | Niels Bohr        | Electrons are restricted to circular orbits with different energy levels.                                |
| 1926   | quantum      | Erwin Schrödinger | Electrons are clouds surrounding the nucleus, the cloud being less dense away from it.                   |

## evidence for atoms

The case for atoms was first built from what matter does. Avogadro proposed that all gases at the same temperature, pressure, and volume contain the same number of molecules (atoms), namely $6.023 \times 10^{23}$ of them. Maxwell derived the speed distribution of atoms in a gas. Robert Brown observed the microscopic "random" motion of grains of pollen suspended in water, and Einstein explained that motion with atomic theory in the 20th century.

### imaging atoms directly

A scanning tunneling microscope image taken at $5\ \text{K}$ shows copper atoms adsorbed on a Cu(111) surface as separate peaks. Raising the temperature sets them in motion: at $13\ \text{K}$ to $14\ \text{K}$ and again at $15\ \text{K}$, images taken over $30\ \text{nm} \times 32\ \text{nm}$ and $60\ \text{nm} \times 60\ \text{nm}$ at a bias of $U = 100\ \text{meV}$ show the atoms shifting position.

## opposition to atomism

Ernst Mach (1838–1916) opposed the theory on the basis of logical positivism: atoms are "unseen", which puts their reality in question. Wilhelm Ostwald (1853–1932) accepted the same premise, calling atoms hypothetical structures for bookkeeping rather than real objects; radioactivity, discrete spectral lines, and the formation of molecular structures were the experimental results at issue.
