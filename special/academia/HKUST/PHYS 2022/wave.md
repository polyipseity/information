# wave

A _wave_ is a perturbation of a mass distribution that propagates through a medium or through space and carries energy with it. The energy travels while matter stays put: a cork on water rises and falls where it sits as the wave passes and is still there afterwards, and a boat rocks and picks up kinetic energy, then settles again.

## wave and particle models

Energy reaches a distant point as a particle or as a wave. The two models differ in what they need to travel in and in how the energy is spread.

### particle model

A particle transfers energy by point-mass interaction, as a transfer of momentum and kinetic energy. It is a point mass with a well-defined position and an adjustable velocity. It is highly directional, and it propagates in vacuum.

### wave model

A wave transfers energy over extended regions, by way of vibrations and rotations of the medium. It is a perturbation in a mass distribution. It propagates in a medium, spreads in space, has a specific speed, and superposes.

## wave-particle duality

In classical physics, waves and particles are distinct: a wave spreads through a medium while a particle travels along a definite path. Quantum mechanics reveals that matter and radiation show both aspects. Louis de Broglie proposed in 1924 that any particle with momentum $p$ has an associated wavelength $\lambda = h/p$, where $h$ is the Planck constant. For a particle of mass $m$ and speed $v$, this gives $\lambda = h/mv$. Electrons, neutrons, and even whole atoms produce diffraction patterns when scattered from crystals, confirming that they behave as waves. The double-slit experiment shows this most clearly. Fired one at a time through two slits, the particles accumulate an interference pattern on the detection screen, each particle interfering with itself. The fringe spacing is $\Delta x \approx \lambda L/d$, where $L$ is the distance from slits to screen and $d$ is the slit separation. A which-way detector at the slits destroys the interference pattern. It determines which path the particle takes, and the measurement collapses the superposition.

## superposition

The wave equation is linear, so the sum of any two solutions is again a solution, and the combined wave is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements at every point and time. Standing waves are one such combination: superpositions of travelling waves. Two waves of nearly equal frequency $\omega_0 \pm \Delta\omega$ and wave number $k_0 \pm \Delta k$ superpose into a carrier at the average frequency surrounded by an envelope that waxes and wanes at the difference frequency $\Delta\omega$, the beats heard when two tuning forks of nearby pitch are struck together. The envelope is where the energy sits, and it moves even though each individual wave is a pure sinusoid.

## group velocity

The envelope of a superposition travels with its own speed. For the two-wave case the envelope's wave number is $\Delta k$ and its angular frequency $\Delta\omega$, so it moves at $u = \Delta\omega/\Delta k$; in the limit of a continuous spread of components this becomes the group velocity $u = d\omega/dk$. Individual crests move at the phase velocity $v_{\text{ph}} = \omega/k$, and the two differ. The group velocity is the speed of the localisation, of a wave [packet](wave%20packet.md), and in quantum mechanics it is the speed at which a particle moves.

- how the group velocity $u$ and phase velocity $v_{\text{ph}}$ can differ ::@:: Crests move at $v_{\text{ph}} = \omega/k$ while the envelope moves at $u = d\omega/dk$, so the two speeds differ.

## dispersion

A medium is non-dispersive when its angular frequency is proportional to its wave number, so that phase and group velocities coincide and a packet travels without spreading. The worked cases and their group-to-phase velocity ratios are set out in [wave packet](wave%20packet.md).
