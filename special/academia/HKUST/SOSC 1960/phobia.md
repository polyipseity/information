# phobia

A push into a swimming pool before a child can swim, or a spider down a sibling's collar, can begin a fear that lasts for years. A phobia is an anxiety disorder: an irrational, persistent, excessive fear of an object or situation, out of all proportion to the danger. A phobia comes with a drive to avoid the object or situation.

A phobia runs the whole [conditioning](classical%20conditioning.md) process. Phobias usually start in childhood or early adulthood, last beyond six months, and around three quarters of people with one have more than one.

## how a phobia forms

A neutral stimulus is paired again and again with one that already produces fear, until it alone produces fear. That is fear conditioning: ordinary conditioning with an aversive outcome. Fear of loud noises is one of the few things a child is born with. A loud noise is an efficient unconditioned stimulus, so the pairing takes fewer trials. A second stimulus the child does not fear needs more pairings.

### conditioning a child to fear a harmless object

In 1920 John B. Watson tried to condition a nine-month-old boy to fear something harmless. He presented a rat, a dog, a rabbit, a monkey, and masks with and without fur. None disturbed the child. Then he presented the rat again and made a loud noise as the child reached for it. The child startled and cried. He repeated the pairing several times, and the child cried each time. With no noise this time, the child was frightened of the rat and cried on sight.

The study is the standard demonstration of fear conditioning. It is also the usual research ethics case with children. No independent review preceded it, the distress was repeated on purpose, and nobody assessed the risk or followed up.

### stimulus generalisation

Watson brought back the earlier animals and furry objects. The child was distressed at a rabbit, a dog, and a mask of Santa Claus, none of which had been paired with the noise. The fear had spread to stimuli that only resemble the conditioned one. That spread is stimulus generalisation. An operant arrangement can spread the same way without conditioned fear. The cue stands for a whole class, so one remembered event does not identify the class. The original cause is unrecoverable. See [stimulus generalisation](stimulus%20control.md#stimulus%20generalization).

## why the fear lasts

The feared object turns up again and again, and nothing bad follows. The association weakens, and the fear fades. This is extinction, and it is why most frightened children never develop a phobia.

Avoidance stops extinction. A frightened child starts avoiding the feared object unless a parent or a clinician discourages it. The longer the avoidance goes on, the stronger the bond between stimulus and fear response becomes instead of fading. By adulthood a phobia can be severe enough to count as an anxiety disorder. It can last for years, with the fear never tested against the object.

## reversing a phobia

Exposure supplies the extinction that avoidance withheld. It works where medication does not. The feared object is presented, the feared outcome does not arrive, and the association weakens. Extinction inhibits rather than erases, so the method needs gradual, repeated exposure, not a single encounter.

Mary Cover Jones, a student of Watson, treated a two-year-old boy who feared rabbits. She raised his tolerance of them by gradual exposure, in the company of children who did not fear them. The method became behavioural therapy and gave rise to the exposure component of cognitive behavioural therapy, which is still recommended for specific phobias.
