# transcranial magnetic stimulation

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) sends a magnetic pulse from a coil held against the scalp. The changing field induces a current in the tissue underneath, and that current activates neurons in a small area. The current is brief, so it interrupts processing there. Processing resumes when the pulse ends.

A stroke leaves a permanent lesion. TMS leaves a transient one, so the same person can be tested before, during, and after the disruption.

The field weakens with distance from the coil, so a claim about a stimulated area covers that patch of cortex, not the whole hemisphere. The field reaches only the cortical surface. The pulse leaves the connections themselves intact, so it interrupts a region temporarily rather than cutting into the pathway.

## the interference with processing

A single pulse over the motor cortex can make a finger twitch. The same pulse, timed just before a participant presses a button, can block the press.

Over visual cortex it produces sensations of flashes of light, and it degrades a visual process where one is needed.

Timing the pulse against the task shows when a process happens. A scanner resolves space better, and pulse timing resolves time better. TMS is useful wherever a scanner has already localized the process.

Repeated pulses have effects that outlast the pulse itself. Those effects either dampen activity in the stimulated area or amplify it, and three things set which way they go: the intensity, the coil's orientation, and the frequency.

The repeated pulses act on plasticity, the capacity of connections between neurons to change. TMS treats disorders by changing how excitable the stimulated area is.
