[Classical conditioning](../../classical%20conditioning.md) sets out the four components each of these cases names.

A beverage company shows its logo on screen while an upbeat song you already love plays. The song is the US, because it lifts your mood without any learning, and that good feeling is the UR. The logo starts neutral and becomes the CS through repeated pairing, and liking the brand on seeing it on a bottle is the CR.

Eating salmon at a sushi restaurant and falling ill with a stomach flu a few hours later, when the salmon caused nothing, gives the clearest aversion case. The virus is the US, since it triggers sickness without any learning, and the nausea and vomiting it causes are the UR. The taste or smell of the salmon is the CS, and nausea at that taste or smell with no virus present is the CR. The same person can eat salmon again without becoming ill and keep the association.

A child bitten by a dog gives the fear-conditioning case. The bite is the US, the innate fear, pain, and defensive reaction is the UR, the sight, sound, or presence of a dog is the CS, and the learned fear and avoidance of dogs is the CR. The same four-step process becomes a [phobia](../../phobia.md) once the conditioned response clears a threshold of strength and generality, and elevators, driving, needles, storms, and heights fit the same pattern.

A conditioned compensatory response runs in the opposite direction to the unconditioned response, and what that costs in drug use is set out in [classical conditioning](../../classical%20conditioning.md): cues associated with morphine make a person more sensitive to withdrawal pain. A student who stops at the same cafe every morning for a coffee before a nine o'clock lecture gives the everyday version. The cafe becomes the CS, and the compensatory response is increased drowsiness and reduced alertness, opposite to the alerting effect of the caffeine. On a morning the lecture is cancelled, the student sits in the same place for breakfast, drinks nothing, and feels unusually sluggish, because the cues are present and the drug is not. The drowsiness is aversive and the coffee removes it, so the relief is negative reinforcement, and the student leans harder on the drug to escape a discomfort the environment itself creates. Taking the usual dose somewhere new, where no cue has been conditioned, leaves the body unprepared and the overdose risk higher.

[Preparedness](../../preparedness%20(learning).md) is what makes a taste aversion form faster, stick better, and attach to fewer cues than ordinary conditioning would predict. Oysters or raw fish followed by food poisoning give the case: the bacteria or the toxin is the US, the nausea and vomiting are the UR, the taste or smell is the CS, and later avoidance is the CR.

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the four terms a classical-conditioning answer has to name ::@:: The unconditioned stimulus, the unconditioned response, the conditioned stimulus, and the conditioned response.
- the shape of a taste aversion ::@:: The food becomes the cue because it was present just before the illness, and the brain cannot rule the two apart.
- the two things that separate a phobia from an ordinary aversion ::@:: It can follow a single intense pairing, and it generalises, so one dog becomes all dogs.
- when the compensatory response fires ::@:: The cues are present and the drug has not arrived yet.
- direction a conditioned compensatory response runs in ::@:: Opposite to the unconditioned response, so morphine cues make a person more sensitive to withdrawal pain rather than less.
- the three differences a prepared association has ::@:: It is fast, it is selective, and it ignores timing.
- threats a prepared fear forms for easily ::@:: Heights, darkness, and snakes, unlike cars and electrical outlets, which our ancestors did not evolve around.

> __Prompt.__ What is the role of prediction error and attention in classical conditioning? Use an example in daily life to illustrate their role.

[Extinction](../../extinction%20(psychology).md) covers the procedure, and a pizza delivery shows it plainly. The smell of hot pizza is the US and makes the mouth water, the UR. The doorbell rings just before the pizza arrives, so after many deliveries the doorbell is the CS and the mouth watering at the doorbell alone is the CR. When the roommate stops ordering, the doorbell still rings but now usually brings boring packages, and the salivation weakens each time.

Spontaneous recovery is the sudden reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period. A phone chime paired with good text messages gives the shortest case: the good text is the US, the happiness and excitement from reading it are the UR, the chime is the CS, and the excitement at the chime alone is the CR. The chime repeats all evening with no text and the excitement stops, and the next morning, after a night's rest, the chime brings it back with nothing new to learn from. Behavioural treatment of phobias and addictions teaches clients to expect and manage a relapse rather than to read it as failure.

The renewal effect returns an extinguished conditioned response when the CS is presented in a different context. A driving phobia gives the phases: in acquisition on the highway, the crash is the US, the fear is the UR, the highway and driving are the CS, and the fear of driving is the CR. In extinction on local streets, driving safely many times near home has the CS present with the US absent, and the fear fades. Back on the highway, the original CS returns and the fear returns with it.

[Blocking](../../blocking%20effect.md) is what a second cue meets once the first has already done the work. A student ID card at a study drive puts that second cue to a test. In the first phase, tapping the card brings a free hot coffee, so the coffee is the US, the salivation and heightened alertness are the UR, tapping the card is CS1, and the salivation at the tap is the CR. In the second, a flashing blue LED on the terminal lights at the exact moment of the tap, repeatedly, through dead week. In the third, the card scanner breaks and the blue light flashes on its own as the student approaches, and nothing happens, because the card already predicted the coffee.

Prediction error is the difference between the expected outcome and the actual one, and what it does to attention is in [blocking](../../blocking%20effect.md#prediction%20error%20and%20attention). A teacher always turns the projector off before giving a surprise quiz, and then starts closing the door whenever the projector goes off. The projector already predicts the quiz, so students pay little attention to the closed door. Then the teacher gives a surprise quiz after closing the door without touching the projector. The quiz is unexpected, so the prediction error directs attention to the door, and students begin learning that the closed door predicts a possible quiz.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what separates extinction, spontaneous recovery, and renewal ::@:: What brings the extinguished response back: nothing, the passage of time, or a change of context.
- what spontaneous recovery shows about extinction ::@:: Extinction suppresses the learned association rather than erasing it.
- how strong a recovered response is ::@:: Usually weaker than the original, and it fades quickly once the CS keeps appearing without the US.
- how spontaneous recovery differs from renewal ::@:: Spontaneous recovery is driven by the passage of time; renewal is driven by a change in environment.
- the three phases of the driving phobia ::@:: Acquisition on the highway, extinction on local streets near home, and renewal back on the highway.
- what renewal shows about extinction ::@:: Extinction inhibits the learned association, and the inhibition is tied to the context where the extinction happened.
- why the second cue in the boss's office stays neutral ::@:: It adds no new predictive information, and conditioning depends on prediction error rather than on co-occurrence.
- what attention does in conditioning ::@:: It focuses learning on the cues that best predict the outcome and leaves redundant ones alone.
- why a pairing alone is not enough to condition ::@:: A pairing that produces no surprise is a pairing nobody attends to.

> __Prompt.__ Use an example in daily life to demonstrate the relationship between classical conditioning and instrumental conditioning.

Washing the dishes runs the whole quadrant. It earns bubble tea, so the washing increases because something pleasant was added, and it stops the nagging texts, so the washing increases because something unpleasant was removed. Leaving the dishes brings a hated K-pop song, so the behaviour decreases because something unpleasant was added, and playing video games instead of doing homework costs the gaming time, so the behaviour decreases because something pleasant was removed. The four share what [operant conditioning](../../operant%20conditioning.md) covers.

[Stimulus control](../../stimulus%20control.md) is a cue coming to determine when a behaviour pays off. Putting on a pair of yellow shoes signals walk time to a dog, while slippers and ordinary shoes get no attention, so sitting calmly is rewarded only when the yellow shoes are on. A study contract runs the same arrangement with money: a friend holds a deposit, returns part of it for every hour studied, and takes a donation to charity for every hour skipped. The friend's presence then signals that studying pays and that skipping costs.

[Reinforcer devaluation](../../reward%20devaluation.md) is a drop in goal-directed behaviour once the outcome loses its subjective value. A bubble tea after the gym shows the whole effect. At first the bubble tea is highly desirable and motivates the person to keep going to the gym, because the exercise is associated with getting a reward. After drinking it very often the person tires of it, the value of the reinforcer goes down, and the motivation to exercise goes down too, even though going to the gym still leads to bubble tea.

A habit starts as a goal-directed action, consciously evaluated against what its outcome is worth. The morning coffee runs through that stage: waking up fatigued in the bedroom or kitchen is the stimulus, and going to the kitchen, operating the machine, and drinking is the conscious response. Within fifteen to twenty minutes the caffeine blocks tiredness and lifts energy and mood, which is positive reinforcement, and the morning dizziness and headache go away, which is negative reinforcement. Repetition and overtraining then hand control of the behaviour over from deliberate planning to automatic execution, and after hundreds of repetitions it runs from the dorsolateral striatum, which runs routines, rather than the prefrontal cortex, which handles conscious planning. The feet hitting the floor or the kitchen doorway starts the brewing without deliberation.

The two conditionings are chained rather than competing, and a workout playlist shows it with every term named. The endorphin rush after exercise is the US and produces the pleasure that is the UR. After many pairings the playlist is the CS and elicits the anticipatory excitement that drives the training, which is the CR. Going to the gym is an instrumental behaviour positively reinforced by the post-workout pleasure, and the playlist doubles as a discriminative stimulus signalling that the reward is available. If exercise stops feeling good, the music loses its effect quickly.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a behaviour is driven by ::@:: The value of the reward to that person at that moment, not availability alone.
- what the association becomes in a habit ::@:: Stimulus and response, so the cue alone triggers the act.
- what makes a behaviour a habit rather than a goal-directed action ::@:: It survives an outcome that has been devalued.
- the test for a habit ::@:: Insensitivity to the current goal, as when a driver takes the weekday route to work on a Saturday when the goal is the grocery store.
- how classical and instrumental conditioning relate ::@:: Classical conditioning gives a cue its motivational meaning, and instrumental conditioning strengthens the voluntary action that contacts the reward.

> __Prompt.__ Why was the bobo doll experiment criticized on ethical grounds? how could these issues be mitigated through ethics review committee mechanisms?

Three axes separate behaviorism from the cognitive revolution, and [psychology](../../psychology.md) sets out both movements. What they study: behaviorism looks only at observable behaviour and treats the mind as a black box, while the cognitive approach takes thinking, memory, attention, expectation, and information processing as measurable topics. How they explain behaviour: behaviorism through environmental stimuli and their consequences, by conditioning, reinforcement, and punishment, while the cognitive approach adds the internal processes that sit between the stimulus and the response. How they use observable evidence: the behaviorist treats the observable behaviour as the object of study itself, the cognitive psychologist uses it as evidence from which to infer the mental process, as in reading a longer processing time as a more complex process.

Behaviorism is deterministic, with the organism passive and shaped only by the environment, while the cognitive approach pictures it as an active information processor whose determinism is soft, with some free will left in. Under the cognitive approach the organism encodes sensory information, stores it in memory, and retrieves it, with schemas organising that information as mental frameworks and learning happening when a schema is created or an existing one is modified. Behaviorism runs laboratory experiments on animals focused on the stimulus and response relation; the cognitive approach runs them on human beings and uses brain imaging.

Imitation runs through four processes, as [observational learning](../../observational%20learning.md) sets out. A cooking video carries all of them: attention is watching, including the demonstrator's hand placement; retention is memorising the steps and the detail; reproduction is trying it in small trials and improving from what the observation showed; motivation is the reason or the reward for performing the act.

The [Bobo doll experiment](../../Bobo%20doll%20experiment.md) illustrates the shift because the children learned the behaviour by watching an adult model, with no direct reinforcement of the child at all. Internal cognitive processes must have taken place, and those are what behaviourism did not admit. The experiment also shows vicarious reinforcement, and it separated learning from performance. The focus moved from the stimulus and the response to attention and retention.

Four factors carry the most weight over whether a model is imitated. A senior in the major who skips lectures and still gets an A is the case: the model is similar and high-status, so the observer pays attention, and the behaviour is visibly rewarded, so it is copied, while a student who skips and fails is less likely to be copied. A model punished for a behaviour is not copied.

Four criticisms of the [Bobo doll experiment](../../Bobo%20doll%20experiment.md) stand out, and [research ethics](../../research%20ethics.md) holds the framework a review applies. The study trained children in aggression, and because observational learning forms cognitive habits the behaviour could persist into their real interactions. It induced frustration on purpose, upsetting the children by taking away appealing toys so that aggression would show. Independent oversight was missing, so the psychological risk went unassessed, and there was no debriefing, no follow-up, and no monitoring afterwards. The benefits were unclear at the time, weighed against risks to mentally immature children.

A review committee answers one question: when children are exposed to aggression for scientific purposes, how the foreseeable psychological and behavioural risks should be weighed against the expected knowledge. Scientific value does not remove the duty to protect vulnerable participants, so ethics approval belongs at the start of protection rather than at the end. Valid parental consent comes first, together with age-appropriate assent from the children explaining the procedures, the risks, and the rights, including the right to stop at any time. Then an immediate post-experiment session using prosocial models, to extinguish the learned aggressive behaviour, and mandatory debriefing with continuous monitoring. Less harmful methods are pushed for where they exist, such as observing natural playground behaviour or studying prosocial learning instead. The multiple approval stages then act as barriers against an inadmissible study reaching the field.

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the three axes that separate the two approaches ::@:: What they study, how they explain behaviour, and how they use observable evidence.
- vicarious reinforcement ::@:: A behaviour increases after seeing a model rewarded for it, and decreases after seeing a model punished for it.
- the four processes in imitation ::@:: Attention, retention, reproduction or initiation, and motivation.
- the four factors behind whether a model is imitated ::@:: Who the model is, what happens to the model, whether the observer can do it, and whether the observer wants the same outcome.
- the criticism of consent ::@:: Informed consent was missing, because young children cannot understand the study or advocate themselves to stop, and they have no choice.
