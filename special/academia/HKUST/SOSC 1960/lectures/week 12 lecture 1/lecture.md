## health psychology and stress

> __Prompt.__ 1.1. What is health psychology, and why is poor health related to psychology? How can clinical health psychologists help? (Slide 4)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.2. How does the biopsychosocial model differ from the biomedical model? Give an example of the mind-body connection. (Slide 5)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.3. What did Cohen et al.'s (1991) cold-virus study find about stress, positive mood and illness? (Slide 7)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.4. How do major life stressors differ from daily hassles? What are the three phases of Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome? (Slide 8)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.5. What is the relationship between stress and memory? How can prolonged stress affect the hippocampus? (Slides 10-11)

[Health psychology](../../health%20psychology.md) studies the part the mind plays in staying healthy and in preventing and treating illness. Poor health tracks psychology because behaviour carries much of the risk, and stress and mood act on the body. A clinical health psychologist treats disorders tied to poor health, such as an eating disorder, and the habits behind them.

The biopsychosocial model treats biology, psychology, and social circumstances as joint causes of disease, while the biomedical model looked only at physical causes. Psychosomatic medicine and psychoneuroimmunology test the link. Caregivers under long strain healed wounds about 24 percent more slowly.

The cold-virus study gave healthy volunteers a cold virus. Those who began less stressed or more positive were less likely to develop the cold, though everyone had been infected.

A major life stressor, such as a death or a disaster, forces a large adjustment, whereas a daily hassle is traffic or a queue. The general adaptation syndrome runs through alarm, resistance, and exhaustion or recovery.

Memory follows an inverted U, where a little stress helps it and a lot harms it. [Prolonged stress](../../stress.md) sends hormones that slow hippocampal neurons. Exposed long enough, the hippocampus shrinks.

## protecting health

> __Prompt.__ 2.3. What is self-efficacy, and how does it relate to health behavior? Give examples. (Slide 14)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 2.4. How do social integration and social support protect health, especially during stress? (Slide 15)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 2.5. What is Type A behavior? How do Type A behavior and positive emotions affect health? (Slide 16)

Coping, one of five [resilience](../../resilience%20(psychology).md) factors, is what a person does about a stressor. Problem-focused coping changes the event, as studying does for an exam. Emotion-focused coping regulates the feeling, as a film distracts from it. Problem-focused coping does more where the stressor can move. A death leaves nothing to solve.

A control belief protects health even when the control is not real. People told they could silence a noise did better at proofreading than people told nothing, though neither group could affect it. Older residents given a plant to water lived longer than residents without one.

Self-efficacy, the belief that one can perform adequately in a situation, decides whether a health behaviour starts.

Social integration is the number of social roles a person holds, without isolation. Social support is the help social ties give: a hug, a loan, or advice. Isolation raises the risk of disease and death by about as much as smoking does.

Cardiologists in the 1950s named the competitive, impatient, hostile, time-urgent patient Type A, a pattern carrying about twice the heart disease risk. Positive emotion runs the other way. Happier people live longer and recover better.

## health practices and medicine

> __Prompt.__ 3.5. How do communication and adherence influence whether people seek medical treatment? Give examples. (Slide 20)

Stress management works on the response when the stressor cannot be removed. Relaxation, breathing exercises, and exercise lower arousal. Biofeedback shows a person a normally hidden signal such as heart rate, then teaches strategies to change it.

The [protective habits](../../health%20behaviour.md) matter most under stress, when the immune system is already weakened and they are the first abandoned. Students under exam stress smoked more, drank more caffeine, exercised less, and slept worse.

The habits are regular activity, a balanced diet, seven to nine hours of sleep, no smoking, limited alcohol, and social contact. Changing a habit starts with noticing its trigger, setting one small goal, swapping in a healthier action, getting support, and tracking progress.

[Medical care](../../medical%20care.md) begins with who seeks it. Older people, women, and people from higher socioeconomic backgrounds go more often. Money blocks a visit, and so do preconceived notions about doctors or the illness. Stigma makes a condition hard to admit.

What the patient reports decides the diagnosis, and embarrassment or haste hides the detail it rests on. Adherence fails when patients stop medication early, leave a prescription unfilled, or do not change the behaviour.

## health psychologists

> __Prompt.__ 4.4. What is "Positive Health"? Why is health care shifting from illness care toward prevention and resilience? (Slide 22)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 4.5. What recent technological advances have been integrated into health psychology or mental health care to improve people's health? (Slide 22)

A clinical [health psychologist](../../health%20psychology.md) treats patients in hospitals, primary care offices, emergency care centres, chronic illness centres, and rehabilitation centres. Treatment happens inside a team of physicians, social workers, allied health professionals, and religious leaders. Whatever the setting, the psychologist weighs the physical, personal, and environmental factors that cause illness and hold back recovery, then plans treatment around them. A research health psychologist finds risk factors and tests interventions at a university, a public health department, a hospital, or a private organisation.

Behavioural medicine is an applied field close to health psychology that brings emotion, behaviour, cognition, and social factors into the treatment of disease. Its clinical areas include occupational therapy, hypnosis, rehabilitation, medicine, and preventive medicine.

Technology reports what asking misses. A pill bottle records when it is opened, and text messages improve adherence in depression, smoking cessation, and weight loss. Mobile health apps send medication reminders and collect outcome data remotely, and trackers such as Fitbit log exercise and food intake. A device that shows which situations raise blood pressure or heart rate lets an intervention target one group or one person.

Health care has mostly been illness care, managing symptoms once they appear. Prevention identifies risk factors and intervenes before the illness arrives. Positive Health names the shift towards what protects health, and studies people whose health is above average.
