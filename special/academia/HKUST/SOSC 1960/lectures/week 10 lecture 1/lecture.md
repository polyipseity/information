## research methods and theories

> __Prompt.__ 1.3. What are the main stages in Piaget's theory of cognitive development? Is it generally classified as a theory of continuous or discontinuous development? (Slide 7)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.4. How do sociocultural theories explain cognitive development? Support your answer with examples. (Slide 4)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.5. How do information-processing theories explain cognitive development? Support your answer with examples. (Slide 4)

A [longitudinal study](../../longitudinal%20study.md) measures the same people repeatedly, so a change it records happens inside one person. A cross-sectional study measures different age groups once, so a gap between groups mixes age with the era each group grew up in.

[Continuous development](../../developmental%20stage%20theories.md) adds small quantitative gains to an ability the child already has, as a vocabulary grows. Discontinuous development replaces one way of thinking with another, as when a child first reads a taller glass as more water and later knows the amount is unchanged.

[Jean Piaget's theory](../../Piaget's%20theory%20of%20cognitive%20development.md) runs through four stages in a fixed order: sensorimotor from birth to about two, preoperational from about two to six or seven, concrete operational from about seven to eleven, and formal operational from about twelve onward. Each stage differs in kind, so the theory counts as discontinuous.

[Sociocultural theory](../../sociocultural%20theory.md) traces development to other people and to the surrounding culture's values and beliefs, as when a parent's questions while counting items become the child's own.

[Information processing theory](../../information%20processing%20theory.md) traces development to the mental processes behind thinking and to the transition processes that change them, as when a child counts on fingers before recalling that three and two make five.

> __Prompt.__ 2.5. Does recent research support a more continuous or stage-like view of cognitive development? Explain with examples. (Slide 10)

[Object permanence](../../object%20permanence.md) is the understanding that an object continues to exist when it is out of sight. Piaget placed its acquisition at the end of the sensorimotor stage, and infants younger than nine months show a limited form.

Symbols and words arrive in the preoperational stage, but perception still dominates thinking. A child says the taller, thinner glass holds more water, because height is the dimension the child reads.

[Conservation](../../Piaget's%20theory%20of%20cognitive%20development.md#conservation) is the understanding that a quantity survives a change in appearance, and reversibility is the understanding that the pour can be undone. Both arrive in the concrete operational stage, where reasoning stays on objects in front of the child.

The formal operational stage adds deductive inference from premises, hypothetical cases, and systematic tests, as when a child concludes that a whale is warm-blooded because it is a mammal. The stage rarely arrives without formal education in scientific reasoning.

Recent research supports the more continuous view. Infants look longer when an object vanishes, which suggests they expected it to stay, and children's specific experiences influence when a developmental change occurs.

[Nature](../../nature%20versus%20nurture.md) is the biological endowment, and nurture is the environment from before birth onward. The case for nature rests on abilities present before experience could teach them, such as the fusiform face area's response to faces, though expertise could explain the same specialisation. Maturation is evidence too: the prefrontal cortex, which carries planning and flexible problem solving, keeps developing throughout adolescence.

The case for nurture rests on input the brain needs. Depth perception depends on patterned light combined with normal brain activity, and parental choices about the people and settings a child meets decide what the child practises. A trait the surrounding culture values draws warmer responses than one it dislikes.

Among kindergarten children, [phonemic awareness](../../phonemic%20awareness.md), the awareness of the individual sounds in a spoken word, is the strongest predictor of reading achievement in third and fourth grade. Training those sounds early prepares a child for phonics, which maps them onto letters.

Board games that move a counter along a numbered path have been proposed to teach numerical magnitude, the size and order of numbers, because that knowledge supports later arithmetic.

## sex differences

> __Prompt.__ 4.5. What factors may contribute to observed sex differences in cognitive development? (Slide 12)

Sex differences in variability show in the spread of scores, not in their averages. In the 1932 Scottish Mental Survey, boys outnumbered girls at both the lowest and the highest IQ bands, and girls clustered near the middle. The same pattern appeared in more than 320,000 pupils in the United Kingdom who took the Cognitive Abilities Test. A wider male spread puts more men among the highest scorers, and observers can read the group as more capable than it is.

On specific abilities the gaps run in opposite directions: women score higher on verbal tests such as spelling and language usage, and men on visual-spatial ones such as space relations and mechanical reasoning.

[Stereotype threat](../../sex%20differences%20in%20cognition.md) is the drop in performance that a stereotype about one's own group can cause. A woman told that women do worse on a mathematics test carries a doubt she did not arrive with, and that doubt lowers her score. Brain size explains little of the difference. Gender-stereotyped behaviour and individual differences are the other explanations.
