## why the brain

The [cognitive revolution](../../psychology.md#the%20cognitive%20revolution) is the shift from about 1956 in which psychology put internal processes back into its explanations, having spent half a century ruling them out. Its approach is to understand the mind in terms of internal representations and information processing. The approaches it developed were information-processing accounts of memory and attention, computational models written against a running program, psycholinguistics after Chomsky, and artificial intelligence after Simon and Newell.

[Cognitive neuroscience](../../cognitive%20neuroscience.md) arrived when the two halves of that history met. Behaviorism had removed the mind from the body; the cognitive revolution put it back as information processing but still had nothing to say about where the work happened. Imaging made mental activity something to watch rather than only infer, so cognitive psychology and neuroscience could be joined and the question of which brain part does what became askable.

The [structure-process trade-off](../../cognitive%20neuroscience.md#the%20structure-process%20trade-off) is what made the join necessary. Two different kinds of information processing can produce the same result, so the result alone cannot say which one produced it, and the deciding evidence has to come from outside the behaviour. Knowledge of the brain is what supplies it, so the trade-off is the reason the field needed the brain in the first place.

The three parts of the mammalian brain are the [forebrain, the midbrain, and the hindbrain](../../human%20brain.md#the%20three%20major%20parts). The complexity is in the naming: the same structures also carry the embryological names telencephalon through myelencephalon, and a third grouping, the brain stem, cuts across both by taking the midbrain, pons, and medulla out of the other two.

The three standard [anatomical planes](../../anatomical%20plane.md) are sagittal, which divides left from right, coronal, which divides front from back, and transverse, which divides top from bottom.

Directional terms are then read against those axes. Medial and lateral name the middle and the side. Anterior and posterior name the head end and the tail end. Superior and inferior name the head end and the feet end.

The last pair is not about position. Ipsilateral names two things on the same side of the body, contralateral on opposite sides. The left hemisphere controls the right side of the body, so almost everything crossing is described as contralateral.

## the cerebral hemispheres

[Grey and white matter](../../human%20brain.md#gray%20and%20white%20matter) are the two tissues the hemispheres are built from. Grey matter holds the cell bodies and dendrites, the parts that receive and integrate, and it covers the outside. White matter consists mostly of axons and is white because those axons are wrapped in myelin. The connections between the hemispheres are white matter: the corpus callosum is a bundle of myelinated axons, so it reads as white wherever it is cut.

[Subcortical structures](../../human%20brain.md#subcortical%20structures) lie beneath the cerebral cortex and above the brain stem. The thalamus is the switching station every sense but smell passes through on its way to the cortex. The hippocampus takes new memories in and moves them to long-term storage. The amygdala processes and recognises emotion, fear first among them. The basal ganglia carry voluntary motor control, habit formation, emotion, and reward processing. The cerebellum belongs here as well as in the hindbrain.

The four parts of the [cerebral cortex](../../cerebral%20cortex.md#the%20four%20lobes) are the frontal, parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes. The frontal lobe carries reasoning, planning, speech, movement, emotion, and problem solving. It holds the motor cortex. The parietal lobe carries visuospatial attention, body sensations, perception, and orientation. It holds the somatosensory cortex. The occipital lobe carries visual processing. The temporal lobe carries auditory processing, speech, visual recognition, and memory.

[Phrenology](../../phrenology.md) was the nineteenth-century claim that the shape of the skull reports the size of the brain tissue beneath it, and so the strength of the faculty sitting there. Its lasting contribution is the doctrine of cerebral localisation. Everything since has tested that claim on the tissue rather than on the bone above it.

The [visual pathway](../../split-brain.md#the%20visual%20pathway) runs from the retina along the optic nerve to the optic chiasm, where the optic nerves partially cross. Each retina has a nasal half, the half nearer the nose, and a temporal half, the half nearer the temple. The nasal fibres cross to the far side and the temporal fibres stay on their own side. What leaves the chiasm as an optic tract reaches the lateral geniculate nucleus, a relay station of the thalamus, then travels through the optic radiations to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

## split-brain patients

The medical reason was epilepsy. A healthy brain keeps the timing of activity in different regions out of step. A seizure puts it into step and spreads, so whatever region it starts in it reaches the rest. Cutting the corpus callosum stops that spread between hemispheres. The operation reduced seizures while leaving patients unchanged in personality and intelligence. See [why the corpus callosum is cut](../../split-brain.md#why%20the%20corpus%20callosum%20is%20cut).

The patient fixes on a point at the centre of the screen. The image is then flashed briefly, entirely on one side, for less time than an eye movement takes, so it reaches one hemisphere alone. Since the optic chiasm partially crosses, everything in the left visual field reaches the right hemisphere and nothing else does. The cut callosum keeps it there. See [sending an image to one hemisphere](../../split-brain.md#sending%20an%20image%20to%20one%20hemisphere).

The two hemispheres differ in language because the language areas are lateralised to the left. A right hemisphere shown something cannot say what it was. The left hand it controls points at or reaches for the object that matches it while the patient gives a completely different answer aloud. See [what the two hemispheres do separately](../../split-brain.md#what%20the%20two%20hemispheres%20do%20separately).

The left hemisphere interprets. Given a claw in one visual field and a snow scene in the other, the patient points to a shovel and says chicken, and explains it: the claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. That explanation is a story built after the fact to account for an act it did not initiate. It is offered without any sign that it is a guess. See [the left hemisphere as interpreter](../../split-brain.md#the%20left%20hemisphere%20as%20interpreter).

An Arcimboldo image is a picture built out of fruits and vegetables that reads as a face. Shown to a split-brain patient, the two hemispheres give two answers: the right hemisphere reports a face and the left reports the fruits. The right hemisphere read the whole, the left read the parts, and neither can be told what the other saw.

[Electroencephalography](../../electroencephalography.md) and [functional magnetic resonance imaging](../../functional%20magnetic%20resonance%20imaging.md) sit at opposite ends of the same trade-off. EEG has a great temporal resolution and a poor spatial resolution, because it reads a voltage that started elsewhere and reached the scalp. fMRI has a great spatial resolution and a poor temporal resolution, because it reads the oxygen in the blood and blood takes seconds to arrive. One says when and the other says where.

[Magnetic resonance imaging](../../magnetic%20resonance%20imaging.md) and fMRI use the same machine and answer different questions. MRI records the structure of the brain, the tissue itself, so it shows what is there. Computerized axial tomography is the other structure method. fMRI records the changes in oxygen levels in blood flowing to the brain during a task, so it shows what was working.

[Diffuse optical imaging](../../diffuse%20optical%20imaging.md) directs infrared light into the brain and measures what comes back. The light changes on the way through oxygenated blood and through active neurons. It reaches only one to three centimetres from the scalp, so only the superficial layers of the cortex. Set up to read blood oxygenation it has a low temporal resolution comparable to fMRI and PET. Set up to detect active neurons directly it has both a high spatial and a high temporal resolution.

[Transcranial magnetic stimulation](../../transcranial%20magnetic%20stimulation.md) applies a brief magnetic pulse to the head that induces a weak electrical current in the brain, interfering with how neurons communicate. Research uses it to interrupt a region at a chosen moment and read what the person can no longer do. That is what gives it a good temporal resolution. It reaches only the surface of the cortex.

[Transcranial direct-current stimulation](../../transcranial%20direct-current%20stimulation.md) uses the electrical current directly rather than inducing it with magnetic pulses, placing small electrodes on the skull. The current is low, no stronger than an AA battery, and it runs for longer than a magnetic pulse. The two methods answer different kinds of question.
