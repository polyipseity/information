> __Prompt.__ 1.5 What are the relationships among working memory, semantic memory, and episodic memory? Use an example to illustrate.

[Working memory](../../memory.md) keeps information alive only while a task is using it, and [episodic memory](../../memory.md) files an event with its time, place, and feeling. Holding an order number while the ticket screen updates is working memory; recalling the afternoon a friend dropped a full tray on the floor is episodic memory. Working memory carries three to five chunks and empties when attention moves on, so a mental multiplication survives only as long as it is being worked. Episodic memory lasts years and replays the scene: the room, what was said, and how it felt.

Semantic memory holds long-term facts with no date attached. Knowing that a bicycle has two wheels is semantic; remembering the day a dog chased you down the road on it is episodic. Knowing the Eiffel Tower stands in Paris differs from remembering coffee underneath it on a rainy afternoon. Semantic and episodic memory also trade places: a person can know they learned to ride at four and no longer hold the ride itself, so the fact outlives the event.

Emotion marks an event as worth encoding, so a car accident last month stays vivid while last week's lunch is forgotten. It also leaves an internal state behind that later becomes a cue, so material learned in fear or delight returns when that state returns. What it cannot do is improve accuracy. Confidence rises with vividness while the exact time, place, and words spoken still fade.

[Collective memory](../../collective%20memory.md) is the memory a family, a community, or a nation holds in common, and it belongs to the group's identity rather than to any member. People who never lived through an event still carry it, along with the stories and customs that grew around it. Emotion holds it together: a shared disaster leaves a whole region feeling shock, sadness, and vulnerability, and the emotionally charged version is the one encoded and passed down.

The three systems run one loop. New information arrives in working memory, is [encoded](../../memory.md) and consolidated into episodic and semantic long-term memory, and returns to working memory when a task needs it. Ordering a coffee draws on all three at once: semantic memory supplies what a café and payment are, episodic memory recalls last week's disappointing cup, and working memory holds the cashier's words while the cashier counts the change. Repetition moves knowledge the other way, from a dated episode to a fact stated without remembering when it was learned.

- the difference between semantic and episodic memory ::@:: Semantic memory holds facts and concepts with no time and place attached; episodic memory holds your own events with when, where, and how they felt.
- an episodic memory that has faded into semantic memory ::@:: Knowing that you learned to ride a bike at age four while the ride itself is gone.
- what strong emotion does to an episodic memory ::@:: It deepens the encoding and the confidence in recall, without improving the accuracy of the peripheral details.
- what collective memory belongs to ::@:: The group's shared identity, so members who never lived through an event still carry it with its stories.
- the route information takes through the three memory systems ::@:: It enters working memory, is encoded into episodic and semantic long-term memory, and is retrieved back into working memory when a task needs it.

> __Prompt.__ 2.5 What are the spacing effect and the generation effect in memory encoding?

A [flashbulb memory](../../flashbulb%20memory.md) is the detailed snapshot of where you were and what you were doing when shocking news arrived. Strong emotion concentrates attention on the central details, personal consequence marks the event as worth keeping, and surprise breaks what was expected. Rehearsal straight afterwards, replaying the moment and telling other people, binds the surrounding context to the facts. Reading a university offer in a bathroom, spotting a fire from the bus home, and escaping a near miss on the road can each produce a flashbulb memory.

[Recoding](../../memory.md) converts information into a form that makes sense to the person doing the remembering, and it decides how good the resulting trace will be. The examples each rebuild the material in a different shape. An acronym turns a list into letters: the seven characteristics of living things, movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, and nutrition, become MRS GREN. Categorising turns scattered measurements into groups: ten commute times become four short, four medium, and two long. Chunking turns one long string into a few units: a phone number splits into two four-digit chunks, and that packing is what lets more of it fit into working memory. A code also leaves room for error, because it interprets what was presented instead of copying it.

[False recognition](../../false%20memory.md) follows from a trace that stores meaning rather than wording. In the DRM (Deese–Roediger–McDermott) paradigm, a list of bed, rest, tired, dream, and wake leaves people certain they saw sleep, and honey, sugar, candy, and chocolate do the same for sweet. Perceptual overlap produces the same effect through appearance: cable, table, and stable make fable feel old, knight and night trade places, and a stranger in the same hoodie and build as a friend earns a hand on the shoulder until they turn around.

[Pragmatic inference](../../false%20memory.md) fills the gap between what was said and what was meant, and the fill-in is stored beside the words. Each example states one fact and leaves the next step for the reader to supply. A friend left dinner early with a headache: the reader supplies that she went home to bed, although the sentence only ever said headache. The cook cut the onion: the reader supplies that it was chopped into pieces. A classmate gamed until six before a nine o'clock lecture: the reader supplies that they slept through it. The karate champion hit the cinder block: the reader supplies that he broke it. What was said, the inference, and the stored version then run together, so the implied line is later recognised as the spoken one.

How practice is arranged changes what sticks. [The spacing effect](../../spacing%20effect.md): thirty minutes a day for a week beats the same half hour the night before. [The generation effect](../../generation%20effect.md): producing an answer beats reading it, as when people who generated the second word of a pair kept it better than people who only read the pair. Both are desirable difficulties: the delay and the effort make learning feel worse at the time and improve what lasts. A retention curve over a week puts generated material well above read material, even though massed rereading still feels easier.

- what recoding does to information ::@:: It converts the material into a form that makes sense to you, such as an acronym, a category, or a chunk.
- what a DRM word list exploits ::@:: The brain stores the meaning shared by the items, so an unstudied word carrying that meaning feels studied.

> __Prompt.__ 3.5 Does memory retrieval alter the representation in our memory storage? Why or why not?

[Retroactive interference](../../interference%20theory.md) runs backwards: new learning makes the old material hard to reach. For example, a new password hides the previous one, a new commuting route blurs the turns of the old, and the French word pomme surfaces where the Spanish word for apple was wanted. The effect is strongest when the two sets are similar, because similar traces compete for the same cue. Reviewing the old material keeps it alive.

Proactive interference runs forward, since established material blocks what comes after it. For example, Spanish answers intrude on French vocabulary, and the first date written in a new year carries last year's year. A well-practised old memory blocks the newer one when the two share contexts and cues.

The [misinformation effect](../../misinformation%20effect.md) changes a memory after the event, through information that arrives later. Recall rebuilds the record rather than playing it, so a leading question, another person's account, or a headline merges into the trace. Both demonstrations run through leading questions. A witness asked how fast the cars were going when they smashed reports more speed than one asked when they hit, and may report glass that was never there. Asked whether they saw the gun, people report a gun. Source misattribution explains it: the later suggestion is stored as part of the event, so confidence stays high while the memory has drifted from what happened. Neutral questions, witnesses kept apart, and statements taken at once hold the effect down.

[Availability](../../memory.md) means the information is still stored; accessibility means it can be brought out now, and the two come apart. A tip-of-the-tongue moment shows the split: the name is there and cannot be reached. An exam answer that arrives the second a hint lands says the same. Weak cues, interference, stress, or a changed context can each keep an available memory inaccessible, so availability is necessary for access without guaranteeing it.

Retrieval itself edits [storage](../../memory.md). Recall rebuilds the trace from the trace plus what the person believes now, and the rebuilt version is stored again, so each recall updates what is in storage. Practice strengthens whatever it reaches, so rehearsing a story also hardens any mistake inside it.

- what makes an interference effect strongest ::@:: Similarity: the closer the old and new material, the more they compete for the same cue.
- what the misinformation effect does to a memory ::@:: Information arriving after the event merges into the reconstructed trace, so a later suggestion is stored as part of the event and changes what a person reports having seen.
- why a witness may report glass that was never there ::@:: The word "smashed" is encoded with the crash and later read back as part of it, so the glass it implies is reported as seen.
- the difference between availability and accessibility ::@:: Availability is information still stored; accessibility is information retrievable at this moment.
- what a tip-of-the-tongue moment shows ::@:: The memory is stored but cannot be reached right now.
- what retrieval does to the memory it brings back ::@:: It rebuilds and restores the trace, so each recall updates the stored representation.

> __Prompt.__ 4.5 What is memory palace technique to enhance your memory performance? How and why does it work?

[Encoding specificity](../../encoding%20specificity%20principle.md) says a cue works to the extent that it matches what was stored with the memory. Divers who learned words underwater recalled them underwater at 32% against 23% on land, while divers who learned on land recalled them on land at 38% against 24% underwater. Studying in the room later used for the exam works for the same reason, because the room was encoded with the material. Forgetting under encoding specificity is a failed match rather than an erased trace.

State-dependent retrieval puts the cue inside the body. Material learned relaxed returns when relaxed. Someone who studied over three coffees draws a blank on an exam taken caffeine-free, because the facts were bound to that state. A penalty taker whose pre-shot routine falls apart after the goalkeeper's antics is recalling under a state that no longer matches practice. In each case the internal state is the cue that has to repeat.

[Cue overload](../../encoding%20specificity%20principle.md) means a cue tied to too many memories identifies none of them. The examples turn on how many targets share the cue. Give one picture among ninety-nine words and "recall the picture" brings the penguin; give twenty-five pictures and the same cue points in every direction. If every page is highlighted orange, orange retrieves nothing. Five people called Jack mean that "Jack" alone names nobody, while "Jack from psychology class" picks one out.

The [retrieval practice effect](../../testing%20effect.md) is the testing effect: pulling a memory out makes it easier to pull out next time, so self-testing beats rereading. Retrieval-induced forgetting is what that practice costs the items left alone. Practise apple and banana from a list of four fruits and orange and grape come back worse than if nothing had been practised, because the items share cues and the strengthened winners hold them down. Recalling one perpetrator's face again and again can cost the features of the accomplice beside them.

The [memory palace](../../mnemonic.md), or method of loci, turns a familiar route into a filing system. Choose a place, fix a path through it, turn each item into a vivid image, set one image at each stop, and walk the path to recall. It works because places outlast lists, a picture encodes more deeply than a word, and the route supplies stable cues in a fixed order. Recalling then becomes a walk rather than a search.
