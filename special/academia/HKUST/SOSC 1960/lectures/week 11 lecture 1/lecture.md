## theory of mind and schemas

> __Prompt.__ 1.1. What is theory of mind, and why is the false belief task used to test it? (Slides 4-5)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.2. What is recursive mind reading? Can current large language models do it? (Slides 4-5)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.3. What is a schema, and how does it simplify our social world? Where do our schemas come from? (Slides 6-7)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.4. Distinguish person schemas, self-schemas, and event schemas (scripts), and give an everyday example of each. (Slide 7)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 1.5. What are heuristics? Give examples to illustrate how they can sometimes lead to biased judgments. (Slide 8)

[Theory of mind](../../theory%20of%20mind.md) attributes a belief, a desire, an intention, an emotion, or a piece of knowledge to another person, and so makes sense of what that person does.

A false belief task gives another person a belief the child knows to be untrue, then asks where that person will look. The answer that keeps that belief apart from the child's knowledge shows theory of mind.

Recursive mindreading nests one belief inside another, as when Elaine does not know that Bernard feels she hardly knows him. Adults handle about seven levels. Large language models reach four before they falter.

A [schema](../../schema.md) is an internal representation of people, objects, and events, covering how one behaves and what one expects of others. Direct encounters, secondhand sources, expectations, motivations, and moods shape it. It saves working each situation out from scratch.

Person schemas cover other people, self-schemas the self, and event schemas, or scripts, a recurring event: a quiet roommate, a morning person, a meal out.

A [heuristic](../../heuristic.md) replaces a complex problem with a simple rule. The representativeness heuristic judges by fit to a category's picture, so a poetry reader passes for a professor though truck drivers are commoner. The availability heuristic judges by ease of recall, so a widely reported plane crash feels frequent.

## prediction and hot cognition

> __Prompt.__ 2.1. What is the planning fallacy, and why does it happen? (Slide 10)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 2.2. How do impact bias and durability bias distort our affective forecasting? Why do they occur? (Slide 11)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 2.3. How can motivations and moods (hot cognition) shape our reasoning about others? Illustrate with directional goals or motivated skepticism. (Slide 12)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 2.4. What is the need for closure, and how does it affect the quality of our decisions? (Slide 12)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 2.5. What is mood-congruent memory? Give examples to illustrate how mood can shape our thinking even when it is irrelevant to the judgment. (Slide 13)

The planning fallacy underestimates how long one's own task will take. The estimate pictures the best case and ignores how long similar tasks took.

[Affective forecasting](../../affective%20forecasting.md) predicts how a future event will feel, and gets the direction right while missing the strength and the duration. The impact bias overestimates intensity, because attention fixes on the event and leaves out what else shapes the mood. The durability bias overestimates how long the feeling lasts, more so after a negative event, because people do not allow for coping.

Hot cognition is thinking shaped by desires and feelings. A directional goal is an incentive to reach one outcome at the expense of reading the information fairly. It produces [motivated skepticism](../../motivated%20skepticism.md): doubt about whatever evidence threatens a wanted belief, however strong it is. People trusted a medical test less when it found a deficiency than when it found them healthy.

The need for closure is the wish to end ambiguity and settle on a decision, and time pressure raises it. A hungry shopper takes the nearest food without comparing, so the decision rests on less information.

[Mood-congruent memory](../../mood-congruent%20memory.md) retrieves memories whose valence matches the current mood, because the mood at encoding stays with the memory and cues it. A rainy day lowers judged life satisfaction, though the weather says nothing about a life.

## automaticity

> __Prompt.__ 3.1. How can experience make behavior automatic? Give examples to illustrate the role of automatic behavior in social cognition. (Slide 14)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 3.2. Give examples of automatic behavior that may arise from innate mechanisms (e.g., the fear response). (Slide 14)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 3.3. What is the chameleon effect, and why does it happen? What social function does it serve? (Slide 14)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 3.4. What is priming? Give examples of how semantic priming can shape our thoughts and social behavior. (Slide 14)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 3.5. How are stereotypes formed? Give examples of how they can influence social behavior without our awareness. (Slide 14)

An [automatic](../../automaticity.md) process is unintentional, uncontrollable, outside conscious awareness, or cognitively efficient, and one of those marks is enough. Repetition turns a hard new skill such as driving into one that runs with little attention, so the mind is free for the conversation.

Innate mechanisms supply automatic behavior too. Perceived danger triggers the physiological changes of fight or flight without a decision. A negative word shown too briefly to read is still detected faster than a neutral one.

Priming makes a concept easier to reach by exposing the person to a strongly associated one, so the word doctor speeds the reading of nurse.

The chameleon effect is the nonconscious copying of a partner's posture, mannerisms, and facial expressions. It earns liking: the mimicked partner likes the mimicker more, and the interaction goes better.

A [stereotype](../../schema.md#stereotype) is a general belief about a group. It forms as a schema does, from direct encounters and from secondhand sources. Once activated, it guides the judgment of a member outside conscious awareness. In a 1989 experiment by Patricia Devine, participants primed with words such as blues and basketball read a target's ambiguous behaviors as more hostile than unprimed participants did.

## attitudes

> __Prompt.__ 4.1. What is an attitude, and how does it influence our social behavior? (Slide 15)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 4.2. How do explicit and implicit attitude measures differ? Why can explicit measures be unreliable? (Slides 15-16)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 4.3. What is the Implicit Association Test, and how does it work? (Slide 16)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 4.4. What is the Evaluative Priming Test, and how does it work? How does it differ from the Implicit Association Test? (Slide 16)

<!-- markdownlint MD028 -->

> __Prompt.__ 4.5. Why do implicit biases arise, and how can we mitigate them? (Slide 16)

An [attitude](../../attitude.md) is a psychological tendency expressed by evaluating an entity with some degree of favor or disfavor. It turns behavior toward what the person likes and away from what they dislike.

Explicit measures ask the person to report the evaluation, and implicit measures infer it from reaction time. A report is unreliable on a controversial issue, because the answer can be edited toward what others approve, or the person may not know their own evaluation.

The Implicit Association Test sorts a concept and an attribute such as good or bad with one key, then reverses the pairing, and compares the times. The faster pairing is read as the stronger association.

The Evaluative Priming Test shows a positive or negative image, then a target word to label good or bad, and times the label. A target that matches the image's valence is labeled faster. It primes one object at a time, where the Implicit Association Test weighs two pairings.

An implicit bias is an association the person does not state, strong enough to fire before a considered judgment. [Implicit attitudes](../../attitude.md#implicit%20attitude) come from a social context that keeps presenting concepts, and that exposure primes particular judgments. Implicit bias falls with attention to the association and a slower judgment, with new experience of the group, or with a changed procedure such as blind review.
