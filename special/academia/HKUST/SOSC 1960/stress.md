# stress

Stress is the body's response to a demand it cannot easily meet. A stressor is the stimulus that produces it. Hans Selye coined the term for it after every hormone he injected into rats produced the same illness. The handling and the injection raised arousal, so the ulcers came from the procedure rather than from the hormone.

- what a stressor is ::@:: A stimulus that produces the aversive experience of high arousal.

## stress response

### general adaptation syndrome

Selye's general adaptation syndrome describes the stress response in three phases. Alarm mobilises physiological resources for fight or flight. Resistance copes with the stressor or habituates to it. Exhaustion comes last: the organism either recovers or depletes the resources it mobilised. A stressor that never stops keeps the body spending what it cannot replace, so exhaustion is the phase that costs health.

---

- the three phases of the general adaptation syndrome ::@:: Alarm, then resistance, then exhaustion or recovery.
- what the alarm phase does ::@:: Mobilises physiological resources for fight or flight.
- what the resistance phase does ::@:: Copes with the stressor or attempts to habituate to it.
- which phase of the general adaptation syndrome costs health ::@:: Exhaustion, where a stressor that never stops depletes what the body mobilised.

## autonomic nervous system

The autonomic nervous system runs the organs that adjust without deliberate control. Its two branches pull each organ in opposite directions.

The split follows what the body is doing. The sympathetic branch prepares the body for action: it spends energy, raising the heart rate, widening the bronchi and pushing blood to the muscles that will do the work. The parasympathetic branch restores equilibrium at rest: it saves, slowing the heart, narrowing the bronchi and running digestion. Each organ is thereby set one way for a threat and the other for a quiet room.

| organ | sympathetic branch | parasympathetic branch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| eyes | dilates pupil, for more light to see by | contracts pupil, for less light and finer detail |
| lungs | relaxes bronchi, for more air per breath | constricts bronchi, for resting air flow |
| heart | accelerates and strengthens heartbeat, sending blood to the muscles | slows heartbeat, saving energy |
| stomach and intestines | inhibits activity, sending blood elsewhere | stimulates activity, digesting food |
| blood vessels of the internal organs | contracts vessels, pushing blood to muscle | dilates vessels, letting the organs run normally |

## kinds of stressor

A major life stressor is a death in the family or a natural disaster. A situation that stays difficult, such as caring for a loved one with Alzheimer's disease, is a stressor in its own right. A daily hassle is traffic, a queue, or an argument. A hassle raises blood pressure, alters stress hormones, and suppresses immune function. A person with many hassles carries a stress load that no list of life events shows. Stress can also be a clinical illness, such as post-traumatic stress disorder.

- what a daily hassle does to the body ::@:: Raises blood pressure, alters stress hormones, and suppresses immune function.
- what counts as a chronically difficult stressor ::@:: Caring for a loved one with Alzheimer's disease.
- which clinical illness counts as a form of stress ::@:: Post-traumatic stress disorder.

## effects of stress

### stress and health

Stress raises the risk of infection. The common cold let researchers test that link, with virus put into volunteers' noses. Everyone in the study was infected, but those who began it less stressed or more positive were less likely to develop the cold. Reported stress predicts minor illness and serious outcomes.

Stress reaches disease along two routes. One runs through [health behaviour](health%20behaviour.md): stress changes what a person does, and the habits that survive it set part of the risk. The other is psychological activation, which raises the risk without passing through behaviour. Positive affect sits upstream of stress and lowers it. The upward arrows mark the two protective links, positive affect and healthy behaviour; the downward arrows mark stress, psychological activation, and disease.

### stress and memory

The effect of arousal and stress on declarative memory follows an inverted U-shaped curve. A small amount of stress improves memory performance, while extreme arousal and stress that lasts impair it. Long-term stress alters hormones, and those changes reduce the firing rate of hippocampal neurons and, once exposure lasts long enough, atrophy the hippocampus (McEwen & Sapolsky, 1995).
