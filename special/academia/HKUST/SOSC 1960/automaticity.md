# automaticity

A process is automatic when it carries one or more of four marks: it is unintentional, it cannot be stopped, it happens outside awareness, or it costs almost no cognitive effort (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999). One mark is enough, so driving is automatic even though the driver intends to drive.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the four marks of an automatic process (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999) ::@:: Unintentional, uncontrollable, outside conscious awareness, and cognitively efficient.
- how many marks an automatic process needs ::@:: One is enough, so driving counts as automatic even though it is intended.

## sources

Automaticity has two sources: practice and innate mechanisms. Repetition turns a difficult new skill into a smooth one that runs partly outside awareness. The fear response shows the innate route. People detect a negative word faster than a neutral one, even when the word was shown too briefly to be read. That points to a mechanism evolved to catch danger early.

- which two routes produce an automatic process ::@:: Practice, and innate mechanisms.
- what faster detection of a briefly shown negative word shows ::@:: An innate mechanism evolved to catch danger early.

## nonconscious social effects

### chameleon effect

The chameleon effect is the nonconscious copying of a partner's posture, mannerisms, and facial expressions. It runs without intention, and it pays: a partner who is mimicked likes the mimicker more, and the interaction goes better.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what the chameleon effect is ::@:: Nonconsciously copying the postures, mannerisms, and facial expressions of an interaction partner.
- what the chameleon effect buys ::@:: A partner who is mimicked likes the mimicker more, and the interaction goes better.

### priming

Priming is making a concept more accessible by exposing the person to a strongly associated one. Showing the word doctor makes the word nurse faster to recognize, because the two concepts sit near each other. Stereotypes prime the same way: a group stereotype makes the judgments associated with it easier to reach, and the person does not notice.
