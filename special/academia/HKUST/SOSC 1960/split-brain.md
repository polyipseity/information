# split-brain

A split-brain patient has had the corpus callosum severed, the band of white matter that joins the two hemispheres. Almost every pathway into the brain crosses the midline, so what one hemisphere knows the other does not. Everyday tasks reach both hemispheres, so the patient behaves normally. A task that reaches only one shows what the cut cost.

## rationale for the cut

Epilepsy means having recurrent and unprovoked seizures. A patient whose seizures do not respond to drugs has no other way to control them.

In a healthy brain the timing of activity in different regions tends not to line up, which is what desynchronised means. An epileptic seizure is the opposite: activity across regions falls into step and spreads. Wherever it starts, it reaches the rest of that hemisphere, then the other hemisphere.

Cutting the corpus callosum stops the seizure's discharge from spreading between hemispheres. William Van Wagenen, a neurosurgeon in Rochester, New York, performed the first human callosotomies on patients with epilepsy in the 1940s. Vogel and Bogen followed, and the operation reduced seizure activity while leaving patients unchanged in personality, intelligence, and behaviour. Roger Sperry's studies of these patients won him the Nobel Prize in 1981.

## after the cut

In the first days after surgery, acute disconnection syndrome brings muteness, difficulty controlling the left side of the body, and a splitting headache. In rare cases the left hand struggles against the right. The struggle suggests hemispheric communication is inhibitory: before the cut, each hemisphere held the other back.

The lasting difficulties are the ones that call for both hemispheres, such as putting a name to a face or solving a geometry problem. Knowledge learned in one hemisphere cannot be transferred to the other.

## visual half-field testing

### visual pathway

Light on the retina becomes a signal that runs along the optic nerve to the optic chiasm, where the optic nerves partially cross. Each retina has a nasal half, the half nearer the nose, and a temporal half, the half nearer the temple. The nasal fibres cross to the far side. The temporal fibres stay on their own side. What leaves the chiasm is called the optic tract. It reaches the lateral geniculate nucleus, a relay station of the thalamus, then runs through the optic radiations to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

![A diagram of the visual pathway from both eyes to the occipital lobe, labelling the ipsilateral and contralateral fibres, the optic nerve, optic chiasm, optic tract, lateral geniculate nucleus, and optic radiations](attachments/Visual%20Pathway%20to%20the%20Brain.jpg)

The partial crossing is what makes the method possible. Each retina sends fibres to both sides, so each eye feeds both hemispheres, but each half of the visual field feeds only one. An intact chiasm is all a researcher needs to send one image to one hemisphere without touching the skull.

### sending an image to one hemisphere

![A line drawing of a patient seated at a table facing a vertical screen on a stand, with small objects on the table beyond it and an examiner seated opposite](attachments/SplitBrainExperiments.jpg)

The patient keeps their gaze on a point at the centre of the screen. A stimulus is flashed briefly, entirely on one side of that point, for less time than an eye movement takes. That leaves no time to shift it into the other half of the visual field. Because the chiasm partially crosses, everything in the left visual field reaches only the right hemisphere. The callosum is cut, so the image cannot cross to the other hemisphere.

![A screen with a fixation cross and a stimulus flashed to the left of it, arrows running from the stimulus to the right-hand half of each eye, two eyes each split into a nasal and a temporal half, four fibres running to the hemispheres with the two nasal fibres crossing at the optic chiasm, and the two hemispheres joined by a corpus callosum marked as cut](attachments/split_brain_test_setup.svg)

The examiner compares what the patient says about the image with what the patient does with it, and the two answers come apart.

## hemisphere specialisation

Language areas are generally lateralised to the left hemisphere, so the right hemisphere cannot say what it has been shown. It can still act. The left hand, which the right hemisphere controls, points at or reaches for the object that matches what was shown, while the patient gives a different answer out loud.

The same dissociation appears without any object to name. A patient asked to reproduce a design does it with the right hand while the left hand works on a different version of the same design. The two hands interfere.

An Arcimboldo image is a face built out of fruits and vegetables. When one is flashed, the two hemispheres give two answers: the right reports a face, the left the fruits. Two words flashed at once, one to each field, come apart the same way. The patient says aloud the word that reached the left hemisphere. Asked about the other word, the patient reports not seeing it, though the left hand can still pick it out.

### left-hemisphere interpreter

A patient shown a claw in one visual field and a snow scene in the other points to a shovel and says chicken. One picture reaches each hemisphere: the claw goes to the left hemisphere, which speaks, and the snow scene to the right, which moves the left hand.

The claw goes with the chicken, and cleaning out a chicken shed takes a shovel. The left hemisphere saw the claw and nothing else, yet it gives an account of an act the snow scene started.

That account is not a recollection. It is a story built after the fact, offered without any sign that it is a guess. A reason invented to explain an act the hemisphere did not initiate is a confabulation.

The left hemisphere keeps making them because it holds the language. The right has no way to explain itself, and the left does not wait for one. It watches what the body did and invents a reason, so the left half is the interpreter.

When the right hemisphere directs the left hand, the patient still gives a fluent account of having chosen the act. The account shows no sign of guessing, so it cannot be checked against what it explains. That leaves one question open: whether an account of a chosen act is evidence that the act was chosen.
