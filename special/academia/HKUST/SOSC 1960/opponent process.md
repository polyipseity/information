# opponent process

The opponent process theory of color vision holds that color is coded as a comparison between paired opposites, not as a mixture of separate signals. Ewald Hering proposed it in 1892. Trichromacy, the theory that three kinds of color-sensitive cell explain color vision, was proposed by Young in 1802 and Helmholtz in 1867.

## channels

Color is compared along three channels: red against green, blue against yellow, and black against white. In each, the stronger member is seen and the weaker one is suppressed, so the two never appear at once. Mixing cannot produce a reddish green or a bluish yellow, though it can produce a greenish blue and a reddish yellow.

- which three opponent channels code color ::@:: Red against green, blue against yellow, and black against white.
- why a reddish green cannot be seen ::@:: Red and green are one opponent pair, and the stronger member suppresses the weaker.
- which colors cannot be produced by mixing ::@:: A reddish green and a bluish yellow.

### after-images

Staring at one member of a pair tires it, so the other dominates. After a blue patch, a blank wall shows a yellow after-image, the opposite of blue in its channel.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- why an after-image appears in the opposite color ::@:: The channel's other member dominates once the first tires.
- which after-image a blue patch produces ::@:: A yellow one, the opposite of blue in its channel.

### color blindness

Color blindness is a retina missing one or more kinds of cone. The channel fed by the missing kind loses a member, so comparisons along it fail. The loss is in the receptors, not in the comparing. The opponent machinery still works, on fewer inputs.

- where the loss in color blindness sits ::@:: In the receptors, not in the comparing, which still works on fewer inputs.
