# classical conditioning

Classical conditioning is learning that one stimulus predicts another. A dog hears a bell and then gets a piece of meat. After enough pairings the bell alone brings on saliva. The bell is a signal, not a cause: it announces the meat rather than producing the saliva. A stimulus that reliably announces an event calls up the response the event would have caused, so the dog can act on the signal before the event arrives.

Any stimulus an organism can perceive can elicit any reaction it can make. Almost any sound, sight, or smell can shift muscle tension, mood, and attitude.

A consequence works the other way: a reward or a punishment follows what the animal did and decides what it does next. [Classical and operant conditioning](operant%20conditioning.md#classical%20and%20operant%20conditioning) sets the two side by side.

Ivan Pavlov, a physiologist, arrived at conditioning from digestion, and won the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for that work. He diverted a dog's saliva into test tubes and measured how much the dog produced. Trial after trial the dogs began salivating before the food reached them: at the sight of the dish, then at the footsteps of Pavlov or his assistants, and at metronomes, lights, and bells. What mattered was reliable signalling that food was on the way, not the kind of stimulus.

## components and procedure

### four components

Meat makes a dog salivate, and a bell alone makes it do nothing, until the two are paired often enough.

The __unconditioned stimulus (US)__ elicits a particular response without prior training. The __unconditioned response (UR)__ occurs to a stimulus without prior training. The __conditioned stimulus (CS)__ elicits no particular response at first but comes to do so through conditioning. The __conditioned response (CR)__ is what the conditioned stimulus elicits after repeated pairings with the unconditioned stimulus. A __neutral stimulus (NS)__ is not yet conditioned, so it belongs to no response.

Organisms are born with the US-UR pair, and conditioning builds the CS-CR pair out of it. The separate names say which half was learned.

### pairing a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned one

A bell in a laboratory produces nothing. Meat produces salivation, as it has since before the dog was born. Pairing the bell with the meat over and over ends with the bell where the meat was: alone it brings on salivation, a conditioned stimulus doing the meat's work. The neutral stimulus has to come first and the unconditioned stimulus second.

Other forms of conditioning sit beside this one, with the plain pairing in the first row.

In a particular house, the shower water comes out very hot or very cold every time the toilet is flushed. The person steps back. Over time the step comes on the flush alone, before the water changes.

The flush is the conditioned stimulus, the temperature change it predicts is the unconditioned stimulus, and the step back is the conditioned response to a change that has not happened yet. Here the cue is a sound rather than a sight.

## conditioned emotional responses

A word that puts someone at ease does nothing on its own. A gunshot sound produces anxiety on its own. Pair the two and the anxiety attaches to the word, so a phrase that once calmed someone now raises anxiety. The inversion can take as few as five or six pairings. The emotion is conditioned the way salivation is.

## prediction error

A pairing is necessary, but not enough: presenting a neutral stimulus before an unconditioned stimulus does not always produce an association. Something has to register that the outcome was not already predicted. A cue that adds nothing to what the organism already knows gets nothing attached to it. This is the [blocking effect](blocking%20effect.md), where attention and surprise decide which cue the association attaches to.

---

- the relation between a pairing and the association it is meant to produce ::@:: Necessary, but not enough on its own.
- what has to register for an association to form ::@:: That the outcome was not already predicted.
- what happens to a cue that adds nothing to what the organism already knows ::@:: No association is attached to it.
- the effect that shows a pairing alone is not sufficient ::@:: The blocking effect.
- what sits at the centre of the account of what conditioning learns ::@:: Attention and surprise.

## conditioning outcomes

The same cue can end up carrying several responses, a liking or a disliking, not only the one it was paired with. Conditioning can also underlie clinical conditions. It cannot form an association from a pairing alone.

- what one cue can come to carry ::@:: Several different responses, a liking or a disliking, rather than only the one it was paired with.
- what conditioning cannot do on its own ::@:: Form an association from a pairing alone.

### preferences and aversions

A person eats a food and then falls ill. The taste or smell of that food now carries an avoidance, out of all proportion to the number of pairings. This is taste aversion conditioning. A conditioned response pulls a person towards something or pushes them away, and the sign of the unconditioned stimulus paired with the cue decides which way. The same process can set a preference instead of an aversion.

---

- taste aversion conditioning ::@:: An illness following a food attaches an avoidance to the taste or smell of that food.
- what decides whether conditioning produces a preference or an aversion ::@:: The sign of the unconditioned stimulus paired with the cue.
- what a taste aversion formed after one illness shows ::@:: Not every association needs many trials.

### fear conditioning in anxiety disorders

Fear conditioning underlies panic disorder and phobias: a cue paired with something frightening comes to carry the fear on its own. The process is detailed in [phobia](phobia.md).

---

### drug cues and compensatory responses

Conditioned compensatory responses oppose the effect of the drug. The body's response to withdrawal pain is itself conditioned, so cues associated with morphine make a person more sensitive to pain, not less. Tolerance works the same way. A dose taken where doses usually arrive meets a compensating response the place predicts. In a new place it meets less of one, so it lands nearer its full strength. That is how a dose a habit has made survivable becomes an overdose.

- why a tolerated dose can kill in an unfamiliar place ::@:: The compensating response is conditioned to the cues of the usual place, so a new place predicts less of it and the dose lands nearer its full strength.
