# correlation

A correlational design measures two variables and reports how far they go together. Nobody manipulates a variable, so neither variable can be shown to have come first.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a correlational design is ::@:: A correlational design measures two variables and reports how far they go together, without intervening in either.
- what a correlational design cannot report ::@:: Which of the two variables came first.

## what a correlational design measures

In one study, people reported how much they gave to others or to charity, then how happy they were. The more they gave, the happier they said they were.

A correlational design only observes variables. That suits questions about things nobody can ethically or practically hand out. A design pairs only two measures from the same people. A third variable measured at the same moment is then a rival explanation, not extra information.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- a question a correlational design can answer ::@:: Whether the income people give to others is related to how happy they report being.
- what a correlational design gives up by not intervening ::@:: Any claim about which variable causes which.
- how many measures one design pairs from the same people ::@:: Only two at a time.
- what a third variable measured at the same time is ::@:: A rival explanation, not extra information.

## the correlation coefficient

One number summarizes the association between the two variables: the correlation coefficient $r$, which runs from $-1$ to $1$.

Age and year of birth have a perfect negative correlation. Everyone born earlier is older, without exception.

A positive $r$ means the two variables go up and down together. A negative $r$ means one goes up as the other goes down. $r=0$ means no straight-line relationship.

A coefficient of zero does not rule out a relationship. The two variables can still be tied by a curve, which $r$ misses.

Judge strength by the absolute value of $r$, not by its sign. A large value means the variables almost always move together. A small one means they correspond some of the time, not most.

A scatterplot shows both variables at once.

| $r$ | Pattern | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| close to $-1$ | dots along a steep line from top left to bottom right | strong negative correlation |
| between $-1$ and $0$ | dots along a line from top left to bottom right, more spread out | moderate negative correlation |
| around $0$ | dots scattered with no slope | no correlation, or a weak one |
| between $0$ and $+1$ | dots along a line from bottom left to top right, more spread out | moderate positive correlation |
| close to $+1$ | dots tightly along a line from bottom left to top right | strong positive correlation |

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the range of the correlation coefficient $r$ ::@:: It runs from $-1$ to $1$.
- what the sign and the size of $r$ tell you ::@:: The sign gives the direction, and the size of the absolute value gives the strength.
- a pair of variables with a perfect negative correlation ::@:: Age and year of birth, because everyone born earlier is older, without exception.
- a positive correlation coefficient ::@:: The two variables go up and down together.
- a negative correlation coefficient ::@:: One variable goes up as the other goes down.
- a correlation coefficient of zero ::@:: The two variables show no straight-line relationship.
- what $r = 0$ does not rule out ::@:: A curved relationship between the two variables.
