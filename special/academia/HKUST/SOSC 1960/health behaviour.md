# health behaviour

A health behaviour is anything a person does that improves or harms health. Flossing, sunscreen, and sober driving protect health. Drunk driving, an all-nighter, and smoking harm it.

Five habits keep a person healthy:

- Moving more: regular physical activity.
- Eating well: a balanced, nutritious diet.
- Sleeping enough: seven to nine hours a night.
- Avoiding harm: no smoking, and limiting alcohol.
- Staying connected: keeping up [social support](resilience%20(psychology).md#social%20relationships).

A behaviour becomes a habit once it is firmly established and runs automatically, without a decision. Habits often form early in life, from parental encouragement or a peer group.

People who slept seven or eight hours, did not smoke or drink to excess, and exercised reported fewer illnesses, rated their own health higher, and were less likely to die over the nine to twelve years that followed (Belloc & Breslow, 1972; Breslow & Enstrom, 1980).

## health in times of stress

A popular joke about college holds that a student can keep only two of grades, social life, and sleep, and test periods make the choice sharpest. Protective practices matter most under stress, when the immune system is already weakened. A crowded lecture hall, cafeteria, or dormitory adds exposure to whatever illnesses other students carry. Students sitting exams were more stressed, smoked more, drank more caffeine, exercised less, and slept worse (Oaten & Chang, 2005). Poor sleep quality and quantity go with weaker learning capacity and academic performance (Curcio, Ferrara, & De Gennaro, 2006).

## changing a habit

Changing a habit runs through five steps.

1. Notice the trigger: the cue that sets the bad habit off.
2. Set one small goal that is specific and realistic.
3. Swap in a substitute, a healthier action in the old one's place.
4. Get support from friends, family, or a group.
5. Track progress and reward it.

Health promotion works on a population rather than one person. It targets the behaviours that carry a risk of future illness and teaches what is known about those risks. A risk factor is either inherited or acquired. Obesity is acquired, and it raises the risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease.
