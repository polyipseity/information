# research ethics

Psychology differs from the natural sciences such as chemistry in that its researchers conduct studies with human participants, who have to be guarded against potential psychological harm. Wanting to see how people handle ridicule does not make it advisable to ridicule research participants, and a study in which a "teacher" was asked to deliver a supposedly painful electric shock to a "learner", as in the Milgram experiment, raises the same question of whether a review board would approve it. Scientific psychologists therefore follow a code of ethics.

## ethical guidelines

In general, people should know when they are involved in research and what will happen to them during the study, and they should be free to choose whether to take part. Information researchers learn about individual participants should not be made public without that individual's consent. Researchers should not make observations of people in private places such as their bedrooms without their knowledge and consent, and should not seek confidential information from others, such as school authorities, without the consent of the participant or their guardian.

Researchers should also weigh the benefits of their proposed research against the potential risks to participants, and expose participants to risk only if they fully understand those risks and the likely benefits clearly outweigh them. Some researchers deceive participants to hide the true nature of the study, typically so that participants do not modify their behavior in unnatural ways. Those researchers are required to debrief participants after the study, which is an opportunity to educate them about its true nature.
