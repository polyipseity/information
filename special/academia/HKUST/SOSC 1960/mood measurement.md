# mood measurement

Three routes measure a person's mood. One asks the participant, one reads behaviour, and one reads the body.

They differ in how much the participant hands over, and they do not measure the same thing. A finding from one route does not carry over to another unless the study says so. A mood measure only means something against a comparison.

## self-report

Asking is the most direct of the three routes, and the most open to bias. A self-report is either one item or a validated set.

A single item asks once, against a fixed set of response options rather than free text:

- a rating from 0 to 9, or from 1 to 7
- an agreement item
- a visual analogue scale running from the worst mood to the best
- a choice among words such as happy, enthusiastic, or melancholic

A number given on demand shows what the participant recalls and is willing to say. Every form shares that weakness.

### validated questionnaires

A study swaps the single item for a validated questionnaire when it needs scores it can compare across people. A question written for the occasion cannot be set beside another study's results; a published score can.

The usual one is the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule, PANAS. A participant rates current positive affect, such as feeling enthusiastic, alert, or inspired, apart from current negative affect, such as feeling distressed, upset, or nervous. The two are scored apart, so a participant high on one and low on the other does not average out to neutral. A second validated instrument is POMS.

The scoring comes published, so everyone who administers a validated instrument scores it the same way.

### experience sampling

With experience sampling, a participant rates their mood on a phone several times a day. Each rating covers the last stretch of experience rather than a day recalled.

The memory bias that troubles a retrospective question is largely gone, because there is too little interval for it to grow. The method gains validity for the state it targets and gives up reliability across participants.

## behavioural measures

A behavioural measure reads what a participant produces while doing something else. A coder reads the face: a smile counts whether or not the participant decides to smile. Facial EMG reads the muscle activity underneath, instead of the movement that may hide it. Neither is easy to produce on purpose.

A reaction time resists faking better than a rating. Someone who wants to feel better can just say so, but speeding up a lexical decision takes more than the intention to look better. A participant who classifies happy words faster against sad ones after exercise counts as being in a better mood.

Every measure here carries its own noise. A coded smile settles nothing outside the situation its coding was written for. A reaction time shifts with sleep, attention, and the choice of words. A difference between conditions has to be read against that noise.

## physiological measures

A physiological measure reads the body without asking. The usual candidates are heart rate variability, skin conductance, cortisol, heart rate from a wearable, and dopamine measured before and after the manipulation. A wearable collects heart rate across a whole session, not one moment in a clinic chair. That suits a transient effect.

They all share one limit: they measure arousal, not mood. A sympathetic nervous system under strain does what a happy person does, for a different reason. A physiological measure shows that something happened, not what it felt like.

A [brain-imaging measure](functional%20magnetic%20resonance%20imaging.md#correlational,%20not%20causal) is correlational at best: a region active during a mood responds to it rather than being the mood.
