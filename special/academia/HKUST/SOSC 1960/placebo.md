# placebo

A placebo looks real to the patient but has no pharmacological effect. Sugar pills, water injections, and sham operations all qualify. The placebo effect is the relief that comes anyway. The word is Latin for "I shall please".

Doctors used such treatments from the 1700s, when nothing better was available or a patient was thought ill. The effect is measurable and partly explicable, and both of those facts complicate the testing of a real treatment.

## the Trivaricaine study

In 1996, 56 volunteers tested a painkiller called Trivaricaine, which was a placebo: it contained nothing that eases pain. Each person had it spread on one index finger and left the other untouched. Both fingers went into painful clamps. The treated finger hurt less, which should not have happened. The treatment looked and felt real, so the relief it produced was the placebo effect.

## what believing a treatment is real changes

One account denies the effect is real. It reads the relief as one of several other factors, such as [participant demand](blinded%20experiment.md): participants falsely reporting improvement to please the experimenter. The competing account believes expecting to recover sets off physiological changes that improve symptoms.

A placebo has been shown to move blood pressure and heart rate, and to raise the release of endorphins, which ease pain. A placebo may also lower adrenaline, a stress hormone that slows the harm an ailment does. Which is right is still open.

## the placebo as a control

A placebo has to be convincing to work as a treatment. The same property makes it a good control. One group gets the real pill, the other an identical-looking placebo. A benefit over the placebo counts as evidence the drug works, and only because the placebo exerts an influence of its own. By the 1950s this was standard in clinical trials.

Ethical worries have made the practice rarer. A seriously ill patient is better off against an existing drug than against nothing.

## what a placebo costs

The costs fall on the patient and on the research.

Someone who believes a fake treatment cured them may miss a drug that does work. The relief itself often fades.

Responses in the placebo arm cloud the clinical results. That is one reason the search for the mechanism continues.

None of that argues against the placebo arm. It argues for knowing what the control is doing before reading anything into the difference between the arms.
