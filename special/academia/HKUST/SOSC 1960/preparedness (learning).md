# preparedness (learning)

Some associations form too easily for repetition and surprise to explain. Preparedness is the name for that gap.

Prediction error explains what a learner attaches to when the outcome arrives unexpectedly. A prepared association forms with little surprise, so there is little prediction error for that explanation to use. Preparedness therefore claims a mechanism, not just a fast result.

The two cover opposite ends of one range. Conditioning is strong where biology has already tied one cue to one outcome, and a cue that carries no information in ordinary conditioning can still pick up learning that an equally frequent unprepared cue would not.

## where the effect is clearest

Oysters or raw fish, then food poisoning, and the food is finished. Taste aversion is the sharpest case of preparedness, and a fall from a high branch does the same for fear.

In the food-poisoning case the bacteria or toxin is the unconditioned stimulus and the nausea and vomiting the unconditioned response. The taste or smell is the conditioned stimulus and the avoidance next time the conditioned response.

In the fall case the impact and the sudden loss of support are the unconditioned stimulus and the reflexive panic, shock, and pain the unconditioned response. The visual cues of height and depth are the conditioned stimulus and the fear, racing heart, and urge to retreat the conditioned response.

Three things separate a prepared association from ordinary conditioning, and the taste-aversion case shows all three.

- One trial is enough, and the aversion lasts for years.
- The association attaches to the food alone, not the plate, the colour of the chair, or the music at the time. A bowl of shrimp in a seafood restaurant brings on disgust and nausea from the smell, taste, or thought of shrimp, while the rest of the room is untouched.
- Timing is ignored. Food eaten at noon and illness arriving at midnight are still linked.

The fall case shows the same shape, and a [phobia](phobia.md) built on an ancestral threat is then the same phenomenon as an aversion built on a bad meal.

## the evolutionary argument

An organism that feared faster than its neighbours survived and reproduced more often. The predisposition that sped the learning was inherited, not acquired.

The account predicts a fear by how dangerous that class of thing was for the ancestors who carried the predisposition, not by how dangerous the object has been lately. Heights, darkness, and snakes form easily; cars and electrical outlets rarely. A car is more dangerous today, but the ancestors who feared one did not have cars. The fear of a snake needs no learning at all.

Taste aversion is selective for the same reason. Avoiding bad food raises the odds, and avoiding one colour or one piece of music does not. The wiring follows whatever there was a gain in avoiding.

The argument deals with the past, not the present, which is what makes it checkable. Its prediction is a claim about where fears should be common, and that can be tested against the fears people actually have.

## equipotentiality and the alternatives

Equipotentiality is the behaviourist assumption that the same principles of learning and conditioning apply to every stimulus and every response. A tuning fork and a mushroom are both learned the same way. Preparedness denies it: some associations form more easily than the assumption allows, for reasons inside the organism.

Preparedness was proposed as a way to knock that assumption down, so the two are rivals. If preparedness holds, equipotentiality is wrong.

The rival to preparedness agrees with equipotentiality and denies inheritance instead. Snakes and heights are common fears while cars and electrical outlets are rare ones, and this account says nothing needs to be built into the organism to explain that. People fear what they expect to be dangerous, and nothing adaptive or ancient has to stand behind that expectation.

Preparedness says the pattern is what avoiding those things once paid off. The rival says it is what people expect right now.

Studies have supported some of the predictions and not others. Neither account is settled.
