# attitude

An attitude is a psychological tendency expressed by evaluating an entity with some degree of favor or disfavor. A negative attitude toward mushrooms makes a person avoid them and think badly of them. A good mushroom dish can later turn that attitude positive. A person can report the evaluation or hold it without awareness.

## explicit attitude

An explicit attitude is consciously held and can be reported. Self-report measures ask the person directly. A semantic differential scale places the attitude object between opposite adjectives. A Likert scale asks how much a person agrees with an evaluative statement. Reported attitudes predict how people vote. On a controversial issue, people may hide what they hold.

## implicit attitude

An implicit attitude is one a person does not state. Many implicit measures infer it from reaction time instead of asking for a report.

The Implicit Association Test measures the strength of an association between a concept and an attribute by timing how fast a person sorts stimuli into combined categories. Two concepts are sorted beside the attributes good and bad. One block pairs the first concept with good and the second with bad. The blocks then swap, so the first concept meets bad and the second meets good. A block whose pairing follows a strong association is the compatible one, and the swapped block is incompatible. The compatible block sorts faster, so the difference in average response time between the compatible and incompatible blocks is the measure.

The evaluative priming task shows a prime very briefly, often too briefly to report, then a target the person classifies as good or bad. The measure is the response time to the target. A congruent prime speeds the classification and an incongruent one slows it, so the size of the speed-up indexes the automatic evaluation of the prime. The prime is often masked, because a person who can see and name it evaluates it deliberately instead.

Implicit and explicit attitudes can disagree, because a person can hold a bias without knowing or without admitting it.

The associations behind an implicit attitude come from a social context that keeps presenting the same concepts. That repetition primes particular judgments. The associations shape behavior most when the person lacks the cognitive resources to override them. Implicit race-violence associations predict shooting an unarmed Black target more often in a video game.

A measured bias can be countered. The person can notice the bias and slow the judgment. New experience with the group can weaken the association. The procedure can change, with a blind review or criteria written before the candidates arrive.
