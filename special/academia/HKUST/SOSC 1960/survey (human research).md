# survey (human research)

A survey is a list of questions that pulls specific data from a group. Researchers hand out paper questionnaires or post them online. A survey reaches what people believe, feel, and prefer, and a laboratory task does not.

## what a survey buys

A survey reaches more people than a laboratory study. It costs less, takes less of the participants' time, and needs no appointment.

What it does not buy is precision. A longitudinal study would measure better, but it costs far more and runs for years, so the two designs trade reach against precision.

In exploratory work the cheap, fast design comes first. The expensive one follows only if the results look promising.

## surveys in correlational and experimental research

A survey is data collection, not a design, so it fits either kind of research.

Most surveys are correlational, because the questions measure rather than manipulate. One such survey asks how happy people are and how much they gave away. The two measures sit side by side, with no sign of which came first.

Correlational work needs no survey. One study compared smile intensity in college yearbook photographs with marriage ten years later, asking nobody.

A survey can carry an experiment. Participants read a form filled in by either a happy or an unhappy writer, then rated how likely that writer was to get into heaven. The writer's mood was the independent variable and the rating the dependent one.
