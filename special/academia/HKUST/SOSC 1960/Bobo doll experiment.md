# Bobo doll experiment

Albert Bandura ran the experiment in 1961 to measure what a child learns by watching another person. He tested aggression. Most people believed watching violence works off the urge to commit it and leaves the viewer calmer. The children did the opposite, hitting a doll after watching an adult hit it. The study is still read for that reason.

A child copies an act the child has only seen, which is [observational learning](observational%20learning.md). The criticism the study drew made it the standard case for research ethics.

## the design

Children aged three to five sat in a room with an adult and a pile of toys, including a large inflatable Bobo doll. A minute in, the adult attacked the doll for roughly ten minutes: mallet blows, kicks, nonstop shouting. The child was left alone with the same toys.

Every child who watched copied much of the attack, striking the doll and improvising with the mallet. Children who watched an adult of their own sex imitated that adult most closely.

No child was rewarded for imitating. Reinforcement explains none of it.

Children who watched an adult play gently played calmly and showed no aggression.

## vicarious reinforcement

In the follow-up, children who saw the model punished for its aggression were less aggressive afterwards. The consequence landed on the model, not on the learner, and the learner changed anyway. Seeing who took the punishment and what it cost was enough. A consequence the learner never receives still changes the learner's behaviour, which is __vicarious reinforcement__.

Two years later Bandura repeated the earlier design with the model on film. Children saw one of two versions: a straight recording of the adult beating the doll, or a fantasy version with the attacker dressed as a cat. The imitation came out the same in almost every case. A model on a screen is a model.

The finding arrived as television moved into the home. Researchers still argue over what it means for media and behaviour. Nobody disputes that it opened the area.

## why it was criticized

Critics said the study trained children toward aggression: it showed children a behaviour at length, then measured how much they picked up. The ethical objection has two parts: what happens to participants who cannot consent for themselves, and who reviews a study before it runs. Both appear under [ethical guidelines](research%20ethics.md).

The criticism changed how such studies run. It did not remove the study from the field. A study showing an adult model harm a child now faces review before it begins, not after the data come in.

## what it changed

Copying what a child has watched needs no stimulus and no habit. The child remembers, decides it is acceptable, and acts on a representation. Learning of that shape happens inside a person, not between a stimulus and a reflex. That was the first shift, from pure behaviorism to cognitive psychology.

The children's aggression came from what they watched, not from what they were given. That was the practical shift. It made what a child watches a live question. The [history of psychology](psychology.md#history%20of%20psychology) covers both movements under behaviorism and the cognitive revolution.
