# heuristic

A heuristic is a mental shortcut that replaces a complex problem with a simple rule. A reader choosing a book may treat a recommendation from a credible source as a safe bet and never compare the books.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a heuristic is ::@:: A mental shortcut that replaces a complex problem with a simple rule.
- what a book recommendation stands in for ::@:: The comparison a reader would otherwise make between the books.

## representativeness heuristic

The representativeness heuristic judges whether something belongs to a category by how closely it resembles the perceiver's mental picture of that category (Kahneman & Tversky, 1972, 1973). A tall, muscular woman in sports apparel is judged an athlete on that fit alone. The shortcut holds while resemblance and frequency agree, and misleads when base rates say otherwise. A quiet, thin man who likes poetry fits the picture of a classics professor, yet professors are far rarer than truck drivers.

- what the athlete case shows about the representativeness heuristic ::@:: A tall, muscular woman in sports apparel is judged an athlete on that fit alone.
- when the representativeness heuristic misleads ::@:: When base rates contradict resemblance: the poetry-reading man fits a professor's picture, yet truck drivers are far more common.

## availability heuristic

The availability heuristic judges how frequent or likely an event is by how easily instances of it come to mind (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973). Frequent events come to mind first, so the estimate is often close. The shortcut fails where recall and frequency part ways. Asked whether English holds more words starting with k or more with k third, people answer that more start with k. Those k-words come to mind more easily, yet three times as many have k third (Schwarz et al., 1991). People overestimate how often planes crash because a crash is rare and widely reported.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- how the availability heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973) judges frequency ::@:: By how easily instances of the event come to mind.
- why the availability heuristic often estimates well ::@:: Frequent events come to mind first, so the estimate is close.
- why the availability heuristic makes plane crashes feel likelier ::@:: A crash is rare and widely reported, so it comes to mind easily and feels more likely.
- what the k-letter question on English words shows (Schwarz et al., 1991) ::@:: Words starting with k come to mind more easily, yet three times as many have k third.
