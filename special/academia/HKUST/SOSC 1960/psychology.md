# psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of the mind and behavior. It asks how the mind works and what causes behavior, and it takes up questions such as how people perceive and recognize faces, how insomnia affects cognition, how children learn to read, how people interact with AI, and where individual differences in cognitive ability come from.

## history of psychology

Psychology's two founding branches appeared in the late nineteenth century: measurement of mental activity in a laboratory, and treatment of mental health conditions through the unconscious mind.

### the first psychology laboratory

Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920) headed the first modern psychology laboratory in 1879, at Leipzig in Germany. He held that mental activity can be broken down into more basic operations, and used introspection, the self-observation of mental activity, as an objective method for assessing it.

## psychology as a science

Many people remain skeptical that psychology is really a science, partly because psychological phenomena such as depression, intelligence, and prejudice are not directly observable the way ocean tides or the speed of light are. Because thoughts and feelings are invisible, early researchers chose to study behavior instead, and behavior remains the route by which personality and other mental attributes are inferred: someone friendly and outgoing is read as one kind of person, someone shy and withdrawn as another. Behavioral measures and rating scales turn thoughts and feelings into something measurable, as educators do with academic performance and economists with quality of life.

In a chimeric face judgment task, two faces are built from the two halves of a target face, and the participant judges which of them looks more similar to the target. The judgments come out with a left-side bias, read as evidence for right-hemisphere involvement in face processing.

Francis Galton, a cousin of Charles Darwin working in England in the late 1800s, pioneered several of those measures. He used patches of color to test how well people could distinguish colors, invented the self-report questionnaire, and used such reports to examine how accurately people judged distances. In 1875 he began studying twins to work out how much of their similarities and differences came from life experience, and he coined the phrase "nature versus nurture". Lacking a modern understanding of genetics, he still proposed comparing identical and fraternal twins to estimate how far genetic and social factors contribute to personality, the puzzle now called the nature–nurture question.

Modern psychology uses better measures, more sophisticated study designs, and better statistical analysis. Happiness shows why one measure will not do: people might lie about their happiness, might be unable to report it accurately, or might not use a numerical scale in the same way. Psychologists therefore add peer report measures, asking close friends and family about the target person and checking the two reports for discrepancies. They also use memory measures, on the idea that dispositionally positive people recall pleasant events more easily while negative people recall unpleasant ones, and biological measures such as saliva cortisol samples for stress and fMRI images of activation in the left prefrontal cortex, an area associated with good mood.

Psychology is nevertheless a young science. Physics and chemistry are hundreds of years old, whereas psychology is barely 150 years old, and most of its major findings have come in the last 60 years. It has legitimate limits, but it is a science, and taking a scientific approach to it means research methods, measuring mind and behavior, subject recruitment and data collection, statistical analysis, and critical thinking and argumentation.

## subfields of psychology

Traditionally the subfields of psychology correspond to different cognitive capacities and share a continuity of methodology, and they are commonly set out in five groups. _Biological_ psychology covers biopsychology and neuroscience, sensation, and consciousness. _Cognitive_ psychology covers perception, thinking, intelligence, and memory. _Developmental_ psychology covers learning and lifespan development. _Social and personality_ psychology covers social behavior, personality, emotion, and motivation. _Mental and physical health_ covers abnormal psychology, therapies, and stress, lifestyle, and health.

## levels of organization

A newer approach studies different cognitive capacities at different levels of organization, from group and individual behavior down to brain systems and neural networks, with different technologies and tools at each level. The nervous system is divided into molecules, synapses, neurons, circuits, maps, systems, and the brain as a whole, and those levels group into four families of networks: gene and protein networks at the level of molecules, synaptic networks at the level of synapses, neuronal networks at the levels of neurons, circuits, and maps, and whole-brain networks at the levels of systems and the brain.

The levels span roughly ten orders of magnitude in space, from 1 Å to 1 m, and as many in time, from a millisecond to a year, so each level has its own instruments. Whole-brain functional networks are imaged with EEG, MEG, fMRI, fNIRS, and ECoG, which resolve millimetre-to-centimetre structure over milliseconds to minutes. Circuit dynamics and neural information flow are measured over about 100 µm to 1 cm and milliseconds to minutes, and optical imaging at about 100 µm to a few millimetres over milliseconds to minutes. Metabolic pathways, transcriptional and gene regulatory networks, and interactome networks sit at about 1 Å to 1 µm, spanning milliseconds to days. Diffusion MR imaging and histological tract tracing reach from 1 cm to 1 m over hours to years, and EM imaging and reconstruction covers about 100 µm to 1 mm over minutes to months; all three map connectome networks. Social networks sit above a metre over minutes to years, with perception–action coupling and brain–behavior interactions at about 10 cm to 1 m over seconds to days.

No single level has a method that reaches the others, so the approach requires interdisciplinary effort.

## measurement and modeling tools

Psychological science measures behavior, looks at the living brain, looks at the deficits that follow brain damage, and builds computational models. No single measurement settles a question, so the aim is converging evidence from multiple methodologies. An eye tracker records where the eyes look, giving the sequence of fixations and the regions a person attends to. Electroencephalography (EEG) records brain waves, the electrical activity of the brain at the scalp. Computational modeling writes a process down as an explicit model rather than a description: a model of face recognition, for instance, samples fixations at several scales and encodes each with a shared multi-scale convolutional network, chooses the next region of interest with a hidden Markov model over the mouth, the nose side, and the eye corner, and confines attention to that region with a Gaussian mask. A shared classifier scores each fixated region, and the losses of the three fixations are combined by their maximum into a multi-task loss.

## applications of psychological science

Applied science is the application of scientific knowledge to practical problems or situations. Psychological science produces interventions that help people live better lives. A growing body of research determines which therapies work and which do not: cognitive behavioral therapy has been shown to be effective for depression and anxiety disorders, while some other types of therapy turn out to be harmful on average. In organizational psychology, several interventions have been found to raise productivity and satisfaction in the workplace. Human factors engineers have increased the safety and utility of products, as when the psychologist Alphonse Chapanis and other researchers redesigned the cockpit controls of aircraft so that they were less confusing and easier to respond to, which cut pilot errors and crashes. Forensic science has made courtroom decisions more valid: Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated the limits and unreliability of eyewitness testimony and memory, alongside the DNA exonerations that freed wrongly imprisoned people. The field has had enough success to show that it works, though a great deal remains to be learned.

### educational psychology

Educational psychology applies psychological findings to learning and teaching. Its cognitive side works on how information is encoded, stored, and retrieved, and it supports spaced retrieval practice over rereading: testing yourself after ten minutes, a day, and a week restamps each memory and flattens the forgetting curve, while a metacognitive checkpoint lets a learner notice what is not yet understood. Expectancy–value theory holds that people engage when they expect to succeed and find the task worthwhile. The behavioral side works on what is observable, so praise, badges, or gentle corrections reinforce or extinguish a behavior; unpredictable praise keeps effort high, while overrewarding can backfire. Neither side suffices alone, and readiness constrains the order of teaching: the prefrontal cortex does not support heavy abstraction until the mid-teens, so third graders build concrete models before tackling algebra. Watching a classmate work a problem adds a social route: peer modelling builds confidence and shows a concrete way to the answer.

### the other-race effect

The other-race effect is the more accurate recognition of faces of one's own race than of other races. Perceptual expertise explains it as a skill built by experience: frequent exposure to own-race faces sharpens the reading of their emotional expressions, and the effect is better documented than explained, since no single model accounts for every part of it. It bears on eyewitness testimony, on interventions that improve relationships between groups, and on face recognition technology.

## studying psychology

People study psychology for several reasons: to understand themselves; to understand other people and groups; to influence others more effectively, as in socializing children or motivating employees; to help others and improve the world, as through effective psychotherapy; to gain a skill that leads to a profession such as social work or teaching; to evaluate research claims they hear or read about; and because the subject is interesting, challenging, and fun in its own right. The field also meets a practical need: much of what society has to solve turns on the mind and behavior.

Graduates go into education institutions, community, social, and personal services, commerce and industry, and the civil service, both in the private and the public sector. Their training carries both technical and soft skills, so the roles they take include training consultant, human resources officer, sales manager, marketing manager, research assistant, rehabilitation coordinator, public relations executive, behavioral therapist, education and teaching roles, advertising account executive, graduate trainee, administrative executive, activity officer, private banking analyst, special educational needs instructor, business development analyst, community organizer, civil servant, and talent management officer.

The share of psychology PhDs awarded to men fell from nearly 70 percent in 1975 to less than 30 percent in 2008 (National Science Foundation), and women are 76 percent of new psychology doctorates, 74 percent of early-career psychologists, and 53 percent of the psychology workforce (American Psychological Association).

### aging and dementia

An aging population raises the question of how aging affects the mind and whether healthy aging can be promoted. Dementia takes away memory, abilities, and personality, and its early signs are easy to miss: memory loss that disrupts daily life, such as repeatedly forgetting important events or asking a question again; difficulty making decisions, or withdrawal from activities once enjoyed; trouble managing money; neglected appearance; and obvious mood changes, which the person may try to cover up. An evaluation is worth encouraging, because early detection can make someone eligible for treatments that relieve symptoms and preserve independence for longer.

### mental health

Mental health is a growing concern: more than 45 percent of teenagers have a mental disorder, one in five has a severe one, and suicide is the second leading cause of death among people aged 10 to 24. Mental illness is a disorder of brain function arising from complex interactions between a person's genes and their environment, and it occurs at similar rates in every culture and socio-economic group. A depressed mood is sometimes normal, as after the loss of a loved one, but it calls for professional care when it persists and gets in the way of daily functioning; early identification and effective treatment prevent future disability, and there is an urgent need in psychology and mental health professions.
