# memory

Memory is the ability to keep information over time and bring it back when needed. George Miller (1956) treated it as information processing with a limited capacity and proposed the magic number 7. Phone numbers typically carry seven or eight digits. Remembering runs through three stages: encoding, storage, and retrieval. Each stage affects the others, and any can fail.

- the three stages of remembering ::@:: Encoding, storage, and retrieval.

## types of memory

Memory keeps four kinds of content: information in use, the episodes of a life, permanent knowledge, and [what a group shares](collective%20memory.md).

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the four kinds of memory ::@:: Working memory, episodic memory, semantic memory, and collective memory.

### working memory

Working memory is the ability to hold information in mind for a brief time and work with it. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex holds that material for short-term manipulation. Doing math in your head and telling a story both draw on it. Two limits bound it: how much a person can hold, and how much a person can focus and resist interference.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what working memory is ::@:: The ability to hold information in mind for a brief time and work with it.
- tasks that draw on working memory ::@:: Doing math in your head and telling a story.
- the brain region that holds material for short-term manipulation ::@:: The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

### episodic memory

Episodic memory is the ability to remember the episodes of one's life. Autobiographical memory covers the specific events of one's entire life. Strong emotion marks an event as worth encoding, so it stays vivid long after an ordinary one fades. The confidence it brings outruns its accuracy: the exact time, place, and words still fade.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what episodic memory is ::@:: The ability to remember the episodes of one's life.
- what autobiographical memory covers ::@:: The specific events of one's entire life.
- what strong emotion does for an episodic memory ::@:: It deepens the encoding and raises confidence in recall, without making the details more accurate.

### semantic memory

Semantic memory is one's storehouse of more or less permanent knowledge. Unlike an episodic memory, it carries no context: the knowledge arrives without the time and place it was learned.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what semantic memory is ::@:: A person's storehouse of more or less permanent knowledge.
- what semantic memory and episodic memory each hold ::@:: Semantic memory holds permanent knowledge; episodic memory remembers the episodes of one's life.
- whether a semantic memory carries the context it was learned in ::@:: No. The knowledge arrives without the time and place it was learned.

## encoding

Encoding is the initial experience of perceiving and learning events. It usually happens automatically.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what encoding is ::@:: The initial experience of perceiving and learning events.
- how encoding usually happens ::@:: Automatically.

### distinctiveness

Distinctiveness sets an event apart. An event that stands out is remembered better than one that repeats. Hundreds of identical drives to school are forgotten. The one that held a car chase is not. A rare and emotional event shows that advantage at its extreme, as in [flashbulb memory](flashbulb%20memory.md).

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what distinctiveness does for memory ::@:: It sets an event apart, so an event that stands out is remembered better than one that repeats.
- why one morning drive to school is remembered and the others are not ::@:: That morning held a car chase, and what stands out is remembered better than what repeats.

### recoding

Encoding involves recoding: taking information in one form and converting it into a form that makes sense to us. Recoding can happen without awareness, as when new material associates with old memories. Recoding uses three strategies: acronyms and associations, elaboration, and creating imagery. The acronym strategy turns the Big five personality traits into OCEAN: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extroversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what recoding is ::@:: Taking information from one form and converting it into a form that makes sense to us.
- how recoding can happen without awareness ::@:: Through associations with old memories.
- the recoding strategies for encoding ::@:: Acronyms and associations, elaboration, and creating imagery.
- how the Big five personality traits are recoded ::@:: As OCEAN: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extroversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism.

## retention

### storage

An experience leaves a memory trace, or engram: the change in the nervous system that represents it. Consolidation is the neural change over time that creates the trace of an experience. The hippocampus binds threads from across the neocortex into an episodic trace, and repeated retrieval rewrites that trace. The retention interval runs from learning to testing. Consolidation works across that interval, while [retroactive and proactive interferences](interference%20theory.md) and the [misinformation effect](misinformation%20effect.md) act within it.

Retrieval reconstructs a past event from memory traces and current belief about what happened, and that reconstruction alters what storage holds.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a memory trace, or engram, is ::@:: The change in the nervous system that represents an experience.
- what consolidation is ::@:: The neural changes that occur over time to create the memory trace of an experience.
- the role of the hippocampus in an episodic memory trace ::@:: It binds threads from across the neocortex into an episodic trace, and repeated retrieval rewrites that trace.
- what the retention interval is ::@:: It runs from learning to testing.
- how a retrieved past event is put back together ::@:: It is reconstructed from memory traces and current belief about what happened.

### retrieval

Retrieval is accessing stored information when it is needed. Most of our memories are never used without a retrieval cue. Available memory is everything a person has stored. Accessible memory is the part a person can retrieve.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what makes stored information usable at a later moment ::@:: A retrieval cue.
- the difference between available and accessible memory ::@:: Available memory is all information stored; accessible memory is what a person is able to retrieve.
- whether available memory is always accessible ::@:: No. Most memories are never used without a retrieval cue.

## forgetting

A memory that kept every detail would hold each case and extract no rule: the overfitting a perfect store produces. Computational models find that dropping up to 80 percent of the redundant knowledge improves search, pattern recognition, and communication speed. Forgetting lets the mind abstract a principle and carry it to a new situation.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- the defect a memory that kept every detail produces ::@:: Overfitting: it holds each case and extracts no rule.
- the share of redundant knowledge that computational models drop ::@:: Up to 80 percent.
- what improves once computational models drop most of the redundant knowledge ::@:: Search, pattern recognition, and communication speed.
- what forgetting lets the mind do ::@:: Abstract a principle and carry it to a new situation.
