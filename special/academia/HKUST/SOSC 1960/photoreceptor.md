# photoreceptor

A photoreceptor is a cell in the retina that turns light into a neural signal. That conversion is a photochemical reaction called photoactivation, complete within a thousandth of a second. The human retina holds two kinds, rods and cones, whose different sensitivities let the eye see from a moonless night to bright sun. Rods far outnumber cones, about 120 million against 6 million, twenty for every cone.

## rod

A single photon can make a rod fire, so rods work at night and in dim rooms. They outnumber cones and fill the periphery of the retina. The center of the retina holds almost none, so a dim star disappears when looked at directly.

## cone

Cones carry fine detail. They need more photons than rods, but their pigment rebuilds faster, so they go on working in bright light. They are packed tight around the fovea, the small central region of the retina, and thin out toward the edges. Three kinds, sensitive to short, medium, and long wavelengths, combine to give color.

### foveal and peripheral coding

A signal leaving the retina carries the pooled response of a patch of photoreceptors, its summation area. At the fovea the patch is small, so tightly packed cones report separately and fine detail reaches the brain. In the periphery one patch spans many rods, so the faint light they catch adds up while the difference between one rod and its neighbour disappears into the sum. A small area buys resolution, a large one buys sensitivity, and no point of the image can have both. The two kinds are distributed to match: cones pack the fovea, rods fill the periphery.
