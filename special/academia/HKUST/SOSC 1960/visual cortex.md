# visual cortex

The visual cortex is the part of the cerebral cortex that processes vision. Its first area is the primary visual cortex, or V1, at the back of the head in the occipital lobe. Signals from the eye reach V1 through a relay in the thalamus, the lateral geniculate nucleus.

The signal then splits. Reflexive eye movements, which swing the eyes toward something of interest and follow it as it moves, are computed below the cortex, in the older part of the brain. The complex work that builds what you see happens in the cortex, the newer part.

## reconstruction

V1 works from contrast. Neurons there compute color, the orientation and thickness of a line, and small-scale motion. Later areas group those pieces: an edge is assigned to the object it belongs to, figure separates from ground, a surface takes on color, and whole-object motion is computed. The signal from the eye carries no picture. The picture is built stage by stage.

An illusory contour such as the Kanizsa triangle has no drawn edge, yet a triangle appears, brighter than the ground around it. The visual system takes the most likely reading of the missing edge.

### specialised areas

Global motion has an area of its own, the middle temporal area (MT), which takes the movement of a whole object rather than motion on a small scale. Places and scenes fall to the parahippocampal place area. The fusiform face area identifies objects that take fine discrimination to tell apart, such as faces. A further region handles letters and words. The areas for places, faces, letters and words lie along the [ventral stream](two-streams%20hypothesis.md#ventral%20stream), the what pathway.

## binocular vision

One eye could supply everything described so far, so the second needs its own explanation. Prey animals carry their eyes on opposite sides of the skull, so a predator anywhere around them is caught. Humans, like most predators, point both eyes the same way, encoding almost the same scene twice.

The repetition is worth something: a faint signal ahead gets a second chance to register. The small difference in perspective between the two eyes is worth more. Each eye alone gives a flat picture drawn on its retina, and combining two of those pictures gives three-dimensional space, with an object's distance, size, and volume.

Experience supplies part of that reading. The visual system learns how an object's distance, size, and volume change as it comes closer or moves away.
