# converging evidence

Nothing inside a skull is directly observable. No instrument measures a brain process as it happens; each reads something beside it. A scanner reads oxygen in the blood, an electrode reads a voltage that began elsewhere and reached the scalp, and a patient with a damaged region reports a deficit. That deficit may have started before the damage. None of them is wrong, but none is enough alone. A claim about how the brain works is only as strong as the independent methods that agree on it. Three instruments agreeing beat one instrument agreeing twice.

You choose a method because it bears on the question. You do not choose it because it is correct. The question also asks what the other methods say about the same claim.

## four routes into brain and cognition

Each of the four routes fails in its own way.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- overview ::@:: Measuring behaviour, imaging the living brain, studying deficits after brain damage, and running computational models.
- how many routes into brain and cognition there are ::@:: There are four.
- the reason the four routes are worth using together ::@:: Each route fails in a different way, so their agreement carries more weight than any single route.

### measuring behaviour

Behaviour takes the least machinery and reaches the furthest. Accuracy is the proportion of a recalled word list, response time the interval from a stimulus to a key press, and a judgment a rating on a multi-point scale. Each measures an output, so the process behind it can only be inferred. Caution belongs at that step.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- overview ::@:: Accuracy, response time, and judgments each measure an internal process indirectly.
- the three major behavioural measures ::@:: The three are accuracy, response time, and judgments given on a multi-point scale.
- an example of an accuracy measure ::@:: The proportion of a word list a participant recalls.
- an example of a response-time measure ::@:: The interval between a stimulus appearing and a key being pressed.
- what a judgment measure is ::@:: A judgment measure is a rating the participant gives on a multi-point scale.
- why every behavioural measure is indirect ::@:: It measures an output, and the process that produced the output can only be inferred.
- where a behavioural measure calls for caution ::@:: At the inference from the output to the process behind it.
- what a behavioural measure does to a claim about the mind ::@:: It constrains the claim without settling it.

### looking at the living brain

Imaging the working brain needs the most machinery. Spatial resolution is precision in localizing the area producing a signal; temporal resolution is precision in tracking change over time. Every method here trades one kind of precision against another: a method good at one is usually poor at the other. The limit is physical, not technical, because some signals reach the sensor faster than others. Two more costs sit on top: invasiveness, how far a method must push a foreign substance into the brain, and what the equipment and each run cost.

An area that lights up during a task is one whose activity is associated with the task. That is a measurement, not a demonstration, and a [correlational design](correlation.md) cannot close the gap. [Magnetic resonance imaging](magnetic%20resonance%20imaging.md), [electroencephalography](electroencephalography.md), and [magnetoencephalography](magnetoencephalography.md) each buy a kind of precision at the price of another.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- overview ::@:: Reading brain activity in a living person, limited by resolution, invasiveness, and cost.
- what a method's spatial resolution measures ::@:: How precisely the method localizes the area producing a signal.
- what a method's temporal resolution measures ::@:: How precisely the method tracks changes in activity over time.
- why a method strong on one resolution is usually weak on the other ::@:: Some methods read a signal that reaches the sensor faster than others, a physical limit rather than a technical one.
- what invasiveness measures about a method ::@:: How far a method has to go in putting a foreign substance into the brain.
- what a method's cost covers ::@:: Both the equipment and each run.
- the three living-brain methods compared here ::@:: Magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography, and magnetoencephalography.
- what imaging gives rather than shows ::@:: A measurement, not a demonstration.
- what activating a region during a task licenses ::@:: That the region's activity is associated with the task, not that the region produces it.
- what decides which method to use ::@:: The resolution, invasiveness, and cost that suit the question.

### deficits after brain damage

The evidence comes from patients with a known lesion. A region that does not work shows what the rest of the brain cannot do without it, and this is the only route that shows a deficit rather than a signal. Nobody can be assigned a brain injury on purpose, so the route is rare, irreproducible, and unassignable. Two cautions apply: were the patients typical before the damage, and is a compensatory strategy doing the work the missing region used to do? [Cognitive neuropsychology](cognitive%20neuropsychology.md) is built on this route.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- overview ::@:: Studying the deficits that follow a known brain lesion to work out what a region contributes.
- what brain damage shows that a signal cannot ::@:: It shows a deficit rather than a signal.
- the three problems with lesion evidence ::@:: It is rare, irreproducible, and impossible to assign on purpose.
- the first standard caution about brain-damaged patients ::@:: Whether the patients were typical before the damage.
- the second standard caution ::@:: Whether a compensatory strategy is doing the work the missing region used to do.
- the field built on studying deficits after brain damage ::@:: Cognitive neuropsychology.

### computational modeling

A computational model writes a process as a program. When its performance tracks a person's on a task, it is a candidate account of that process. Damaging a component then predicts what the person should fail at. A human subject cannot offer this: an explanation rather than a correlation, and a factor you can vary at will to watch what changes. [Computational model](computational%20model.md) covers the standard and what a match licenses.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- overview ::@:: Writing a process as a program and running it, so it can be manipulated in ways no brain and no participant can be.
- what a computational model is compared against ::@:: It is compared against the performance of a person on the same task.
- what a model that performs similarly to a human licenses ::@:: Treating it as a candidate account of the human process.
- what damaging a component of a model predicts ::@:: It predicts what the human should fail at.
- the first advantage of modeling over a human subject ::@:: It gives an explanation rather than a correlation.
- the second advantage ::@:: You can vary a factor inside it at will.

## what the agreement buys

An fMRI scan, a MEG recording, and the deficit profile of two lesion patients all point at the same region. Three instruments reached it, and they do not share a failure mode, so one systematic error is unlikely to produce all three. The strongest evidence for a role or a function comes from similar findings in studies using different methods. A claim resting on one instrument alone needs a second method most.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- overview ::@:: Independent methods that agree make a stronger claim than one method reporting the same result twice.
- the condition on a claim about a brain region ::@:: It has been reached by several instruments that do not share a failure mode.
- why independence between methods is what makes agreement informative ::@:: One systematic error is unlikely to produce the same result in several instruments at once.
- the strongest evidence for the role or function of a brain region ::@:: Similar findings from multiple studies that used different methods.
- the claim most in need of a second method ::@:: A claim that rests on a single instrument.
