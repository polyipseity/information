# cross-race effect

You pick out your own group's faces more accurately than anyone else's. The edge shows up for any group you have been around. It does not depend on which group a study measures.

Much perception research assumes one general faculty does the work. A group advantage in one skill does not fit that assumption. So the advantage comes from experience, not from two kinds of face.

You meet your own group's faces constantly, so you practise reading them more than anyone else. Practice sharpens your grasp of the features that carry expression. People who live around others from similar backgrounds read emotion on those faces more easily. The usual explanation is perceptual expertise. It is still only a hypothesis: no one model covers the whole effect.

## why the effect is awkward

The effect matters wherever a face is read under pressure: eyewitness testimony, law enforcement, and the design of face recognition technology.

Social psychology calls the same pattern an ingroup advantage. The name files it with the findings about how people treat their own group.

A neurological account would look for a brain difference in handling same-race and other-race faces. Imaging methods could test for it.

The cognitive account says nothing special happens to other-race faces: the same machinery handles both kinds, and the other kind gets less practice. Its claim is easier to test.

Training is the practical answer. If exposure creates the bias, changing exposure shrinks it.
