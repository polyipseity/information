# dependent source

An independent source fixes its own value, so its voltage or current can be read off the element. A dependent source, also called a controlled source, has a value that depends on, or is controlled by, a voltage or current at another location of the circuit. Its law is an expression in another circuit variable rather than a number, so the controlling quantity has to be found elsewhere before the element's voltage or current is known.

Dependent sources are high-level models of amplifiers: one rhombus with a gain replaces the amplifier's internal devices. The rhombus is the circuit symbol, and the source value is printed beside it as an expression in the controlling quantity, for example $\mu V_1$, $\beta I_1$, $g_m V_1$, or $r_m I_1$. The name of a type gives its pair directly: the first word names what controls the source, the second names what the source delivers. The controlling quantity takes subscript 1, and the controlled quantity at the output port takes subscript 2.

## types of dependent source

A voltage-controlled current source takes a controlling voltage and delivers a current. A current-controlled voltage source takes a controlling current and delivers a voltage.

The VCVS and the VCCS are driven by a voltage $V_1$; the CCCS and the CCVS by a current $I_1$.

A controlled voltage, such as $\mu V_1$ or $r_m I_1$, is drawn with $+$ and $-$ marks at the rhombus. A controlled current, such as $\beta I_1$ or $g_m V_1$, is drawn with an arrow. The two converting types take the prefix trans in their gain names: the current $I_2 = g_m V_1$ comes from a voltage $V_1$ elsewhere in the circuit, and the voltage $r_m I_1$ from a current $I_1$.

### voltage-controlled voltage source

The controlling quantity is a voltage $V_1$ across a branch at another location of the circuit, and the output is a voltage source of value $\mu V_1$ with terminal voltage $V_2$. The element is also called a voltage amplifier, since the gain $\mu$ is dimensionless and maps a voltage onto a voltage. Its rhombus carries $+$ and $-$ marks, and $V_1$ is measured across its own branch, with its own $+$ and $-$ marks.

### current-controlled current source

The controlling quantity is a current $I_1$ in a branch at another location of the circuit, and the output is a current source of value $\beta I_1$ delivered as $I_2$. The gain $\beta$ is dimensionless, mapping a current onto a current, so the element is also called a current amplifier. Its rhombus carries an arrow, and the controlling branch carries its own arrow labelled $I_1$.

### voltage-controlled current source

The controlling quantity is a voltage and the controlled quantity is a current. The rhombus of value $g_m V_1$ carries an arrow and delivers the current $I_2 = g_m V_1$. The gain $g_m$ is a transconductance, in units of current per voltage. The current $I_2$ comes from a voltage elsewhere in the circuit, so the gain carries the prefix trans. The element is also called a transconductance amplifier.

### current-controlled voltage source

The controlling quantity is a current $I_1$ and the controlled quantity is a voltage. The rhombus of value $r_m I_1$ carries $+$ and $-$ marks and provides the voltage $V_2$. The gain $r_m$ is a transresistance, in units of voltage per current. The prefix trans has the same origin as for $g_m$: the voltage $r_m I_1$ comes from a current elsewhere in the circuit, so the element is also called a transresistance amplifier.
