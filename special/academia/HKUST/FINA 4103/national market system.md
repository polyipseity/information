# national market system

The _national market system_ (NMS) was the project, run from the mid-1970s to 2007, that made the sixteen United States stock exchanges behave as one venue. Execution services at the New York Stock Exchange were to be the same as those at Nasdaq.

It was a project about connection. Sixteen matching engines trading separately are not one exchange, however similar their rules.

## connecting the venues

The three dimensions a fragmented market has to join are information, price tick, and execution price. Information comes first: each exchange holds a private view of its own book, and a trader needs the union of those views, which is what a [securities information processor](securities%20information%20processor.md) assembles.

The price tick has to be the same everywhere, or two venues quoting the same stock are not comparable. The [sub-penny rule](tick%20size.md) fixes it at one cent.

The execution price has to be protected across venues, or the best price on the market is not the price a trader can obtain. The [order protection and access rules](trade%20through.md) supply that protection.

## from a project to a rulebook

The national market system was not itself a rulebook. In June 2005 the SEC adopted [Regulation NMS](Regulation%20NMS.md). It carries the same aim of consolidated execution services and states that aim as four numbered rules. The privilege that lets any venue trade any listed stock, and so keeps the venues comparable in what they can trade, is recorded in [Unlisted Trading Privileges](Unlisted%20Trading%20Privileges.md).
