# market structure (finance)

In market microstructure, _market structure_ is the set of arrangements that decide how a trade happens: the trading architecture, the rules, the types of order that are available, how orders are executed, and how much of the process is visible. Markets are not all the same, and the differences change what a given trade costs.

---

## types of market

Markets differ in timing, which is speed, in order matching and handling, in flexibility and fungibility, in liquidity, and in pricing. Broadly they fall into three types: over-the-counter markets, fixing and auctions, and continuous trading with a central limit-order book. A pre-IPO stock is not publicly listed, is held by management, founders, and private investors, and changes hands by bilateral negotiation, which raises the question of how to compare its price efficiency with that of a listed stock. A bond is auctioned in the primary market when it is first issued and then traded over the counter in the secondary market. Whether the auction achieves higher market quality than a centralized limit-order book, and how over-the-counter trading compares, are open questions. A single stock can trade at venues that use different protocols, such as a dealer market and a double auction.

## centralized markets

In a centralized market, trading happens on an exchange and participants place orders into a limit-order book, which matches them much like a double-sided auction. Search frictions are absent or very small and the process is highly transparent. The trading structure of major stocks is centralized.

## decentralized markets

In a decentralized market, trading is over-the-counter and bilateral: a customer trades with a dealer, and dealers trade among themselves. Finding a counterparty takes a costly search-and-matching process, hence the name. The foreign exchange market is the standard example.

- cost that defines a decentralized market ::@:: Finding a counterparty requires a costly search-and-matching process.
- which market is the standard decentralized example ::@:: The foreign exchange market.

## choosing a trading structure

Neither structure dominates. A participant who wants to buy a large amount of an asset faces a choice between an over-the-counter market with search costs and a centralized exchange with a public order book. The structures offer different benefits and costs for different trading purposes, so the right choice depends on the motive for trading. Whether either structure is socially optimal, and whether optimality is even attainable, is a separate question.

## exchange competition and fragmentation

The United States runs 16 stock exchanges registered with the SEC as national securities exchanges, alongside many alternative trading systems such as dark pools. A venue breakdown from an earlier date, attributed to Cboe Global Markets, FINRA, and SIFMA estimates, counts 13 exchanges beside 33 equity alternative trading systems and over-the-counter venues. Three families own most of them: four Cboe venues (BYX, BZX, EDGA, and EDGX), four NYSE venues (the New York Stock Exchange, Arca, American, and National), and three Nasdaq venues (BX, PHLX, and the Nasdaq Stock Market). The other two are the Chicago Stock Exchange and IEX, and Intercontinental Exchange owns the NYSE venues. Fragmentation is not universal: Hong Kong runs the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, the United Kingdom the London Stock Exchange, and Japan the Tokyo Stock Exchange, while China's market is organized into A-shares and B-shares. The SEC has promoted exchange competition, and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 is one example, on the premise that competition is good for consumers. Regulation National Market System, whose Rule 611 is the order protection rule, is a complicated rulebook that keeps the competing exchanges workable. Explicit trading fees are very small, roughly 0.05 basis points, yet traders still pay large costs elsewhere. Why markets differ in this way, what fragmented markets do to price, fees, and execution quality, how to trade in a fragmented market, and which design serves users and social welfare, all remain open questions.

## electronic trading

Information technology has altered market structure. An electronic trading system is more accurate, faster, and more transparent, and it triggered structural changes in trade execution and in pricing, including the shift between a call auction and a limit-order book. AI has since spread across Wall Street, and the physical trading floor itself has been transformed.

The trading floor shows the change. In 1879 it is a crowd: an engraving of a hall packed shoulder to shoulder with men in top hats and bowlers, arms raised and papers in hand, all trading in person. Today it is a data centre where electronic matching happens: racks studded with status lights along a blue-lit aisle, and nobody in the room. Matching buyers and sellers no longer needs a room full of people; machines do that work.
