# Bayes' theorem

Trading in [financial markets](financial%20system.md) runs on information, and acting on it means updating a belief: an event is observed, and the probability of something unobserved has to move. Bayes' rule performs that update, and in a market the event observed is usually a signal about an asset's value.

The rule starts from the probability held before the observation, the prior, and returns the probability after it, the posterior. For that it needs the probability of the observation itself under each possible state, which the conditional probability of one event given another supplies.

## conditional probability

Two random events $A$ and $B$ are related when the occurrence of one changes how likely the other is. The _conditional probability_ of $A$ given $B$, written $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B)$, is the probability of $A$ happening once $B$ is observed: for $\operatorname{Pr}(B) \neq 0$, $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B) = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B)}{\operatorname{Pr}(B)}$, where $A\cap B$ is the event that both $A$ and $B$ occur. Dividing by $\operatorname{Pr}(B)$ keeps only the cases where $B$ holds, and the probability of $A$ is then read off among those cases alone.

The numerator $\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B)$ is the mass of the worlds where both events hold, and the denominator $\operatorname{Pr}(B)$ is the mass still available once $B$ is known, so the division rescales the surviving worlds until their probabilities add to one again. A denominator of one says the observation was going to happen whatever the state: no case was ruled out, and $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B)$ comes back as $\operatorname{Pr}(A)$. A denominator close to zero does the opposite: the observation is a coincidence confined to a thin slice of the world, and dividing by that small number turns the slice into a near certainty.

Toss a coin three times and let $A$ be "at least 2 heads" and $B$ "the first toss is tails". The eight outcomes HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT are equally likely, so $\operatorname{Pr}(A) = \operatorname{Pr}(B) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $A\cap B = \{\text{THH}\}$. Given tails on the first flip, the probability of at least two heads is $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B) = \frac{1/8}{1/2} = \frac{1}{4}$: the observation changes the inference.

When $A$ and $B$ are independent, such as two separate tosses, $\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B) = \operatorname{Pr}(A)\operatorname{Pr}(B)$, and the conditional probability collapses to the unconditional one, $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B) = \operatorname{Pr}(A)$: learning $B$ says nothing about $A$.

Financial markets run the same pattern: the price dynamics inferred from historical information, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) announcement from the current market condition, and the fundamental asset value of a firm from its financial statement. In each case an observed event $B$ moves the probability of an unobserved event $A$, and together those inferences rebuild the conditional expectation $\operatorname{E}[v \mid \text{available information}]$ that the efficient price of [price discovery](price%20discovery.md) rests on.

## law of total probability

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability gives the _multiplicative rule_: $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B) = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B)}{\operatorname{Pr}(B)}$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B) = \operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B)\operatorname{Pr}(B)$, because "$A$ and $B$ occur" is the same event as "$B$ occurs, and $A$ occurs conditional on $B$".

Now let several conditions cover every possibility: $\Omega = \{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_N\}$ is the set of all events, with the $B_k$ mutually exclusive. The pieces $A\cap B_1, \ldots, A\cap B_N$ split $A\cap\Omega$ into disjoint parts, and the multiplicative rule turns that split into the law of total probability: $\operatorname{Pr}(A) = \operatorname{Pr}(A\cap\Omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B_i)\operatorname{Pr}(B_i)$. In words: divide into all possible cases and weight each case by how likely it is.

The partition is what makes the sum legal. The $B_i$ are disjoint and together exhaust $\Omega$, so the pieces $A\cap B_1, \ldots, A\cap B_N$ are disjoint and together make up $A$: each piece is the part of $A$ living in one case, and adding the pieces counts every way $A$ can happen once and none of them twice. That same sum supplies the denominator of Bayes' rule, and it has to run over the whole space. Leave one case out and $\operatorname{Pr}(B)$ comes out too small, so dividing by it inflates the posterior of every case that was kept. A total of one says the news was going to arrive whichever case held, which leaves the observation unable to separate one case from another and returns the prior.

The first coin toss splits the world into $B_1$ = tails and $B_2$ = heads, mutually exclusive and covering all possibilities, so $\operatorname{Pr}(A) = \operatorname{Pr}(B_1)\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B_1) + \operatorname{Pr}(B_2)\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B_2)$. A macroeconomic instance weights its cases the same way: $A$ is the Federal Reserve (Fed) raising the interest rate, $B_1$ is inflation above 2%, and $B_2$ is the unemployment rate above 3%.

## Bayes' rule

Bayes' rule inverts a conditional probability by combining the definition of conditional probability with the multiplicative rule: $\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B) = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(A\cap B)}{\operatorname{Pr}(B)} = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(B\mid A)\operatorname{Pr}(A)}{\operatorname{Pr}(B)}$. The numerator is the joint probability that the state is $A$ and the news arrives, and dividing by $\operatorname{Pr}(B)$ keeps only the cases where it did arrive, so what is left is a probability over the states given the news.

The prior $\operatorname{Pr}(A)$ says where the belief stood, the likelihood $\operatorname{Pr}(B\mid A)$ says how readily each state produces this news, and their product is the mass each state contributes to seeing it. The denominator is one number shared by every state, so it sets the level of the posterior and never the ranking: it rescales the products back into probabilities that add to one, and dropping it leaves the posterior odds, $\frac{\operatorname{Pr}(A\mid B)}{\operatorname{Pr}(A^{c}\mid B)} = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(B\mid A)}{\operatorname{Pr}(B\mid A^{c})}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{Pr}(A)}{\operatorname{Pr}(A^{c})}$, where $A^{c}$ is the event that $A$ does not occur.

The posterior reweights the prior rather than replacing it. A state whose prior probability is zero stays at zero whatever the likelihood, because zero times anything is zero, and a state that produces the news exactly as readily as its rival keeps its prior odds whatever the level of the denominator. A likelihood ratio of one is that second case: the news is a coincidence equally likely in both states, and the belief has not moved.

To infer an asset value $A$ from news $B$, it suffices to know how likely the news is under each value, $\operatorname{Pr}(B\mid A)$. When a firm's fundamental is bad, its earnings announcement should be bad with some probability, and an observed announcement moves the probability of the bad fundamental. Signals and news come from unobservable underlying fundamentals, so each one is described by a conditional probability of being right or wrong: that probability is the precision of the signal.

### updating on a test result

Before a rapid antigen test, a person believes they are infected, the state $s = I$, with probability 0.6, and not infected, $s = N$, with probability 0.4, while the true state stays unobservable. The test reads $T = +1$ or $T = -1$ by the rule $\operatorname{Pr}(+1\mid I) = \operatorname{Pr}(-1\mid N) = 0.95$ and $\operatorname{Pr}(+1\mid N) = \operatorname{Pr}(-1\mid I) = 0.05$: the 0.95 is the precision of the test, the conditional probability that its signal is correct.

After the result $T = -1$, Bayes' rule gives $\operatorname{Pr}(I\mid -1) = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(I)\operatorname{Pr}(-1\mid I)}{\operatorname{Pr}(-1)}$, and the law of total probability supplies the denominator, $\operatorname{Pr}(-1) = \operatorname{Pr}(N)\operatorname{Pr}(-1\mid N) + \operatorname{Pr}(I)\operatorname{Pr}(-1\mid I)$, a correct negative plus a false negative. Plugging the numbers in, $\operatorname{Pr}(I\mid -1) = \frac{0.6 \times 0.05}{0.4 \times 0.95 + 0.6 \times 0.05} = \frac{3}{41} \approx 0.07$. The negative result drops the belief in infection from 0.6 to about 0.07.

The prior odds of infection are $\frac{0.6}{0.4} = 1.5$ to one, a negative result is $\frac{0.05}{0.95} = \frac{1}{19}$ as likely under infection as under no infection, and the posterior odds are the product, about one to thirteen, which is the $\frac{3}{41}$ above. The test moves the odds by a factor of 19 whichever way it reads, so how far it carries a belief depends on where that belief started. A positive result from prior odds of one to a hundred would reach nineteen to a hundred, still about one chance in six. A base rate far from even survives a test that is 95% accurate in each direction, because the prior multiplies the likelihood ratio instead of being replaced by it.

### updating on an asset value

The true value $V$ of an asset is unobservable but is either $V_H$ or $V_L$, each with probability $1/2$. A signal $S$ about the value takes the values $S_H$ and $S_L$ with precision $\delta > 1/2$, meaning it is more often right than wrong: $\operatorname{Pr}(S_H\mid V_H) = \operatorname{Pr}(S_L\mid V_L) = \delta$ and $\operatorname{Pr}(S_H\mid V_L) = \operatorname{Pr}(S_L\mid V_H) = 1 - \delta$. Earnings announcements are signals of this kind.

After observing $S = S_H$, Bayes' rule with the law of total probability in the denominator gives $\operatorname{Pr}(V_H\mid S_H) = \frac{\operatorname{Pr}(V_H)\operatorname{Pr}(S_H\mid V_H)}{\operatorname{Pr}(V_H)\operatorname{Pr}(S_H\mid V_H) + \operatorname{Pr}(V_L)\operatorname{Pr}(S_H\mid V_L)} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\delta}{\frac{1}{2}\delta + \frac{1}{2}(1-\delta)} = \delta$. The posterior $\delta$ sits above the prior $1/2$: a good signal shifts the belief toward $V_H$, and the shift grows with the precision $\delta$. At $\delta = 1$ the signal is always correct, so $S_H$ settles $V = V_H$; at $\delta = 1/2$ the signal is uninformative and the posterior returns to the prior $1/2$.

The posterior equals $\delta$ because the numbers are symmetric: the prior odds are one, so the posterior odds are the likelihood ratio $\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}$, and a probability is that ratio over one plus it, which returns $\delta$. The jump is set by the ratio, so a $\delta$ barely above one half buys almost nothing. At $\delta = 0.55$ the ratio is about $1.22$ and the belief moves from $0.5$ to $0.55$; at $\delta = 0.9$ the ratio is $9$ and the same update moves it to $0.9$.

## conditional moments of normal variables

To infer the value of a normal random variable $v \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, such as an investment return, the value itself is never observed; only its distribution is known. A noisy signal of $v$ is observed instead: $s = v + e$, where the noise follows $e \sim N(0, \sigma_e^2)$ and is independent of $v$. The signal is unbiased, $\operatorname{E}[s] = \operatorname{E}[v]$.

Precision is the inverse variance. The value carries $\tau = 1/\operatorname{Var}(v) = \sigma^{-2}$ and the noise carries $\tau_e = 1/\operatorname{Var}(e) = \sigma_e^{-2}$, called the signal precision. The two extremes pin down what a signal can be: $\tau_e = \infty$ ($\sigma_e = 0$) makes $s = v$ a perfect signal, and $\tau_e = 0$ ($\sigma_e = \infty$) makes $s$ pure noise. The two quantities to compute are the conditional expectation $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s]$ and the conditional variance $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s)$, also written $\mu_{v\mid s}$ and $\sigma^2_{v\mid s}$.

A first route to conditioning runs through the densities. Take two normal densities over the same score, $N(\mu_a, \sigma_a^2)$ and $N(\mu_b, \sigma_b^2)$, and split the score at a cutoff $t$ placed between the two means. The densities overlap: a score near $t$ could plausibly have come from either of them, so the score is a reading of which density produced it rather than a label.

The overlap is measured by two errors, each a slab of one density on the wrong side of the cutoff. The error $e_{a\mid b}$ is the mass of the $b$-density left of $t$, the case where a $b$-score is read as an $a$-score, and $e_{b\mid a}$ is the mass of the $a$-density right of $t$, the reverse mistake. A narrow density keeps most of its mass within a few standard deviations of its own mean, so little of it reaches across a cutoff sitting between the means. That is what a narrow density buys: both errors shrink at once, and a density narrow enough makes the score almost a label. The two errors are the conditional probabilities of a test reading: the chance of a false positive and the chance of a false negative.

The two densities can be integrated to get those errors, which is the tedious route and unnecessary. The joint normal density is a quadratic form in the exponent, so completing the square in one variable splits it into a part in that variable and a part in the other. The conditional density comes out normal as well, with a mean and a variance that are coefficients of the squared term rather than integrals over the two errors. That is why the closed forms below exist at all.

### posterior mean as a precision-weighted average

The conditional expectation works out to $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s] = \frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_e}\mu + \frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}s$. Initially the belief about the mean is $\operatorname{E}[v] = \mu$; observing $s$ folds the signal in, and a very good earnings announcement raises the expected return. The size of the shift is the weight $\frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$ on the signal, and the more precise the signal, the heavier that weight. As $\tau_e \to 0$ the expectation returns to $\mu$, since pure noise earns no weight; as $\tau_e \to \infty$ it lands on $s$, the perfect signal. Before $s$ is drawn, the conditional expectation is again a normal random variable, because $s$ is normal.

This combines two estimates of one unknown, and each enters in proportion to the precision it brings. A sharp number is nearly the answer on its own and a vague one hardly moves it, so the weight $\frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$ is the signal's share of the total precision $\tau + \tau_e$. The pair of weights is that share and nothing more: the prior mean carries $\frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_e}$ of the belief and the signal carries $\frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$. The two weights add to one, which is what stops the average itself from scaling the belief up or down.

The prior keeps the share $\frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_e}$ however much arrives, and that share is not a concession to convention. A prior with finite precision is an estimate like the signal, worth exactly what its precision is worth, so it stays in the average for the reason a second opinion stays. Only a prior of infinite precision holds the belief fixed against any signal, and only a signal of infinite precision erases the prior.

The difference $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s] - \mu = \frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}(s-\mu)$ reads as a response: the signal sits $s - \mu$ away from the belief, and the belief adopts the fraction $\frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$ of that gap. A trader holding a view acts on the same rule: the position taken is proportional to the gap between the signal and the view, and the constant of proportionality is the weight, so the sharper the signal the harder the trader trades for the same gap.

- why the prior keeps the weight $\frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_e}$ on $\mu$ however precise the signal becomes ::@:: The prior is an estimate with precision $\tau$, so it stays in the average in proportion to what it is worth; only $\tau = \infty$ holds the belief fixed against any signal and only $\tau_e = \infty$ erases it.
- read $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s] - \mu = \frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}(s - \mu)$ as a response to the signal ::@:: The belief adopts the fraction $\frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$ of the gap $s - \mu$ between the signal and the belief, the way a trader's position is proportional to the gap between the signal and the view, with the weight as the constant.

### posterior variance as added precision

The conditional variance is $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \frac{1}{\tau+\tau_e}$. Observing the signal leaves less uncertainty than the prior, $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) < \operatorname{Var}(v)$, because $s$ conveys information about $v$. The reduction is large when the signal reflects $v$ precisely, that is when $\tau_e$ is large and the noise is insignificant. In precision terms $\frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s)} = \tau + \tau_e$: observing a signal is worth exactly its precision, since the prior precision and the signal precision add. How much that added precision buys against the prior variance is the informativeness $\eta = \frac{\tau+\tau_e}{\tau}$, treated in [signal informativeness](signal%20informativeness.md). The extremes match the mean: $\tau_e = \infty$ leaves no uncertainty, and $\tau_e = 0$ leaves $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \operatorname{Var}(v)$.

The sum is legitimate because the two pieces of information are independent. The prior mean $\mu$ is an estimate of $v$ whose error $\mu - v$ has variance $\sigma^2$, and the signal $s = v + e$ is an estimate whose error $-e$ has variance $\sigma_e^2$. The noise $e$ is independent of $v$, so those two errors are independent as well. Combine the estimates as $w\mu + (1-w)s$ and the combination carries the error $w(\mu - v) - (1-w)e$, of variance $w^2\sigma^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_e^2$: the cross term $2w(1-w)\operatorname{Cov}(\mu - v, e)$ is the covariance of two independent errors, so it is zero. Minimizing that variance puts the weight $w = \frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_e}$ on the prior mean and $\frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$ on the signal, which is the weighted average of the section above, and the variance at the minimum is $\frac{1}{\tau+\tau_e}$. Read the last step backwards and the precisions are being added: with the cross term gone, the variance-minimizing weights are proportional to the precisions, so the reciprocal of the smallest variance is their sum.

Independence is what earns the sum, and correlation is what voids it. If $e$ moves with $v$, the cross term turns positive and $\frac{1}{\tau+\tau_e}$ understates the variance left, so the added precision overstates the gain. The part the two estimates share has been counted twice, once in $\tau$ and once in $\tau_e$, and weights that add to one cannot repair that. Two analysts reading one research note are cases in point, and the more correlated the two errors the larger the double count. The correlation case is worked out for a normal pair at the end of the note, where a correlation $\rho$ leaves $(1-\rho^2)\sigma_x^2$ of the variance rather than removing a share equal to $\rho$.

The form is a reciprocal sum, $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_e^2}\right)^{-1}$, and that is why it is not an average of the two variances. An average would sit between them and leave the uncertainty of the worse look in the answer. Two independent looks at the same unknown have to beat the better single look, and a reciprocal sum does beat it: the posterior variance lies below $\sigma^2$ and below $\sigma_e^2$ whatever the precisions are. Equal precisions give $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$, exactly half the prior variance, since the precision doubled. A signal worth a tenth of the prior precision leaves $\frac{10}{11}$ of the variance, the modest return on a weak signal.

### derivations for jointly normal variables

The closed forms are a special case of conditioning one normal variable on another. Let $x \sim N(\mu_x, \sigma_x^2)$ and $y \sim N(\mu_y, \sigma_y^2)$ with $\rho = \operatorname{Corr}(x, y)$. The conditional density is $f(x\mid y) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f(y)}$: the joint density over the marginal density of $y$. The joint density of the pair is $f(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\sigma_x^2(x-\mu_x)^2 + \sigma_y^2(y-\mu_y)^2 - 2\rho\sigma_x\sigma_y(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y)}{2\sigma_x^2\sigma_y^2(1-\rho^2)}\right\}$, and dividing by the marginal density of $y$ leaves a normal density in $x$: $f(x\mid y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_x\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left[x - \left(\mu_x + \frac{\rho\sigma_x}{\sigma_y}(y - \mu_y)\right)\right]^2}{2(1-\rho^2)\sigma_x^2}\right\}$. The conditional moments are therefore $\mu_{x\mid y} = \mu_x + \frac{\rho\sigma_x}{\sigma_y}(y - \mu_y)$ and $\sigma^2_{x\mid y} = (1-\rho^2)\sigma_x^2$.

The same two moments fall out of a covariance matrix in one step, which is the route to recall. Stack the pair as $z = (x, y)^{\top}$, with mean vector $\mu_z = (\mu_x, \mu_y)^{\top}$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_x^2 & \rho\sigma_x\sigma_y\\ \rho\sigma_x\sigma_y & \sigma_y^2\end{pmatrix}$, so that the joint density is $f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi\lvert\Sigma\rvert^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(z-\mu_z)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(z-\mu_z)\right\}$.

Partitioned into the $x$ block first and the $y$ block second, the joint density gives the conditional directly: $x\mid y \sim N\left(\mu_x + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(y-\mu_y),\ \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\right)$. Reading a conditional off a partitioned covariance matrix needs only the blocks. The two variances sit on the diagonal as $\Sigma_{11} = \sigma_x^2$ and $\Sigma_{22} = \sigma_y^2$, and the off-diagonal blocks $\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21} = \rho\sigma_x\sigma_y$ are where the correlation sits, so every term above is built from those three numbers.

The blocks work out from the inverse. Each block is a scalar here, so $\Sigma_{22}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma_y^2}$, and the block inverse $\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_x^{-2} & -\rho\sigma_x^{-1}\sigma_y^{-1}\\ -\rho\sigma_x^{-1}\sigma_y^{-1} & \sigma_y^{-2}\end{pmatrix}$ carries the same three numbers. Then $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1} = \frac{\rho\sigma_x\sigma_y}{\sigma_y^2} = \rho\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y}$ and $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} = \sigma_x^2 - \rho^2\sigma_x^2 = (1-\rho^2)\sigma_x^2$, the conditional mean and variance the density split produced. The density split shows where the blocks come from, and the matrix route is the one to reconstruct on demand.

The blocks are enough because they hold everything the observation can teach. The off-diagonal block $\Sigma_{12}$ is the only number tying $x$ to $y$, and $\Sigma_{22}$ is how much $y$ moves on its own, so their product $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ is a covariance over the observer's variance: the response in $x$ per unit of surprise in $y$, scaled by how strongly the pair moves together. The leftover $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$ takes what the observation accounts for away from the variance $x$ started with, and nothing is left once the correlation reaches one. For a normal pair the mean and the covariance describe the joint distribution completely, so no third block exists that the conditional could need.

Both routes describe one regression. The slope $\rho\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y}$ is the regression coefficient $\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(y)}$, since $\operatorname{Cov}(x,y) = \rho\sigma_x\sigma_y$ and $\operatorname{Var}(y) = \sigma_y^2$, and the variance left after the observation is $\sigma^2_{x\mid y} = (1-\rho^2)\sigma_x^2$. The share of the variance of $x$ that observing $y$ explains is therefore $1 - \frac{\sigma^2_{x\mid y}}{\sigma_x^2} = \rho^2$, so a correlation of one half explains a quarter of the variance. The share is $\rho^2$ rather than $\rho$ because a share of a variance is measured in variance units. A correlation of one half says the pair moves together at half the scale of the value's own standard deviation, and what stays unexplained is $1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$ of the variance, not one half of it. A source that tracks the value half as strongly as the value tracks itself settles a quarter of the uncertainty, and a source that settles half the variance must correlate $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.71$ with the value.

The share $\rho^2$ is also the weight the update puts on a signal, which [signal informativeness](signal%20informativeness.md) takes up.
