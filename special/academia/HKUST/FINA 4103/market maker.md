# market maker

A _market maker_ is a participant that stands ready to trade an asset on both sides. On a stock exchange the role splits into the specialist, who runs the auction for a stock assigned to it and may not trade for profit, and the dealer, who competes for order flow and may.

## dealer and specialist markets

An exchange intermediates the trade, and so does the mechanism it runs: most modern exchanges run a limit-order book, which is a double auction. What differs is how the auction is run: automated order-matching technology is one answer, specialists another. The New York Stock Exchange and the American Stock Exchange use specialist auctions, while Nasdaq runs a dealer market with an automated limit-order book.

## designated market maker

A designated market maker is assigned a specific stock and holds the double auctions for it as a market maker. The role is not a profit centre: a designated market maker does not buy and sell for profit and is prohibited from outbidding or offering against floor traders for profit. New York Stock Exchange Rule 104 charges it with trading for its own account to help maintain a fair and orderly market (the lecture slides give the citation as "code 12a", which does not match the current rule number), and it receives orders from traders, executes them as an auctioneer, and intervenes by actively buying and selling when the market faces a disturbance.

Floor traders stand between part of the order flow and the book. Regular traders, brokers, and dealers either place orders directly into the limit-order book or place them with a floor trader, and a floor trader can internalize an order or pass it to the book. The designated market maker interacts with the floor traders and feeds the same book.

## registered market maker

Nasdaq has no trading floor and no designated market makers. Its auctions are held by an automated matching algorithm: traders submit quotes, the limit-order book displays them, and incoming orders are matched at the best available prices on the book. Standing limit orders take over the specialist's job of accepting or offering to incoming traders. Registered market makers play a role similar to a designated market maker's, but they are dealers and are allowed to trade for profit, and competition among them keeps liquidity provision fair.
