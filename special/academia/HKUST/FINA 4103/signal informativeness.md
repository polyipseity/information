# signal informativeness

A signal is worth what it removes from the uncertainty about the value. Let the value be $v \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with precision $\tau = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(v)}$, and the signal be $s = v + e$, with noise $e \sim N(0, \sigma_e^2)$ independent of $v$ and precision $\tau_e = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(e)}$. [Bayes' theorem](Bayes'%27%20theorem.md) takes the prior variance $\operatorname{Var}(v) = \frac{1}{\tau}$ to the posterior variance $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \frac{1}{\tau + \tau_e}$.

A traded price is a signal about the value as well. How much of the informed trader's knowledge a price carries is measured by pairing two conditional variances, the informed trader's against a price watcher's, rather than a prior against a posterior. An observed signal that mixes the value with a constant and with noise needs no separate theory: the constant shifts it and a coefficient scales it, both reversibly, so every such signal reduces to $s = v + e$. Reduced that way, the update is a regression, and the weight it puts on the signal is the share of the signal's variance that belongs to the value.

- how the weight an update puts on a signal reads as a regression, for a value $v$ and a signal $s = v + e$ with noise $e$ independent of $v$ ::@:: It is the ordinary least squares (OLS) slope of $v$ on $s$, which equals the squared correlation $\rho^2$: the share of the signal's variance that is the value.

## measuring informativeness

How useful a signal is has a measure: $\eta \equiv \frac{\operatorname{Var}(v)}{\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s)} = \frac{\tau + \tau_e}{\tau} = 1 + \frac{\tau_e}{\tau}$, the _informativeness_ of the signal, also called its signal-to-noise ratio. The conditional variance sits underneath: the numerator $\operatorname{Var}(v)$ is the uncertainty before the signal arrives and the denominator $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s)$ the uncertainty left after it, so the measure is the prior uncertainty over the leftover uncertainty. The precision form looks inverted, $\frac{\tau + \tau_e}{\tau}$, and is not: a precision is a reciprocal variance, and inverting the conditional variance $\frac{1}{\tau + \tau_e}$ puts $\tau + \tau_e$ on top. That posterior precision is a sum because the prior and the signal's error are independent: with no correlation between them, no cross term survives in the variance of a weighted combination of the two, so the weights that minimize that variance are the precisions themselves. [Bayes' theorem](Bayes'%20theorem.md#posterior%20variance%20as%20added%20precision) works the argument through, and its correlated case is the warning: a signal that repeats part of the prior adds less than its own precision, because the overlap is counted in both terms.

$\eta - 1 = \frac{\tau_e}{\tau}$ is the information the signal adds relative to what was already known. That remainder is a noise-to-signal ratio, the noise precision over the prior precision. A sharp prior, with large $\tau$, starves the signal of anything to add and holds informativeness near 1; a sharp signal, with large $\tau_e$, dominates whatever was already known, and informativeness grows with it. At $\tau_e = 0$ the signal is pure noise, $\eta = 1$, and nothing is learned.

$\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \frac{1}{\eta}\operatorname{Var}(v)$ is the same definition read as a scaling. Observing the signal multiplies the prior uncertainty by $\frac{1}{\eta}$, so $\eta$ is the factor by which the signal shrinks it.

That factor is worth reading on its own, because it is dimensionless and so compares signals of different units. The counting runs upward from one: $\eta = 2$ means the signal halves the variance, $\eta = \frac{11}{10}$ means it takes about a tenth off, and $\eta$ below one never arises because an observation cannot make the world less predictable. The quantity $1 - \frac{1}{\eta} = \frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}$ is the share of the prior variance the signal removes, which is the same number as the weight the update puts on the signal. Informativeness and the updating weight are one quantity written two ways: a signal that removes about a tenth of the variance is one a belief follows about a tenth of the way. The weight carries the two limits the informativeness carries: as $\tau_e \to 0$ the belief stays at $\mu$, and as $\tau_e \to \infty$ it lands on $s$.

## price efficiency

A traded price informs the way a signal does, and measuring it needs both quantities conditional. Write the value's prior precision as $\tau_v$, which leaves $\tau_p$ free for the precision of the price as a signal about $v$. An informed trader who observes $s = v + e$ is left with $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s) = \frac{1}{\tau_v + \tau_e}$. An observer who sees only the market price $p^{*}$ is left with $\operatorname{Var}(v\mid p^{*}) = \frac{1}{\tau_v + \tau_p}$.

Price efficiency pairs the two, the informed trader's uncertainty over the observer's: $\frac{\operatorname{Var}(v\mid s)}{\operatorname{Var}(v\mid p^{*})} = \frac{\tau_v + \tau_p}{\tau_v + \tau_e} \le 1$. The ratio reaches 1 only when $\tau_p = \tau_e$, that is when the price carries everything the signal does, and only an absence of noise trading gets it there. Noise trading is what holds it below 1: the price is the signal with noise added, so its precision falls short of the signal's own, and the shortfall is exactly what the ratio measures. How much of the informed trading reaches the price is what [price discovery](price%20discovery.md) asks about a market. Where $\tau_p$ comes from is the [Grossman–Stiglitz paradox](Grossman%E2%80%93Stiglitz%20paradox.md).

The comparison runs the other way round from the informativeness ratio. There the benchmark was the prior, and anything the signal added pushed the ratio above 1. Here the benchmark is the informed trader, who already holds the signal, so the price can only fall short of that knowledge. Both terms are conditional variances for the same reason: the top is the uncertainty left to a trader who has the signal and the bottom the uncertainty left to one who has only the price.

Because the two quantities belong to two people, the ratio answers a question about advantage. A ratio of one half says the price leaves an outsider with twice the variance the informed trader carries: the price has already handed over half the stock of precision the insider holds, and what the insider keeps is the edge. A ratio near one means the price watcher knows nearly as much as the trader who saw the signal, which is what it means for the market to have priced the information. A ratio near zero means the price tells its watcher little beyond the prior. Neither end says anything about how uncertain the market is, so a noisy market and a quiet one with the same ratio leave their participants equally unequal.

## standardized form of a signal

Order flow is the standard case of a signal that does not arrive as $s = v + e$. Let the value be $v$, and let an observer see the total flow $s_o = \alpha + \beta v + \gamma e$ for known constants $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$. Each coefficient names a group of traders. A mass $\beta$ of insiders, or a single insider trading with intensity $\beta$, trades on the information $v$. A mass $\gamma$ of non-fundamental random traders each trade their noise $e$. A mass $\alpha$ of constant traders, such as a fund rebalancing, trades the constant. The observer sees $s_o$ and has to infer $v$ from it. Splitting that flow into informed trading and noise is the question [market microstructure](market%20microstructure.md) takes up.

The order flow is informationally equivalent to $s_{\text{new}} = \frac{s_o - \alpha}{\beta} = v + \frac{\gamma}{\beta}e$. Subtracting a known constant and dividing by a known coefficient are invertible operations, so neither adds information about $v$ nor removes any: knowing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ recovers $s_o$ from $s_{\text{new}}$. Every signal that mixes a constant, the value, and noise is standardized into $s = v + e$ before the updating formulas are applied, and nothing is lost by doing it.

The reduction carries the noise with it. The new noise $e_{\text{new}} \equiv \frac{\gamma}{\beta}e$ is normal with mean zero and variance $\frac{\gamma^2}{\beta^2}\sigma_e^2$, so its precision is $\tau_{\text{new}} = \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e$: carrying the noise weight $\gamma$ through the division by $\beta$ rescales the precision by $\frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}$, up when informed trading outweighs noise trading and down when it does not.

Only the ratio $\frac{\gamma}{\beta}$ survives the reduction, and it is a noise-to-signal ratio: how much noise rides on each unit of value. Two flows with the same $\frac{\gamma}{\beta}$ carry the same information about $v$ whatever their constants and their separate coefficients, so one number summarizes what such a signal says. At $\beta = 0$ no informed trader is in the market, $\frac{\gamma}{\beta}$ is infinite, and the flow says nothing about $v$.

The constant $\alpha$ says nothing about $v$, and subtracting it removes it. The coefficient $\beta$ is the scale at which the value arrives, and dividing by it sets that coefficient to one, so any $\beta$ and $\gamma$ scaled by the same amount give the same standardized signal. What cannot be normalized away is the size of the noise relative to the value, and that is exactly $\frac{\gamma}{\beta}$.

At $\beta = 0$ the flow is a constant plus noise whatever $v$ is, so many values of $v$ produce the same observation and no reading separates them. A market whose flow carries no informed trading gives its observers nothing: a price built from that flow says nothing about the value, and a trader who sees only the flow stays at the prior.

With $s_o = 100$ and $\alpha = 10$ the observer computes $90 = \beta v + \gamma e$, then divides by $\beta$ to reach $s_{\text{new}}$. The update runs on the standardized signal, $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s_o] = \operatorname{E}[v\mid s_{\text{new}}] = \frac{\tau}{\tau + \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e}\mu + \frac{\frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e}{\tau + \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e}s_{\text{new}}$, and restating it in the observed flow gives $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s_o] = \frac{\tau}{\tau + \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e}\mu + \frac{\frac{\beta}{\gamma^2}\tau_e}{\tau + \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e}(s_o - \alpha)$. Both coefficients are shares of the total precision $\tau + \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e$, so the standardized flow enters with the share $\frac{\tau_{\text{new}}}{\tau + \tau_{\text{new}}}$ of it, where $\tau_{\text{new}} = \frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\tau_e$ is the precision left after the scaling by $\frac{\gamma}{\beta}$, and the prior mean keeps the rest.

The response sits in the second coefficient. A unit of unexpected flow moves the standardized signal by $\frac{1}{\beta}$, since the value is recovered by dividing the flow by $\beta$, and the weight on $(s_o - \alpha)$ is the standardized weight carried across that division. Both extremes leave the response at zero. With $\beta$ near zero a dollar of flow carries almost no value, so it moves nothing. With $\beta$ large the same value moves the flow a long way, so one dollar of flow is a small part of one unit of $v$ and barely moves the belief. The response is largest at an intermediate insider mass, where a dollar of flow is informative without being diluted.

## weight as a regression coefficient

The conditional mean has a form that needs no density: $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s] = \mu + \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s,v)}{\operatorname{Var}(s)}(s - \operatorname{E}[s])$. In the signal setting the slope works out to $\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s,v)}{\operatorname{Var}(s)} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + \sigma_e^2} = \frac{\tau_e}{\tau + \tau_e}$, the weight on $s$ in $\operatorname{E}[v\mid s] = \frac{\tau}{\tau+\tau_e}\mu + \frac{\tau_e}{\tau+\tau_e}s$.

That slope is the coefficient of the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of the value on the signal, so the Bayes updating coefficient is a regression coefficient and the posterior mean is the regression's prediction.

The same weight is the squared correlation. For $s = v + e$ with $e$ independent of $v$, $\rho^2 = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s,v)^2}{\operatorname{Var}(s)\operatorname{Var}(v)} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + \sigma_e^2}$, so the weight on the signal is the share of the signal's variance that is the value, and it is the regression's $R^2$. A signal that is half noise has half its variance in $v$ and takes weight $\frac{1}{2}$; a signal with no noise has all of it and takes weight $1$.

The same weight can be read as advice. A trader who sees $s$ and knows the noise should move by the variance share the signal carries: with $\rho^2 = \frac{1}{2}$ half of every surprise is the value and half is noise, so the belief adopts half the surprise and lets the rest pass. Moving by the whole surprise treats the noise as news, and moving by less throws away part of the signal. The slope is $\rho^2$ and not $\rho$ because a weight is a share of variance, so a signal correlating $0.6$ with the value earns weight $0.36$, and a signal needs a correlation near $0.71$ to earn half.
