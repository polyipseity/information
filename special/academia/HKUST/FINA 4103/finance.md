# finance

Finance is a tool for working out what a participant should and can do in markets. As a field of research it investigates financial assets, the financial system, and the participants who trade in them.

## financial asset

A _financial asset_ is a contract that governs when and how resources are distributed across time and states. People and firms want resources at different points in time, such as today and tomorrow, and in different states of the world, such as a boom and a recession. A contract that moves resources along those two dimensions therefore carries value. Its familiar forms fall into three groups. Bonds, stocks, and derivatives are claims the holder buys and sells in a market. Bank deposits, certificates of deposit, and loans and receivables are claims the holder keeps with a bank or another lender. Cash or cash equivalents are money or near-money the holder keeps in hand.

## branches of finance

Research in finance splits into three branches by the question each asks. _Asset pricing_ asks what the fair price of an asset is, and the Black–Scholes–Merton formula for option prices is its canonical result. _Corporate finance_ asks how a firm's value and its decision-making are determined, and the Modigliani–Miller theorem on capital structure is its canonical result. _Market microstructure_ asks how the details of trading shape market quality.

## analytical perspectives

The _positive_ perspective asks what is happening, mostly about the financial system and its participants, and answers by working out who is playing what kind of game from data. The _equilibrium_ perspective asks how to play and what results: given the environment, what should an individual do, and what happens to the market when individuals behave optimally? The _normative_ perspective asks how to design and regulate: what should a government or a market designer do, typically to maximise social welfare?

## theoretical and empirical analysis

A policy question such as what would happen if the SEC imposed a short-selling ban is answered in two ways. _Theoretical_ analysis develops an economic model, solves it, and derives testable implications; it typically keeps a couple of important factors and omits complicated detail, and the result is a guideline for implementing the policy. _Empirical_ analysis brings data to test those implications and to find anomalies and new questions, for example to measure the actual impact of the policy once it is in place.
