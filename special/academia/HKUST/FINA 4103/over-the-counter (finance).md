# over-the-counter (finance)

An _over-the-counter_ market is decentralized trading: a trade is struck directly between two parties instead of through an exchange, so the dealer-customer relation and the inter-dealer relation replace a central venue. The two parties may agree on the trade over the telephone, by email, or through a proprietary electronic trading system, and because the trade is private the asset has no public price. _Decentralized_ carries a second, unrelated meaning in finance, where blockchain-based trading is also called decentralized.

- why an over-the-counter asset has no public price ::@:: The trade is private to the two parties, so nothing publishes a price for the asset.

## what over-the-counter markets trade

Over-the-counter markets trade non-standard securities that few investors want to hold, which makes each one illiquid: mortgages on housing, tranches of collateralized debt obligations, and synthetic derivative securities. The same arrangement lets a contract be custom-tailored, and it also carries assets as ordinary as foreign exchange and government and corporate bonds. A breakdown of inter-dealer broker revenue for 2019 puts currencies and rates at 43%, securities and security derivatives at 32%, commodities at 21%, and non-IDB online retail foreign-exchange brokerage at 4%.

## dealer-customer markets

In the dealer-customer market, clients ask dealers, usually banks, for the availability and price of a specific security. Traditionally the request went over the telephone, by email, or face to face, and from the 2000s clients reached several dealers at once through an electronic request-for-quote platform. A request for quote is raised on an existing order: the trader picks Request for Quote and submits it, and a request window opens carrying the symbol and venue, a description such as an option on a stock, and the quantity asked for, for example 100 contracts.

## inter-dealer markets

Dealers trade with each other as well, above all to unwind positions that are off their target. Because both sides are dealers rather than clients, the inter-dealer market is more stylized and more liquid than the dealer-customer market, and it looks more like a centralized limit-order book. Quotes there are made, requested, and executed through inter-dealer brokers, through request for quote, or through electronic communication networks. The structure links clients to dealers and dealers to dealers, with the inter-dealer brokers sitting between the dealers.

## electronic communication networks

An electronic communication network connects multiple market participants directly: each participant can post and observe quotes like a limit-order book, place and execute orders, and stay anonymous. Foreign exchange shows what the network changes. Before the 2000s the market was plain-vanilla over the counter; it now runs through two major electronic brokers for inter-dealer spot trading, Reuters, later Refinitiv, and Electronic Broking Services, which organize limit-order books that dealers use to submit quotes, to trade, and to take reference prices. Customer access is wider and more opaque: more than 30 secondary electronic communication networks, more than 20 dealers running a single-dealer proprietary platform, principal trading firms, and a few dark pools, where order information is not revealed to other participants.

## risks and costs

Several mechanisms coexist inside one over-the-counter market, limit-order books among them, and they differ in the price and execution quality they deliver, so the quality of the market as a whole is often opaque. Against a centralized limit-order book the open questions are transparency and the mechanism by which an order is executed. What a trader expects to incur by trading over the counter is usually split into an ex-ante part and an ex-post part. The costs that are clear are the search-and-matching friction of finding a counterparty and the counterparty risk of a bilateral contract.

## central clearing

Synthetic derivatives and securitized assets traded over the counter contributed to the global financial crisis of the late 2000s, and the counterparty risk they carried was large but ambiguous, or obscured. Major countries responded by introducing a central counterparty, following the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision framework of 2013. A central counterparty stands between the two sides of a trade, takes inventory and margin as an intermediary, and limits the impact of a default by a single player. Central clearing has not been applied everywhere: it covers interest-rate swaps and credit default swaps.

## fragmentation of the venues

Volume on the primary foreign-exchange venues has been falling, and it moves with volatility rather than with the futures market: over 2010 to 2021 a scatter of primary-venue volume against foreign-exchange volatility has an R-squared of 0.6, while turnover in exchange-traded currency futures has not fallen the same way. Candidate explanations follow from what the inter-dealer market is for, which is unwinding positions that are off target: the number of dealers, the other systems that now offer the same service, and market volatility. Recent technology has fragmented the market further, because limit-order-book-like mechanisms and secondary electronic communication networks now compete with the primary venues, and with no data covering all the venues, the impact is hard to measure.
