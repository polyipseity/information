# over-the-counter (finance)

An _over-the-counter_ market is decentralized trading. A trade is struck directly between two parties instead of through an exchange. The dealer-customer relation and the inter-dealer relation therefore replace a central venue. The two parties may agree over the telephone, by email, or through a proprietary electronic trading system. Because the trade is private, the asset has no public price. _Decentralized_ carries a second, unrelated meaning in finance, where blockchain-based trading is also called decentralized.

## traded assets

These markets trade non-standard securities that few investors want to hold. Each one is therefore illiquid. Examples are mortgages on housing, tranches of collateralized debt obligations, and synthetic derivative securities. The same arrangement lets a contract be tailored. It also carries ordinary assets: foreign exchange, and government and corporate bonds. Tradition, an inter-dealer broker, reported 2019 revenue by product group. Currencies and rates took 43%, securities and security derivatives 32%, commodities 21%, and non-IDB online retail foreign-exchange brokerage 4%.

## venue types

### dealer-customer markets

In the dealer-customer market, clients ask dealers, usually banks, for the availability and price of a specific security. The request went over the telephone, by email, or face to face. From the 2000s clients reached several dealers at once through an electronic request-for-quote platform. A request for quote is raised on an existing order. The trader picks Request for Quote and submits it. A request window then opens. It carries the symbol and venue, a description such as a stock option, and the quantity asked for, for example 100 contracts.

- how clients reached dealers before the 2000s ::@:: Over the telephone, by email, or face to face.
- what a request-for-quote platform does ::@:: It lets a client contact several dealers at once and learn the terms of trade.
- what a request for quote carries ::@:: The symbol and venue, a description of the instrument, and the quantity asked for.

### inter-dealer markets

Dealers trade with each other as well, above all to unwind positions that are off their target. Both sides are dealers rather than clients, so the inter-dealer market is more stylized and more liquid than the dealer-customer market. It looks more like a centralized limit-order book. Quotes there are made, requested, and executed through inter-dealer brokers, through request for quote, or through electronic communication networks. The structure links clients to dealers, and dealers to dealers. The inter-dealer brokers sit between the dealers.

### electronic communication networks

An electronic communication network connects multiple market participants directly. Each can post and observe quotes like a limit-order book, place and execute orders, and stay anonymous. Foreign exchange shows what the network changes. Before the 2000s the market was plain-vanilla over the counter. It now runs through two major electronic brokers for inter-dealer spot trading: Reuters, later Refinitiv, and Electronic Broking Services. They organize limit-order books that dealers use to quote, trade, and take reference prices. Customer access is wider and more opaque. The venue count runs to roughly 30 secondary electronic communication networks. It also counts 22 dealers running a single-dealer proprietary platform, 12 principal trading firms, and 2 or 3 dark pools. A dark pool does not reveal order information to other participants.

## risks and costs

Several mechanisms coexist inside one such market, limit-order books among them. They differ in the price and execution quality they deliver. The quality of the market as a whole is therefore often opaque. Against a centralized limit-order book, the open questions are transparency and the mechanism by which an order is executed. A trader's expected cost of trading over the counter splits into an ex-ante part and an ex-post part. The costs that are clear are the search-and-matching friction of finding a counterparty and the counterparty risk of a bilateral contract.

## central clearing

Synthetic derivatives and securitized assets traded over the counter contributed to the global financial crisis of the late 2000s. The counterparty risk they carried was large but ambiguous, or obscured. Major countries responded with a central counterparty, following the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision framework of 2013. A central counterparty stands between the two sides of a trade. It takes inventory and margin as an intermediary. That limits the impact of a default by a single player. Central clearing has not been applied everywhere: it covers interest-rate swaps and credit default swaps.

- framework under which major countries introduced central counterparties ::@:: The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision framework of 2013.

## fragmentation of the venues

Volume on the primary foreign-exchange venues has been falling. It moves with volatility rather than with the futures market. Over 2010 to 2021 a scatter of primary-venue volume against foreign-exchange volatility has an R-squared of 0.6. Turnover in exchange-traded currency futures has not fallen the same way. The inter-dealer market exists to unwind positions that are off target. Candidate explanations follow from that: the number of dealers, the other systems that now offer the same service, and market volatility. Recent technology fragmented the market further. Limit-order-book-like mechanisms and secondary electronic communication networks now compete with the primary venues. With no data covering all the venues, the impact is hard to measure.
