# financial market participants

Market participants are those who get involved in financial transactions in the [financial system](financial%20system.md). Traders in financial markets are broadly classified into several groups: intermediaries, investors, speculators and arbitrageurs, hedgers, producers, and government, or public agencies. The population is highly heterogeneous, and many constraints limit what each participant may do.

- the groups traders in financial markets are broadly classified into ::@:: Intermediaries, investors, speculators and arbitrageurs, hedgers, producers, and government, or public agencies.

## intermediaries

Intermediaries help match up buyers and sellers. Banks and large financial institutions take part as brokers and dealers, and it is common to play both roles at once: broker-dealers, such as Charles Schwab and Fidelity Investment. The two roles put the risk in different places. A broker finds the other side and keeps no position, so the client carries the price risk and the broker is paid a fee. A dealer is the other side, so it carries whatever the trade leaves on its books until it can unwind it.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what intermediaries do in financial markets ::@:: They help match up buyers and sellers.
- how banks and large financial institutions take part as market participants ::@:: As brokers and dealers, and it is common to play both roles at once as broker-dealers, such as Charles Schwab and Fidelity Investment.
- who carries the price risk in an intermediated trade, broker or dealer ::@:: The dealer, which takes the other side out of its own inventory; a broker only finds the other side and keeps no position.

### brokers

A broker does not take a position of its own. It is just a match-maker: it earns fees from the trade, and research and advice are part of what it offers. The client keeps the price and the risk, so the broker is paid for finding the counterparty rather than for being right about the market.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- whether a broker takes a position of its own ::@:: No. A broker is just a match-maker.
- how a broker earns from a trade ::@:: Fees from the trade, with research and advice also part of what it offers.
- why a broker's income does not depend on the direction of the market ::@:: It is paid a fee for matching and takes no position, so the price risk stays with the client.

### dealers

A dealer takes its own positions with inventory and trades with buyers and sellers. It typically tries to unwind its position on a daily basis, and it earns the [bid-ask spread](bid-ask%20spread.md) along with make and take fees. The position is the risk: a dealer that buys from a seller has to find a buyer, and until it does, a price move works against the inventory it holds. A daily unwind keeps that exposure short, and the spread charged on the way in and the way out pays for carrying it in the meantime.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what a dealer does with inventory ::@:: It takes its own positions and trades with buyers and sellers.
- what a dealer typically tries to do with its position ::@:: Unwind it, on a daily basis.
- how a dealer earns from trading ::@:: The bid-ask spread, along with make and take fees.
- why a dealer unwinds its position on a daily basis ::@:: The inventory is exposed to price moves until the other side appears, so unwinding quickly shortens the time the risk is carried, and the spread pays for carrying it.

## market making

The [market maker](market%20maker.md) is the more general term for a liquidity provider, and dealers can be categorized as market makers. A market maker makes money by quoting bid and ask and by collecting make fees (rebates), and it loses money when the market moves against its position or when an informed or high-speed speculator trades against it. In the broad sense, anyone who places a [limit order](limit%20order.md) that is not immediately executed is making market, that is, providing liquidity. In typical usage, though, the term refers to a more specialized financial institution, or a service, obligated to quote.

Market making is a service, and the spread is what pays for it. The maker stands ready to trade when nobody else will, and the trader who takes the quote buys that readiness. The trader taking a quote consumes liquidity, while the trader resting a limit order provides it, and the two meet on the same book. The maker's losses to an informed or fast counterparty are the cost of the service, which is why a market with more informed flow needs a wider quote.

- when a market maker loses money ::@:: When the market moves against its position, or when an informed or high-speed speculator trades against it.

- what the spread pays a market maker for ::@:: Standing ready to trade when nobody else will, which is what the trader taking the quote buys.

## investors

Investors put money for long-run growth, in contrast to speculators, or day traders. Investors come in two kinds: retail investors and institutional investors.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- how investors differ from speculators ::@:: Investors put money for long-run growth; speculators, or day traders, do not.
- the two kinds of investors ::@:: Retail investors and institutional investors.

### retail investors

Retail investors are ordinary individuals, you and me, and households trading on their own account. They have no direct access to the market and trade via brokers.

---

- who the retail investors are ::@:: Ordinary individuals, you and me, and households trading on their own account.
- how retail investors reach the market ::@:: They have no direct access to the market and trade via brokers.

### institutional investors

Institutional investors are large banks and funds, and private equity. They are constrained in many ways: they only hold "vetted" securities, such as investment-grade bonds and index constituents. The constraint triggers some liquidity events: increased trading activity and potential liquidity challenges around specific time frames. For a firm, inclusion in or exclusion from the "vetted" set has a major impact: inclusion in an index alters the investor base, the index effect. Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) are evaluated at month-end or quarter-end returns with periodic reporting, so a surge in redemptions or subscriptions potentially impacts the fund's portfolio and the underlying markets.

---

## speculators and arbitrageurs

Speculators and arbitrageurs trade for profits only, with no material interests in the securities per se. Their duration varies depending on the trading strategies, from milliseconds to years. What separates a speculator from an arbitrageur turns on the definition of arbitrage.

A speculator takes a view on where the price will go, and it consumes liquidity when it takes a quote and provides it when it rests an order. An arbitrageur trades a price difference between two markets or two instruments: it buys where the price is low and sells where it is high, and the two trades push the prices together until the gap no longer covers the cost of trading. The gap was there to be taken, and taking it is the reason it will not last.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- what speculators and arbitrageurs trade for ::@:: Profits only; they have no material interests in the securities per se.
- the range of holding durations among speculators and arbitrageurs ::@:: From milliseconds to years, depending on the trading strategies.
- what separates a speculator from an arbitrageur ::@:: The definition of arbitrage.
- how a speculator provides and consumes liquidity ::@:: It consumes it when it takes a standing quote and provides it when it rests a limit order.
- what an arbitrageur does to a price difference ::@:: Buys where the price is low and sells where it is high, pushing the two prices together until the gap no longer covers the cost of trading, so the profit is the gap closing.

## hedgers

Hedgers trade securities to offload risk that emanates from other positions. A utility company carries the risk of oil and natural gas prices, a retailer or restaurant the risk of food and raw materials prices, and banks interest rate risk and exchange rate risk. Hedgers do not trade with a fundamental expectation in mind, and they are not price sensitive. Their duration is typically long, though it depends on the duration of the hedged position. In theory, hedgers are referred to as "noise" traders.

A hedger's reason to trade sits somewhere else, so its order says nothing about what the hedger thinks the asset is worth. That is what makes it noise: the market maker cannot learn the value from the flow, and it is the hedger's willingness to pay that recovers the maker's losses to informed traders. A hedger is insensitive to price because removing the risk matters more than the level paid to remove it.

---

- what hedgers trade securities for ::@:: To offload risk that emanates from other positions.
- the risks hedgers offload, by participant ::@:: A utility company: oil and natural gas price risk. A retailer or restaurant: food and raw materials price risk. Banks: interest rate risk and exchange rate risk.
- how hedgers trade relative to fundamentals and price ::@:: They do not trade with a fundamental expectation in mind, and they are not price sensitive.
- the typical duration of hedger trades ::@:: Typically long, though it depends on the duration of the hedged position.
- what hedgers are referred to as in theory ::@:: Noise traders.
- why theory treats hedgers as noise traders ::@:: Their reason to trade is a risk held elsewhere, so their orders carry no information about the asset's value and the market maker learns nothing from them.
- why a hedger is not price sensitive ::@:: Removing the risk matters more than the price paid to remove it, so the hedger trades even when the quote goes against it.

## producers

Producers sell products and goods, and they come in many types: farmers, mine operators, well operators, and listed firms. They differ from hedgers because they actually have products or production rights. They can affect the production amount and, to a lesser extent, the price, and they have private information. The supply of actual assets is taken as given.

A producer's order carries information, unlike a hedger's, because the producer knows its own output and costs, so a mine selling forward is trading on what it knows about supply. The supply of the asset is fixed in the analysis, which leaves the question of what a producer does against that quantity rather than how the quantity comes to exist.

---

Flashcards for this section are as follows:

- who the producers are ::@:: Many types of sellers of products and goods: farmers, mine operators, well operators, and listed firms.
- how producers differ from hedgers ::@:: They actually have products or production rights.
- what producers can affect, and what else they have ::@:: The production amount, and the price to a lesser extent; they also have private information.
- how the supply of actual assets is treated ::@:: As given.
- why a producer's order carries information that a hedger's does not ::@:: It has products or production rights and private information about its own production, so its trade says something about supply while a hedger's says nothing about value.

## government

Governments issue and trade, and their trading generates major liquidity and price events. Monetary policy is the standard example: the Federal Reserve (Fed) and central banks in major countries use open market operations (OMO) and quantitative easing (QE), and Singapore and China have government investment companies. Analysis typically assumes that government behavior as a trader is given, while normative analyses take the government's point of view.

A central bank buying bonds is setting policy, not forecasting the bonds' value, so its trades move prices for reasons no other participant can read as information. That is why the behavior is taken as given: it is an input to the market, and a normative study instead asks what that input should be.

---

- the monetary-policy tools central banks use as traders ::@:: Open market operations (OMO) and quantitative easing (QE), used by the Federal Reserve (Fed) and central banks in major countries.
- government investment companies cited as market participants ::@:: Those of Singapore and China.
- how government behavior as a trader is treated in analysis ::@:: Typically assumed to be given, while normative analyses take the government's point of view.
- why government trading is taken as given rather than derived ::@:: Its trades follow policy rather than information about value and are large enough to move prices, so the behavior is an input to the market and a normative study asks what that input should be.
