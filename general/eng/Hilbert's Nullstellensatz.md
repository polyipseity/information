# Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

In {@{[mathematics](mathematics.md)}@}, {@{__Hilbert's Nullstellensatz__}@} \(German for {@{"theorem of zeros" or, more literally, "zero-locus-theorem"}@}\) is {@{a theorem that establishes a fundamental relationship between [geometry](geometry.md) and [algebra](algebra.md)}@}. It was proven by {@{[David Hilbert](David%20Hilbert.md) in his second major paper on [invariant theory](invariant%20theory.md) in 1893}@} \(following his {@{seminal 1890 paper in which he proved [Hilbert's basis theorem](Hilbert's%20basis%20theorem.md)}@}\) and became {@{a foundational result of [algebraic geometry](algebraic%20geometry.md)}@}.

There are several {@{formulations of the Nullstellensatz}@}, {@{the most elementary of which}@} deal with {@{conditions for the existence of solutions to systems of multivariate polynomial equations over an algebraically closed field}@} \(such as {@{the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$}@}\). {@{The __weak Nullstellensatz__}@} is {@{a [corollary](corollary.md) \(or a [lemma](lemma%20(mathematics).md), depending which is proved first\) of the Nullstellensatz}@} which can be stated as follows.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup> Consider {@{a [system of polynomial equations](system%20of%20polynomial%20equations.md)}@} {@{$$\begin{cases} f_1(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0 \\ \vdots \\ f_m(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = 0 \end{cases}$$}@} in {@{the variables $x_1, \dots, x_n\in K$}@}, where {@{$f_1, \dots, f_m \in K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@} are {@{[multivariate polynomials](multivariate%20polynomial.md#multivariate%20polynomial) over an [algebraically closed field](algebraically%20closed%20field.md) $K$}@}. If the system {@{does _not_ have a solution $(a_1, \dots, a_n) \in K^n$}@}, then there is {@{an "algebraic reason" for this situation}@}: namely, this occurs {@{precisely when there exist polynomials $g_1, \dots, g_m \in K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ such that $$g_1 f_1 + \cdots + g_m f_m = 1.$$}@} Since {@{the expression on the left-hand side}@} must {@{evaluate to 0 at any $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ that solves the system of equations}@}, it is obvious from {@{the inconsistency that no solution can exist if this condition holds}@}. Informally, {@{the \(weak\) Nullstellensatz}@} asserts that in {@{the absence of such an inconsistency, a solution to the system of equations must exist}@}.

{@{The full Nullstellensatz}@} is {@{the following refinement}@}: If {@{$f\in K[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is a polynomial}@} such that {@{every solution of the system of equations is also a solution of $$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0,$$}@} then there is {@{a similar type of "algebraic reason" for this occurrence}@}: this occurs {@{precisely when there exist a natural number $r$ and polynomials $g_1, \dots, g_m$}@} such that {@{$$f^r = g_1 f_1 + \cdots + g_m f_m.$$}@}

## formulations

Let _k_ be {@{a [field](field%20(mathematics).md) \(such as the [rational numbers](rational%20number.md)\)}@} and _K_ be {@{an algebraically closed [field extension](field%20extension.md) of _k_ \(such as the [complex numbers](complex%20number.md)\)}@}. Consider {@{the [polynomial ring](polynomial%20ring.md) $k[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$}@} and let $J$ be {@{an [ideal](ideal%20(ring%20theory).md) in this ring}@}. (annotation: {@{An ideal in this context}@} is {@{a self-contained set of polynomials that absorbs products when multiplied by _any_ ring element}@}, behaving precisely like {@{a set of standard numerical multiples}@}. For example, {@{the ideal $(x^2)$ in $\mathbb{R}[x]$}@} collects {@{every polynomial divisible by $x^2$}@}, exactly mirroring how {@{the ideal $(2)$ in the integers}@} collects {@{all even numbers}@}.) {@{The [algebraic set](algebraic%20set.md) $\mathrm V(J)$ defined by this ideal}@} consists of {@{all $n$-tuples $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ in $K^n$ such that $f(a) = 0$ for all $f\in J$}@}. (annotation: It is {@{the geometric footprint of an ideal}@}, collecting every {@{coordinate point where all its polynomials simultaneously vanish}@}. For example, in {@{$\mathbb{R}^2$, the algebraic set defined by the ideal $(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$}@} is precisely {@{the unit circle where that equation equals zero}@}.) {@{Hilbert's Nullstellensatz}@} states that if $p$ is {@{a polynomial in $k[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ that vanishes on the algebraic set $\mathrm V(J)$}@}, i.e., {@{$p(a) = 0$ for all $a \in \mathrm V(J)$}@}, then there {@{exists a [natural number](natural%20number.md) $r$ such that $p^r\in J$}@}.<sup>[\[2\]](#^ref-2)</sup> (annotation: If a polynomial {@{vanishes across a shape's zero set}@}, some {@{power of it must belong to the shape's defining ideal}@}. For example, while {@{$p(x) = x$ vanishes at the origin}@} where {@{$J = (x^2)$ without being in $J$ itself}@}, its {@{square $x^2$ is}@}.)

With {@{the notation common in algebraic geometry}@}, {@{the Nullstellensatz}@} can be formulated as {@{$$\hbox{I}(\hbox{V}(J))=\sqrt{J}$$}@} for every {@{ideal $J$ in $K[X_1,...,X_n]$ with $K$ algebraically closed}@}. Here, {@{$\sqrt{J}$}@} denotes {@{the [_radical_](radical%20of%20an%20ideal.md) of $J$}@} \($p\in\sqrt{J}$ {@{[if and only if](if%20and%20only%20if.md) $\exists r\in\mathbb{N},\ p^r \in J$}@}\), {@{$\mathrm{I} (U)$}@} is {@{the _vanishing ideal_ of $U$}@} \(the set of {@{polynomials that vanish at the points in $U$}@}\), and {@{$\mathrm{V} (J)$}@} is {@{the _zero locus_ of $J$}@} \(the set of {@{points at which the polynomials in $J$ vanish}@}\). {@{The assertion that $\hbox{I}(\hbox{V}(J))\subseteq\sqrt{J}$}@} is equivalent to {@{the first formulation above with $k = K$ algebraically closed}@}, while {@{the opposite inclusion}@} is {@{a straightforward consequence of the definitions}@}.

{@{An immediate corollary}@} is {@{the __weak Nullstellensatz__}@}: If $J$ is {@{a proper ideal in $k[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$}@}, then {@{$\mathrm{V} (J)$ is nonempty}@}, i.e., for every {@{algebraically closed extension $K\supseteq k$}@}, there {@{exists a common zero in $K^n$ for all the polynomials in the ideal $J$}@}.<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup> (annotation: Let $J$ be {@{a proper ideal in $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@}, so {@{$1 \notin J$}@}. Extending {@{scalars to an algebraically closed field $K \supseteq k$}@} generates {@{the extended ideal $J_e = J \cdot K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@}. Because $J_e$ consists of {@{finite $K$-linear combinations of elements from $J$}@}, if {@{$1 \in J_e$}@}, we could write {@{$1 = \sum_j c_j f_j$ for some $c_j \in K$ and $f_j \in J$}@}; choosing {@{a $k$-basis $\{e_\alpha\}$ of $K$ that includes $e_0 = 1$}@} and expanding {@{each coefficient as $c_j = \sum_\alpha a_{j,\alpha} e_\alpha$ with $a_{j,\alpha} \in k$}@} gives {@{$1 \cdot e_0 = \sum_\alpha \left(\sum_j a_{j,\alpha} f_j\right) e_\alpha$}@}, so matching {@{the $e_0$ component yields $1 = \sum_j a_{j,0} f_j \in J$}@}, contradicting that {@{$J$ is proper}@}. Applying {@{the strong Nullstellensatz $\mathrm{I}(\mathrm{V}(J_e)) = \sqrt{J_e}$ in $K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@} gives {@{$\mathrm{I}(\mathrm{V}(J_e)) \neq K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@}. Since {@{$\mathrm{I}(\emptyset) = K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@}, it follows that {@{$\mathrm{V}(J) = \mathrm{V}(J_e) \neq \emptyset$ in $K^n$}@}.) This is {@{the reason for the name of the theorem}@}, {@{the full version of which}@} can be {@{proved easily from the 'weak' form using the [Rabinowitsch trick](Rabinowitsch%20trick.md)}@}. The assumption of {@{considering common zeros in an algebraically closed field}@} is essential here; for example, {@{the elements of the proper ideal $(X^2+1)$ in $\R[X]$}@} do not {@{have a common zero in $\R$}@}.

Specializing to the case of {@{a single polynomial when $K=\mathbb{C}$ and $n=1$}@}, one immediately recovers {@{a restatement of the [fundamental theorem of algebra](fundamental%20theorem%20of%20algebra.md)}@}: {@{A polynomial $J$ in $\mathbb{C}[X]$}@} has {@{a root in $\mathbb{C}$ if and only if $\deg P\neq 0$}@}. For this reason, {@{the \(weak\) Nullstellensatz applied to $K=\mathbb{C}$}@} can be thought of as {@{a generalization of the fundamental theorem of algebra to systems of multivariable polynomial equations}@}.<sup>[\[4\]](#^ref-4)</sup>

Taking _K_ to be {@{algebraically closed}@}, {@{the Nullstellensatz}@} establishes {@{an order-reversing [bijective](bijective.md) correspondence between the algebraic sets in $K^n$ and the [radical ideals](radical%20ideal.md) of $K[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$}@}. (annotation: Over {@{an algebraically closed field $K$}@}, {@{the Nullstellensatz}@} establishes {@{a bijective correspondence between algebraic sets in $K^n$ and radical ideals in $K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@}. This correspondence is {@{order-reversing}@}: {@{a larger ideal $J_1 \subseteq J_2$}@} imposes {@{stricter constraints, shrinking the zero locus $V(J_2) \subseteq V(J_1)$}@}, whereas {@{a larger solution set $U_1 \subseteq U_2$}@} admits {@{fewer vanishing polynomials, yielding $I(U_2) \subseteq I(U_1)$}@}.) In fact, more generally, one has {@{a [Galois connection](Galois%20connection.md) between subsets of the space and subsets of the algebra}@}, where {@{"[Zariski closure](Zariski%20closure.md)" and "radical of the ideal generated"}@} are {@{the [closure operators](closure%20operator.md)}@}. (annotation: For {@{arbitrary subsets $S \subseteq K^n$ and $J \subseteq K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@} that {@{need not be algebraic sets or ideals}@}, {@{the zero locus map $V$ and vanishing ideal map $I$}@} form {@{a Galois connection whose closure operators}@} send any {@{spatial subset $S$ to its Zariski closure $\overline{S} = V(I(S))$}@} and any {@{polynomial subset $J$ to the radical of its generated ideal $\sqrt{\langle J \rangle}$}@}. Restricting {@{this Galois connection to closed sets and radical ideals}@} yields {@{the order-reversing bijection}@}, as these {@{sets and ideals are precisely fixed under their respective closure operators}@}.)

As {@{a particular example}@}, consider {@{an algebraic set consisting of a single point}@} {@{$a = (a_1, \dots, a_n) \in K^n$}@}. Then {@{$\mathrm{I} (\{a\}) = (X_1 - a_1, \ldots, X_n - a_n)$}@} is {@{a maximal ideal}@}. (annotation: Consider {@{the evaluation homomorphism $\operatorname{ev}_a \colon K[X_1, \dots, X_n] \to K$}@} defined by {@{$f \mapsto f(a)$}@}. {@{This map is surjective}@} because every {@{constant scalar $c \in K$ is the image of the constant polynomial $f = c$}@}, and its {@{kernel is the ideal $(X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@}. By {@{the first isomorphism theorem}@}, {@{the quotient ring $K[X_1, \dots, X_n] / (X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@} is {@{isomorphic to the field $K$}@}. By {@{the correspondence theorem}@}, {@{ideals of a quotient ring $R/I$}@} correspond {@{bijectively to ideals of $R$ containing $I$}@}. For example, in {@{the ring $\mathbb{Z}$}@}, ideals containing {@{the principal ideal $(n)$ for an integer $n$}@} correspond {@{bijectively to ideals in the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$}@}. Applying {@{this structural correspondence}@}, any {@{ideal containing $(X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@} maps {@{bijectively to an ideal in the quotient field $K$}@}. A field {@{contains no proper non-trivial ideals}@}, since any {@{ideal containing a non-zero element $x$ must contain $x^{-1}x = 1$}@}, forcing {@{the ideal to equal the entire ring—no intermediate ideals can exist}@}. {@{No strictly larger proper ideal}@} can {@{contain $(X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@}, thereby {@{proving the ideal is maximal}@}.) Conversely, every {@{[maximal ideal](maximal%20ideal.md) of the polynomial ring $K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$}@} \(note that $K$ is {@{algebraically closed}@}\) is of {@{the form $(X_1 - a_1, \ldots, X_n - a_n)$ for some $a_1,\ldots,a_n \in K$}@}. (annotation: Conversely, let {@{$\mathfrak{m} \subset K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@} be {@{a maximal ideal over an algebraically closed field $K$}@}. {@{The quotient ring $L = K[X_1, \dots, X_n] / \mathfrak{m}$}@} forms {@{a field, which is also a ring}@}. By {@{Zariski's Lemma}@}, any {@{field finitely generated as an algebra over a field}@} is {@{itself a field extension algebraic over it}@}. so $L$ is {@{a finite algebraic extension of $K$}@}. Because $K$ is {@{algebraically closed, $L$ is isomorphic to $K$}@}. This yields {@{a surjective composite ring homomorphism $\phi \colon K[X_1, \dots, X_n] \to L \cong K$}@} formed by composing {@{the canonical quotient projection with the field isomorphism}@}, acting as {@{the identity on all base field scalars}@}. Because {@{$\phi$ is a ring homomorphism}@}, it {@{strictly preserves addition, multiplication, and scalar constants}@}, allowing any {@{polynomial expression $f(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ to be evaluated by distributing through sums and products}@} to give {@{$\phi(f(X_1, \dots, X_n)) = f(\phi(X_1), \dots, \phi(X_n))$}@}. Setting {@{$a_i = \phi(X_i) \in K$ for each coordinate}@}, this reduces to {@{$f(a_1, \dots, a_n)$}@}, which directly {@{links coordinate evaluation to polynomial evaluation}@}. Consequently, a polynomial belongs to {@{the kernel of $\phi$, which is the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$}@}, {@{if and only if it evaluates to zero at the point $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n) \in K^n$}@}, proving that {@{$\mathfrak{m}$ is precisely of the form $(X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@}.) This {@{characterization of maximal ideals of polynomial rings over algebraically closed fields}@} is {@{another common formulation of the weak Nullstellensatz}@}. As another example of {@{this correspondence and a consequence of the Nullstellensatz}@}, one can show that {@{an algebraic subset $W$ in _$K^n$_}@} is {@{[irreducible](irreducible%20space.md) \(in the Zariski topology\)}@} {@{if and only if $\mathrm{I} (W)$ is a [prime ideal](prime%20ideal.md)}@}. (annotation: Just as {@{a maximal ideal}@} is {@{defined algebraically by its quotient ring being a field}@}, {@{a prime ideal}@} is {@{defined by its quotient ring being an integral domain}@}, meaning {@{$ab \in \mathfrak{p}$ implies $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ or $b \in \mathfrak{p}$}@}. In {@{the polynomial ring $K[X, Y]$}@}, {@{the ideal $(X)$}@} is {@{prime because the quotient $K[X, Y]/(X)$ is isomorphic to the integral domain $K[Y]$}@}, but it is not {@{maximal because $K[Y]$ is not a field}@}, allowing {@{the strictly larger proper ideal $(X, Y)$}@}. By contrast, {@{the ideal $(X, Y)$ is maximal}@} because its {@{quotient $K[X, Y]/(X, Y)$ is isomorphic to the field $K$}@}, leaving {@{no room for intermediate proper ideals}@}. Under {@{the order-reversing bijection}@}, {@{this algebraic hierarchy}@} mirrors {@{the geometric correspondence}@} where {@{a maximal ideal defines a single point}@}, whereas {@{a prime ideal corresponds to an irreducible algebraic subset $W$ in $K^n$}@}.)

More generally, for any {@{ideal $J$ in $K[X_1,...,X_n]$}@}, {@{$$\sqrt{J} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}\supseteq J} \mathfrak{m} = \bigcap_{(a_1,\dots, a_n) \in \mathrm{V} (J)} (X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n),$$}@} where the first intersection is taken over {@{maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m} \subsetneq K[X_1,...,X_n]$}@}. (annotation: Substituting each {@{maximal ideal with its point-ideal form $(X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@}, {@{the intersection $J = \bigcap_{(a_1, \dots, a_n) \in V(J)} (X_1 - a_1, \dots, X_n - a_n)$}@} ranges over {@{all points in the zero locus $V(J)$}@}. {@{Intersecting over more points}@} enforces {@{stricter simultaneous vanishing constraints}@}, yielding {@{an ideal of polynomials vanishing across every solution}@}. For example, in {@{$K[X]$, the ideal $J = (X^2 - X)$}@} has {@{zero locus $V(J) = \{0, 1\}$}@}, and its radical equals {@{the intersection of the maximal point ideals $(X)$ and $(X - 1)$}@}, capturing all {@{polynomials vanishing at both points}@}.) This relationship is {@{yet another common formulation of the Nullstellensatz}@}. \({@{The first equality}@} actually holds for {@{ideals in any [Jacobson ring](Jacobson%20ring.md)}@}, including any {@{finitely generated algebra over a field}@}, while {@{the second equality holds for algebraically closed _K_}@}.\)

## proofs

There are many {@{known proofs of the theorem}@}. Some are {@{[non-constructive](constructive%20proof.md)}@}, such as {@{the first one (annotation: using Zariski's lemma)}@}. Others are {@{constructive}@}, as based on {@{[algorithms](algorithm.md) for expressing 1 or _p<sup>r</sup>_}@} as {@{a [linear combination](linear%20combination.md) of the generators of the ideal}@}.

### using Zariski's lemma

{@{[Zariski's lemma](Zariski's%20lemma.md)}@} asserts that if {@{a field is [finitely generated](finitely%20generated%20algebra.md) as an [associative algebra](associative%20algebra.md) over a field _K_}@}, then it is {@{a [finite field extension](finite%20field%20extension.md#definition%20and%20notation) of _K_}@} \(that is, it is also {@{finitely generated as a [vector space](vector%20space.md)}@}\). If $K$ is {@{an algebraically closed field}@} and $\mathfrak{m}$ is {@{a maximal ideal of the ring of polynomials $K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$}@}, (annotation: {@{The polynomial ring $K[X_1, \dots, X_n]$}@} is {@{a finitely generated associative $K$-algebra}@} whose quotient by {@{a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ preserves the algebra structure and yields a field}@}.) then Zariski's lemma implies that {@{$K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]/ \mathfrak{m}$ is a finite field extension of $K$}@}, and thus, by {@{algebraic closure, must be _K_}@}. From this, it follows that there is {@{an $a = (a_1,\dots,a_n)\in K^n$ such that $X_i-a_i\in\mathfrak{m}$ for $i=1,\dots, n$}@}. In other words, {@{$$\mathfrak{m} \supseteq \mathfrak{m}_a=(X_1 - a_1, \ldots, X_n - a_n)$$}@} for some {@{$a = (a_1,\dots,a_n)\in K^n$}@}. (annotation: See previous annotations.) But $\mathfrak{m}_a$ is {@{clearly maximal, so $\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}_a$}@}. This is {@{the weak Nullstellensatz}@}: every {@{maximal ideal of $K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ for algebraically closed _K_}@} is of {@{the form $\mathfrak{m}_a=(X_1 - a_1, \ldots, X_n - a_n)$ for some $a = (a_1,\dots,a_n)\in K^n$}@}. Because of {@{this close relationship}@}, some texts refer to {@{Zariski's lemma as the weak Nullstellensatz}@} or as {@{the 'algebraic version' of the weak Nullstellensatz}@}.<sup>[\[5\]](#^ref-5)</sup><sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup>

{@{The full Nullstellensatz}@} can also be {@{proved directly from Zariski's lemma}@} without employing {@{the Rabinowitsch trick}@}. Here is {@{a sketch of a proof using this lemma}@}.<sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup>

Let {@{$A = K[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$}@} for {@{algebraically closed field $K$}@}, and let $J$ be {@{an ideal of $A$}@} and {@{$V=\mathrm{V}(J)$}@} be {@{the common zeros of $J$ in $K^n$}@}. Recall that {@{$\mathrm{I}(V)$ is radical}@}, (annotation: Because {@{$f^r \in I(V)$ implies $f(x)^r = 0$ for all $x \in V$}@}, {@{the absence of nilpotents in the field}@} yields {@{$f(x) = 0$ and thus $f \in I(V)$}@}.) and therefore {@{$\sqrt{J} \subseteq \mathrm{I}(V)$}@}, where {@{$\mathrm{I}(V)$ is the ideal of polynomials in $A$ vanishing on $V$}@}. To show {@{the opposite inclusion (annotation: $\sqrt{J} \supseteq \mathrm{I}(V)$)}@}, let {@{$f \not\in \sqrt{J}$}@}. Then {@{$f \not\in \mathfrak{p}$}@} for some {@{prime ideal $\mathfrak{p} \supseteq J$ in _A_}@}. Let {@{$R = (A/\mathfrak{p}) [1/\bar{f}]$}@}, where $\bar{f}$ is {@{the image of $f$ under the natural map $A \to A/\mathfrak{p}$}@}, and $\mathfrak{m}$ be {@{a maximal ideal in $R$}@}. (annotation: {@{The ring $R = (A/\mathfrak{p})[1/\bar{f}]$}@} is constructed by taking {@{an integral domain $A/\mathfrak{p}$}@} and formally {@{adjoining a multiplicative inverse for $\bar{f}$}@}, making $\bar{f}$ {@{a unit (an invertible element possessing a multiplicative inverse)}@}. For a concrete example, let {@{$A = K[X, Y]$ and $\mathfrak{p} = (Y - X^2)$}@}, where {@{the quotient $A/\mathfrak{p}$}@} is {@{isomorphic to $K[X]$}@} because dividing by {@{the ideal relation $Y - X^2$ leaves a remainder where every $Y$ is replaced by $X^2$}@}, collapsing {@{polynomials into the single variable $X$}@}. Taking {@{$f = X$}@}, its {@{image $\bar{f} = X$ is this exact remainder projection}@}, and adjoining its {@{inverse yields the localization ring $R = K[X][1/X]$}@} of {@{rational expressions $\frac{P(X)}{X^k}$}@}, where $X$ explicitly becomes {@{a unit because $X \cdot \frac{1}{X} = 1$}@}.) By {@{Zariski's lemma}@}, {@{$R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a finite extension of $K$}@}, and thus, is {@{$K$ since $K$ is algebraically closed}@}. Let {@{$x_i$ be the images of $X_i$}@} under {@{the natural map $A \to A/\mathfrak{p}\to R \to R/\mathfrak{m}\cong K$}@}. (annotation: {@{The natural composite map $\phi: A \to A/\mathfrak{p} \to R \to R/\mathfrak{m} \cong K$}@} is constructed by first forming {@{the integral domain $A/\mathfrak{p}$ (valid because $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime)}@} and then adjoining {@{the multiplicative inverse $1/\bar{f}$}@} to create {@{the localization ring $R = (A/\mathfrak{p})[1/\bar{f}]$}@}. {@{This algebraic inversion}@} is {@{valid for integral domains and forces $\bar{f}$ to become a unit}@}, guaranteeing that it {@{cannot "disappear" or map to zero when quotienting by any maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$}@}.) It follows that, by {@{construction, $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in V$}@} but {@{$f(x) \ne 0$, so $f \notin \mathrm{I}(V)$}@}. (annotation: Setting each {@{coordinate $x_i = \phi(X_i)$}@} defines {@{a point $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$}@}. For every {@{polynomial $g \in J \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$}@}, its {@{residue $\bar{g} = 0$, forcing $\phi(g) = g(x) = 0$ so that $x \in V$}@}. Finally, because {@{$\bar{f}$ is a unit in $R$}@}, its {@{image in the field $R/\mathfrak{m} \cong K$ remains non-zero}@} (as {@{zero possesses no multiplicative inverse}@}), which forces {@{$f(x) = \phi(f) \neq 0$ and proves $f \notin I(V)$}@}.)

### using resultants

{@{The following constructive proof of the weak form}@} is one of {@{the oldest proofs}@} \({@{the strong form}@} results from {@{the [Rabinowitsch trick](Rabinowitsch%20trick.md), which is also constructive}@}\).

{@{The [resultant](resultant.md) of two polynomials}@} depending on {@{a variable _x_ and other variables}@} is {@{a polynomial in the other variables that is in the ideal generated by the two polynomials}@}, (annotation: {@{The vanishing of the resultant $\text{Res}_x(P, Q)$}@} is {@{a _necessary_ condition for the existence of a common zero}@} because {@{$\text{Res}_x(P, Q) \in (P, Q)$}@}, meaning any {@{simultaneous zero of $P$ and $Q$ forces the resultant to evaluate to zero}@} through {@{the algebraic relation $\text{Res}_x(P, Q) = A P + B Q$}@}, where $A$ and $B$ are {@{some polynomials such that $x$ is eliminated from the resultant}@}. Conversely, {@{vanishing alone is not sufficient in general}@}.) and has {@{the following properties}@}: if {@{one of the polynomials is [monic](monic%20polynomial.md) in _x_}@}, every {@{zero \(in the other variables\) of the resultant}@} may be {@{extended into a common zero of the two polynomials}@}. (annotation: When {@{the leading coefficient of at least one polynomial}@} with {@{respect to $x$ does not vanish at the evaluation point}@}, any {@{zero of their resultant necessarily extends to a common root}@}. For example, given {@{$P(x, y) = yx^2 + x + 1$}@} and {@{$Q(x, y) = yx^2 + 2x + 3$}@}, their {@{resultant $\text{Res}_x(P, Q) = 4y^2 - y$}@} yields {@{zeros at $y = 1/4$ and $y = 0$}@}. At {@{$y = 1/4$}@}, {@{the leading coefficients do not vanish}@}, and the zero {@{extends directly to the shared finite root $(-2, 1/4)$}@}. Conversely, when {@{both leading coefficients vanish simultaneously}@}, the resultant evaluates to {@{zero but introduces extraneous solutions where the polynomials fail to intersect}@}: at {@{$y = 0$, both $x^2$ coefficients vanish}@}, causing {@{a degree drop that yields $P(x, 0) = x + 1$ and $Q(x, 0) = 2x + 3$}@}, which share no {@{common zero because the simultaneous root has escaped to infinity}@}.)

The proof is as follows.

If {@{the ideal is [principal](principal%20ideal.md)}@}, generated by {@{a non-constant polynomial _p_ that depends on _x_}@}, (annotation: {@{Maximal ideals}@} are formed as {@{linear combinations of principal ideals}@}, and since every {@{proper ideal is contained within a maximal ideal}@}, {@{principal ideals}@} provide {@{the inductive base case}@} for establishing {@{existence of solutions across all proper ideals}@}.) one chooses {@{arbitrary values for the other variables}@}. {@{The [fundamental theorem of algebra](fundamental%20theorem%20of%20algebra.md)}@} asserts that this {@{choice can be extended to a zero of _p_}@}. (annotation: In simpler language, fixing {@{the other variables as constants}@} reduces {@{the polynomial to a single-variable equation in $x$}@}, and {@{the fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees a solution for $x$}@}.)

In the case of {@{several polynomials $p_1,\ldots, p_n$}@}, (annotation: Taking {@{a linear combination of the $n$ principal ideals generated by these polynomials}@} yields {@{a maximal ideal}@}.) {@{a linear change of variables}@} allows to suppose that {@{$p_1$ is monic in the first variable _x_}@}. (annotation: {@{A linear coordinate substitution}@} replaces {@{variables with generic linear combinations like $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_1$}@} to {@{shear the coordinate system}@}, ensuring {@{the highest-degree term's leading coefficient becomes a non-zero constant}@}.) Then, one introduces {@{$n-1$ new variables $u_2, \ldots, u_n$}@}, and one considers {@{the resultant $$R=\operatorname{Res}_x(p_1,u_2p_2+\cdots +u_np_n).$$}@} As _R_ is {@{in the ideal generated by $p_1,\ldots, p_n$}@}, the same is {@{true for the coefficients in _R_ of the [monomials](monomial.md) in $u_2, \ldots, u_n$}@}. (annotation: The resultant expands as {@{$R = A p_1 + B u_2 p_2 + \dots + B u_n p_n$}@}, where $A$ and $B$ are {@{some polynomials such that $x$ is eliminated from the resultant}@}. Equating {@{coefficients for each monomial in $u_2, \dots, u_n$}@} directly extracts {@{the coefficients $(B p_2, B p_3, \dots, B p_n)$}@}, proving every {@{coefficient belongs to the ideal $(p_1, \dots, p_n)$}@}.) So, if 1 is {@{in the ideal generated by these coefficients}@}, it is {@{also in the ideal generated by $p_1,\ldots, p_n$}@}. (annotation: In simpler language, if the ideal {@{generated by $(B p_2, \dots, B p_n)$ has no solution}@}, then {@{so is $(p_1, \dots, p_n)$}@}.) On the other hand, if these {@{coefficients have a common zero}@}, this zero can be {@{extended to a common zero of $p_1,\ldots, p_n$}@}, (annotation: In simpler language, if the ideal {@{generated by $(B p_2, \dots, B p_n)$ has a solution}@}, then this {@{solution extends to a solution of $(p_1, \dots, p_n)$}@}.) by {@{the above property of the resultant (annotation: together with $p_1$ being monic)}@}. (annotation: Treating each {@{extracted coefficient $Bp_i$}@} as {@{generating a principal ideal $(Bp_i)$}@}, if any {@{$Bp_i$ is a non-zero constant}@}, the ideal {@{contains $1$ and yields no common zero}@}. Otherwise, because each {@{$Bp_i$ depends on fewer variables}@}, we {@{recursively repeat the reduction by setting $p_1 \leftarrow Bp_2, \dots, p_{n-1} \leftarrow Bp_n$}@}, descending until {@{either a constant inconsistency arises}@} or {@{the base case reaches a single non-constant principal ideal in one variable}@}, whose solution is {@{guaranteed by the fundamental theorem of algebra}@} and {@{lifted back to $(p_1, \dots, p_n)$}@} via {@{the property of the resultant}@}.)

This {@{proves the weak Nullstellensatz}@} by {@{induction on the number of variables}@}. (annotation: Intuitively, this method {@{systematically reduces a multivariate system $p_1 = 0, \dots, p_n = 0$ to univariate equations}@} by {@{iterative elimination}@}: {@{a linear coordinate substitution $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_1$}@} shears {@{the variables to make $p_1$ monic in $x$}@}, and successive {@{resultants eliminate variables layer by layer}@}. Such a substitution is {@{always possible over an infinite field}@} by evaluating {@{the highest-degree homogeneous part non-zero at $(1, c_2, \dots, c_n)$ and scaling}@}. For a concrete system with {@{$p_1(x, y) = x^2 + y - 2$ and $p_2(x, y) = xy - 1$}@}, where $p_1$ is {@{monic in $x$}@}, computing {@{the resultant $R(y) = \text{Res}_x(p_1, p_2)$}@} eliminates {@{$x$ to yield the univariate polynomial $y^3 - 2y^2 + 1 = 0$}@}, producing {@{roots like $y = 1$}@} that {@{back-substitute into $p_1(x, 1) = x^2 - 1 = 0$ to solve for $x = \pm 1$}@}, of which only {@{$x = 1$ also satisfies $p_2(1, 1) = 0$}@}, yielding {@{the common solution $(1, 1)$}@}.)

### using Gröbner bases

{@{A [Gröbner basis](Gröbner%20basis.md) is an algorithmic concept}@} that was {@{introduced in 1973 by [Bruno Buchberger](Bruno%20Buchberger.md)}@}. It is presently {@{fundamental in [computational geometry](computational%20geometry.md)}@}. {@{A Gröbner basis}@} is {@{a special generating set of an ideal}@} from which {@{most properties of the ideal can easily be extracted}@}. Those that are related to {@{the Nullstellensatz}@} are the following: (annotation: 4 items: {@{whether an ideal containing 1, number of common zeros}@}, {@{iteratively solving univariate polynomials, strong Nullstellensatz via saturation}@}.)

- {@{An ideal contains 1}@} {@{if and only if its [reduced Gröbner basis](reduced%20Gröbner%20basis.md#reduced) \(for any [monomial ordering](monomial%20ordering.md)\) is 1}@}.
- {@{The number of the common zeros of the polynomials}@} in {@{a Gröbner basis}@} is strongly {@{related to the number of [monomials](monomial.md) that are [irreducibles](Gröbner%20basis.md#reduction) by the basis}@}. Namely, {@{the number of common zeros is infinite}@} {@{if and only if the same is true for the irreducible monomials}@}; if {@{the two numbers are finite}@}, {@{the number of irreducible monomials}@} equals {@{the numbers of zeros \(in an algebraically closed field\), counted with multiplicities}@}.
- With {@{a [lexicographic monomial order](lexicographic%20order.md)}@}, {@{the common zeros}@} can be computed by {@{solving iteratively [univariate polynomials](univariate%20polynomial.md#univariate)}@} \(this is not {@{used in practice since one knows better algorithms}@}\).
- {@{Strong Nullstellensatz}@}: {@{a power of _p_ belongs to an ideal _I_}@} {@{if and only the [saturation](Gröbner%20basis.md#saturation) of _I_ by _p_}@} produces {@{the Gröbner basis 1}@}. Thus, {@{the strong Nullstellensatz}@} results {@{almost immediately from the definition of the saturation}@}.

## generalizations

{@{The Nullstellensatz is subsumed}@} by {@{a systematic development of the theory of [Jacobson rings](Jacobson%20ring.md)}@}, which are {@{those rings in which every radical ideal is an intersection of maximal ideals}@}. \text{Spec}\,A_i$}@}) lets us assemble {@{a single affine scheme $X' = \text{Spec}\,A'$ over $X$}@}, whereas assuming {@{$X$ is both quasi-compact and quasi-separated}@} ({@{intersections of quasi-compact open subsets}@} are {@{quasi-compact}@}, so {@{pairwise overlaps $U_i \cap U_j$}@} are {@{finite unions of open affines and cannot harbor infinitely many overlap obstructions}@}) enables choosing {@{$g: X' \to X$ to be quasi-finite}@} ({@{quasi-compact}@} with {@{finite fibers $g^{-1}(x)$ for every $x \in X$}@}, upgrading {@{locally quasi-finite}@}) as well as {@{faithfully flat and locally of finite presentation}@}.) and if {@{$f$ is [smooth](smooth%20morphism.md) surjective}@}, then one may take {@{$g$ to be [étale](étale%20morphism.md)}@}.<sup>[\[10\]](#^ref-10)</sup> (annotation: For {@{$f_0: \text{Spec}(\mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2+1)) \to \text{Spec}\,\mathbb{R}$}@}, {@{the inclusion $\varphi_0: \mathbb{R} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$}@} is {@{a degree-$2$ field extension}@} ({@{$\dim_{\mathbb{R} } \mathbb{C} = 2$}@} with {@{$\mathbb{R}$-basis $\{1, \bar{t}\}$ and $\bar{t}^2 + 1 = 0$}@}), giving {@{a _residue_ field extension}@} {@{$\kappa((0)) \cong \mathbb{C}$ over $\kappa((0)) \cong \mathbb{R}$}@} because {@{the local ring at the unique prime $(0) \in \text{Spec}(\mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2+1))$}@} is already {@{the field $\mathbb{C}$ with maximal ideal $(0)$}@} and {@{_residue_ field $\mathbb{C}/\mathfrak{m}_{(0)} = \mathbb{C}/(0) = \mathbb{C}$}@} (and {@{$\mathbb{R}/(0) = \mathbb{R}$ at $(0) \in \text{Spec}\,\mathbb{R}$}@}), which is {@{_separable_ since $\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{R}) = 0$ and the minimal polynomial $t^2 + 1$}@} has {@{derivative $(t^2+1)' = 2t$ coprime to $t^2+1$ in $\mathbb{R}[t]$}@} (with {@{distinct roots $i$ and $-i$}@} in $\mathbb{C}$); for any {@{quotient $A[t]/(p(t))$ over a ring $A$}@}, {@{the module of Kähler differentials $\Omega^1_{(A[t]/(p(t)))/A}$}@} is presented over {@{$A[t]/(p(t))$ by the generator $d\bar{t}$}@} modulo {@{the relation from applying the $A$-linear derivation $d$ to $\bar{p}(\bar{t}) = 0$}@}, giving {@{$d(\bar{t}^2 + 1) = 2\bar{t}\,d\bar{t} + d(1) = 2\bar{t}\,d\bar{t} = 0$ in $\Omega^1_{(\mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2+1))/\mathbb{R} }$}@}, and since {@{$2 \in \mathbb{R}^\times$ and $\bar{t}$}@} is {@{a unit in $\mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ with $\bar{t}^{-1} = -\bar{t}$}@} (as {@{$\bar{t}^2 = -1$}@}), $2\bar{t}$ is {@{invertible with $(2\bar{t})^{-1} = -\bar{t}/2$}@}, so multiplying {@{$2\bar{t}\,d\bar{t} = 0$ by $-\bar{t}/2$ yields $d\bar{t} = 0$}@}, hence {@{$\Omega^1_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R} } = 0$}@} and both {@{$f_0$ and $g_0: \text{Spec}\,\mathbb{C} \to \text{Spec}\,\mathbb{R}$ are _smooth_ and _surjective_}@} with {@{$g_0$ _étale_}@}. This exemplifies how whenever {@{$f: Y \to X$}@} is {@{_smooth_ and _surjective_}@} ({@{locally of finite presentation, flat, and formally smooth}@} so {@{infinitesimal lifts exist without singularities, branching, or empty fibers}@}), we may choose {@{the cover $g: X' \to X$ to be _étale_}@} ({@{flat, locally of finite presentation, and unramified}@}, meaning {@{$\Omega^1_{X'/X} = 0$ and each residue field extension $\kappa(x')/\kappa(g(x'))$}@} is {@{separable, or finite and separable when $g$ is quasi-finite}@}), so $g$ acts like {@{a local isomorphism in the étale topology}@} that supplies {@{missing roots}@} and yields {@{a section after base change without ramification or repeated-root pathologies}@}.)

{@{[Serge Lang](Serge%20Lang.md) gave an extension}@} of {@{the Nullstellensatz}@} to {@{the case of infinitely many generators}@}:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Let $\kappa$ be {@{an [infinite cardinal](infinite%20cardinal.md)}@} and let $K$ be {@{an algebraically closed field}@} whose {@{[transcendence degree](transcendence%20degree.md) over its [prime subfield](prime%20subfield.md#case%20of%20fields) is strictly greater than $\kappa$}@}. Then for any {@{set $S$ of cardinality $\kappa$}@}, {@{the polynomial ring $A = K[x_i]_{i \in S}$}@} satisfies {@{the Nullstellensatz, i.e., for any ideal $J \sub A$ we have that $\sqrt{J} = \hbox{I} (\hbox{V} (J))$}@}.<sup>[\[11\]](#^ref-11)</sup> (annotation: For {@{$S = \mathbb{N}$ ($\vert{}S\vert{} = \aleph_0$)}@} and {@{$K = \mathbb{C}$ ($\mathrm{tr.deg}_{\mathbb{Q} }\,\mathbb{C} = 2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$)}@}, {@{$A = \mathbb{C}[x_n]_{n \in \mathbb{N} }$}@} extends {@{finite-variable Nullstellensatz on $\mathbb{C}[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ to infinitely many variables}@} while keeping {@{residue fields of maximal ideals equal to $K$}@}; intuitively, scaling {@{the index set to an infinite cardinal $\kappa$}@} only preserves {@{$K$-rational point correspondence when $\mathrm{tr.deg}_{k_0}\,K > \kappa$}@} supplies {@{enough algebraically independent elements to prevent nontrivial field extensions}@}; formally, $\kappa$ is {@{an infinite cardinal ($\kappa \ge \aleph_0$)}@}, $K$ is {@{an algebraically closed field (every non-constant $p(t) \in K[t]$ has a root in $K$)}@}, its {@{prime subfield $k_0$}@} is {@{the unique smallest subfield ($\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{F}_p$)}@}, and {@{$\mathrm{tr.deg}_{k_0}\,K > \kappa$}@} means {@{a transcendence basis $B \subset K$ (maximal $k_0$-algebraically independent subset)}@} satisfies {@{$\vert{}B\vert{} > \kappa$}@}.)

## effective Nullstellensatz

In {@{all of its variants}@}, {@{Hilbert's Nullstellensatz}@} asserts that some {@{polynomial _g_ belongs or not to an ideal generated}@}, say, by {@{_f_<sub>1</sub>, ..., _f<sub>k</sub>_}@}; we have {@{_g_ = _f<sup> r</sup>_ in the strong version}@}, {@{_g_ = 1 in the weak form}@}. This means {@{the existence or the non-existence of polynomials _g_<sub>1</sub>, ..., _g<sub>k</sub>_}@} such that {@{_g_ = _f_<sub>1</sub>_g_<sub>1</sub> + ... + _f<sub>k</sub>g<sub>k</sub>_}@}. {@{The usual proofs of the Nullstellensatz}@} are {@{not constructive, non-effective}@}, in the sense that they {@{do not give any way to compute the _g<sub>i</sub>_}@}.

It is thus {@{a rather natural question to ask}@} if there is {@{an effective way to compute the _g<sub>i</sub>_ \(and the exponent _r_ in the strong form\)}@} or to {@{prove that they do not exist}@}. To {@{solve this problem}@}, it suffices to {@{provide an upper bound on the total degree of the _g<sub>i</sub>_}@}: such {@{a bound reduces the problem to a finite [system of linear equations](system%20of%20linear%20equations.md)}@} that may be {@{solved by usual [linear algebra](linear%20algebra.md) techniques}@}. {@{Any such upper bound}@} is called {@{an __effective Nullstellensatz__}@}. (annotation: With {@{$\deg(g_i) \le D$ for each $g_i \in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$}@} in {@{$\sum_{i=1}^m g_i f_i = h^r$}@} (where {@{$h^r = 1$ in the weak form}@}), write {@{$g_i(x) = \sum_{\vert{}\alpha\vert{} \le D} c_{i,\alpha} x^\alpha$}@} using multi-indices {@{$\alpha = (\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^n$}@}, total degree {@{$\vert{}\alpha\vert{} = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j \le D$}@}, and monomials {@{$x^\alpha = x_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$}@} with unknown {@{coefficients $c_{i,\alpha} \in k$}@}; since there are {@{$\binom{n+D}{n}$ multi-indices with $\vert{}\alpha\vert{} \le D$}@} for each of {@{the $m$ indices $i \in \{1,\dots,m\}$}@}, there are {@{$m\binom{n+D}{n}$ unknowns $c_{i,\alpha}$}@} in total. Substituting {@{$g_i(x)$ into $\sum_{i=1}^m g_i(x) f_i(x) = \sum_{\vert{}\beta\vert{} \le M} b_\beta x^\beta$}@}, where {@{$M = \max(D + \max_i \deg(f_i),\, r\deg(h))$}@} bounds {@{all resulting monomial degrees}@} and $b_\beta \in k$ are {@{the known coefficients of $h^r$}@}, gives {@{one equation $\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{\vert{}\alpha\vert{} \le D} A_{\beta,(i,\alpha)} c_{i,\alpha} = b_\beta$}@} for each {@{monomial $x^\beta$ with $\vert{}\beta\vert{} \le M$}@} (where $A_{\beta,(i,\alpha)} \in k$ is {@{the coefficient of $x^\beta$ in $x^\alpha f_i(x)$, read off from $f_i$}@}), yielding {@{$\binom{n+M}{n}$ linear equations in those $m\binom{n+D}{n}$ unknowns}@}.)

{@{A related problem}@} is {@{the __ideal membership problem__}@}, which consists in testing if {@{a polynomial belongs to an ideal}@}. (annotation: Testing whether {@{$h \in \langle f_1,\dots,f_m \rangle \subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ (ideal membership)}@} asks for {@{$g_1,\dots,g_m \in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ such that $\sum_{i=1}^m g_i f_i = h$}@}, matching {@{the same representation structure as $\sum_{i=1}^m g_i f_i = 1$}@} in {@{the weak Nullstellensatz (with $h = 1$)}@} and likewise reducing to {@{linear algebra once $\deg(g_i) \le D$ is bounded}@}.) For this problem also, {@{a solution is provided}@} by {@{an upper bound on the degree of the _g<sub>i</sub>_}@}. {@{A general solution of the ideal membership problem}@} provides {@{an effective Nullstellensatz, at least for the weak form}@}. (annotation: Conversely, {@{an algorithm or degree bound solving ideal membership for any target polynomial $h$}@} translates back to {@{the weak Nullstellensatz by setting $h = 1$}@}, so testing whether {@{$1 \in \langle f_1,\dots,f_m \rangle$}@} (which holds {@{over an algebraically closed field $k$ if and only if $V(f_1,\dots,f_m) = \emptyset$}@}) and bounding {@{$\deg(g_i)$ when $1 = \sum_{i=1}^m g_i f_i$}@} yields {@{an effective weak Nullstellensatz}@}.)

In {@{1925, [Grete Hermann](Grete%20Hermann.md) gave an upper bound}@} for {@{ideal membership problem that is doubly exponential (annotation: $a^{b^x}$ where $a, b > 1$) in the number of variables}@}. In {@{1982 Mayr and Meyer gave an example}@} where {@{the _g<sub>i</sub>_ have a degree that is at least double exponential}@}, showing that every {@{general upper bound for the ideal membership problem}@} is {@{doubly exponential in the number of variables}@}.

Since most {@{mathematicians at the time assumed the effective Nullstellensatz}@} was {@{at least as hard as ideal membership}@}, few {@{mathematicians sought a bound better than double-exponential}@}. In {@{1987, however, [W. Dale Brownawell](W.%20Dale%20Brownawell.md) gave an upper bound}@} for {@{the effective Nullstellensatz that is simply exponential in the number of variables}@}.<sup>[\[12\]](#^ref-12)</sup> {@{Brownawell's proof}@} relied on {@{analytic techniques valid only in [characteristic](characteristic%20(algebra).md) 0}@}, but, {@{one year later, [János Kollár](János%20Kollár.md) gave a purely algebraic proof}@}, valid in {@{any characteristic, of a slightly better bound}@}.

In the case of {@{the weak Nullstellensatz}@}, {@{Kollár's bound}@} is the following:<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup>

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (annotation: {@{Kollár's bound}@}, first paragraph) Let {@{_f_<sub>1</sub>, ..., _f<sub>s</sub>_}@} be {@{polynomials in _n_ ≥ 2 variables, of total degree _d_<sub>1</sub> ≥ ... ≥ _d<sub>s</sub>_ (annotation: ordered byb descending total degree)}@}. If there {@{exist polynomials _g<sub>i</sub>_ such that _f_<sub>1</sub>_g_<sub>1</sub> + ... + _f<sub>s</sub>g<sub>s</sub>_ = 1}@}, then they can be {@{chosen such that $$\deg(f_ig_i) \le \max(d_s,3)\prod_{j=1}^{\min(n,s)-1}\max(d_j,3).$$}@} (annotation: In words, {@{floor each degree at 3}@}, multiply {@{the first $\min(n,s)-1$ degrees}@}, and multiply by {@{the last degree or 3, whichever is larger}@}.)

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (annotation: {@{Kollár's bound}@}, second paragraph) {@{This bound is optimal}@} if {@{all the degrees are greater than 2}@}. (annotation: In this case, {@{the flooring has no effect}@}, and the bound is {@{the product of the first $\min(n,s)-1$ degrees and the last degree}@}.)

If {@{_d_ is the maximum of the degrees of the _f<sub>i</sub>_}@}, {@{this bound may be simplified}@} to {@{$$\max(3,d)^{\min(n,s)}.$$}@} (annotation: This bound is {@{looser but easier to remember}@}.) {@{An improvement due to M. Sombra}@} is<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup> {@{$$\deg(f_ig_i) \le 2d_s\prod_{j=1}^{\min(n,s)-1}d_j.$$}@} (annotation: In words, {@{multiply the first $\min(n,s)-1$ degrees}@}, multiply by {@{the last degree and then by 2}@}. Compared to {@{Kollár's bound}@}, the flooring {@{each degree at 3 is replaced by multiplying a factor of 2}@}.) {@{His bound improves Kollár's}@} as soon as {@{at least two of the degrees that are involved are lower than 3}@}. (annotation: To see this, {@{the contribution of each degree}@} only {@{differs if it is 1 or 2}@}. Only consider {@{those that differ}@}. Then the remaining {@{degrees contribute the same factor to both bounds}@}. For {@{the $k \ge 2$ differing degrees}@}, Kollár's bound {@{contributes a factor of exactly $3^k$}@}, while Sombra's bound {@{contributes a factor of at most $2^{k+1}$}@}. Since {@{$3^k > 2^{k+1}$ for all $k \ge 2$}@}, Sombra's bound is {@{better than Kollár's}@}.)

## projective Nullstellensatz

We can {@{formulate a certain correspondence}@} between {@{homogeneous ideals of polynomials and algebraic subsets of a projective space}@}, called {@{the __projective Nullstellensatz__}@}, that is {@{analogous to the affine one}@}. To do that, we introduce {@{some notations}@}. Let {@{$R = k[t_0, \ldots, t_n]$}@}. {@{The homogeneous ideal}@}, (annotation: In {@{$R = k[t_0, t_1, t_2] = \bigoplus_{d \ge 0} R_d$}@} with {@{$R_d = \mathrm{span}_k\{t_0^a t_1^b t_2^c : a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0},\, a+b+c = d\}$}@} (so {@{$R_0 = k$, $R_1 = \mathrm{span}_k\{t_0, t_1, t_2\}$, and $R_2 = \mathrm{span}_k\{t_0^2, t_1^2, t_2^2, t_0 t_1, t_0 t_2, t_1 t_2\}$}@}), for {@{$I = \langle t_0, t_1^2 \rangle$}@}, {@{$g = t_0 + t_1^2 \in I$}@} splits into {@{$g_1 = t_0 \in R_1 \cap I$ and $g_2 = t_1^2 \in R_2 \cap I$}@}, and for any {@{$f \in R_d$ and $\lambda \in k^\times$}@}, {@{$f(\lambda t_0, \lambda t_1, \lambda t_2) = \lambda^d f(t_0, t_1, t_2)$}@} so {@{$f(p) = 0$ is well-defined on $[t_0:t_1:t_2] \in \mathbb{P}^2_k$}@}, whereas for {@{$J = \langle t_0^2 - t_1 t_2 - 1 \rangle$}@}, {@{$h = t_0^2 - t_1 t_2 - 1 \in J$}@} has {@{$h_2 = t_0^2 - t_1 t_2 \notin J$ and $h_0 = -1 \notin J$}@} with {@{$h(\lambda t_0, \lambda t_1, \lambda t_2) = \lambda^2(t_0^2 - t_1 t_2) - 1$ depending on $\lambda$}@}; intuitively, {@{homogeneous ideals}@} preserve {@{degree grading so zero loci scale uniformly on projective space}@}, whereas {@{non-homogeneous ideals lack scaling invariance}@}; formally, {@{an ideal $I \subset R$ is homogeneous}@} if {@{$I = \bigoplus_{d \ge 0} (I \cap R_d)$}@} (equivalently {@{generated by homogeneous elements $p \in R_d$}@}), whereas {@{a general ideal $J \subset R$}@} is {@{merely an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by all elements of $R$}@}.) {@{$$R_+ = \bigoplus_{d \geqslant 1} R_d$$}@} is called {@{the _maximal homogeneous ideal_ \(see also [irrelevant ideal](irrelevant%20ideal.md)\)}@}. (annotation: In {@{$R = k[t_0, t_1, t_2]$}@}, {@{the irrelevant ideal $R_+ = \bigoplus_{d \ge 1} R_d = \langle t_0, t_1, t_2 \rangle$}@} consists of {@{polynomials with zero constant term}@} and is {@{maximal among proper homogeneous ideals}@} ({@{any element outside $R_+$}@} has {@{non-zero constant term $c \in k^\times$, generating $1 \in R$}@}), whereas {@{$\mathfrak{m} = \langle t_0 - 1, t_1, t_2 \rangle$}@} is {@{a maximal ideal of $R$ ($R/\mathfrak{m} \cong k$, matching $(1,0,0) \in k^3$)}@} that is not {@{homogeneous because $t_0 - 1 \in \mathfrak{m}$ has parts $t_0 \notin \mathfrak{m}$ and $-1 \notin \mathfrak{m}$}@}.) As in {@{the affine case}@}, we let: for {@{a subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n$}@} and {@{a homogeneous ideal $I$ of $R$}@}, {@{$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{I}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(S) &= \{ f \in R_+ \mid f = 0 \text{ on } S \}, \\ \operatorname{V}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(I) &= \{ x \in \mathbb{P}^n \mid f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I \}. \end{aligned}$$}@} (annotation: {@{The projective ideal $I_{\mathbb{P}^n}(S)$}@} is {@{the set of homogeneous elements in $R_+$ that vanish at every point of $S \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n$}@}. {@{The projective zero locus $V_{\mathbb{P}^n}(I)$}@} is {@{the set of points in $\mathbb{P}^n$ where every polynomial in the homogeneous ideal $I$ vanishes}@}.) By {@{$f = 0 \text{ on } S$}@} we mean: for every {@{homogeneous coordinates $(a_0 : \cdots : a_n)$ of a point of $S$}@} we have {@{$f(a_0,\ldots, a_n)=0$}@}. This implies that {@{the homogeneous components of $f$}@} are {@{also zero on $S$}@} and thus that {@{$\operatorname{I}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(S)$ is a homogeneous ideal}@}. Equivalently, {@{$\operatorname{I}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(S)$}@} is {@{the homogeneous ideal generated by homogeneous polynomials $f$ that vanish on $S$}@}. Now, for any {@{homogeneous ideal $I \subseteq R_+$}@}, by {@{the usual Nullstellensatz}@}, we have: {@{$$\sqrt{I} = \operatorname{I}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(\operatorname{V}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(I)),$$}@} (annotation: Viewing {@{points of $\mathbb{P}^n$ as lines through the origin in $K^{n+1}$}@} makes {@{$V_{K^{n+1} }(I)$ an algebraic set in $K^{n+1}$}@}, and because {@{a homogeneous polynomial vanishes along a line through $0 \in K^{n+1}$}@} {@{if and only if it vanishes at the corresponding point of $\mathbb{P}^n$ (with non-constant homogeneous elements in $R_+$)}@}, {@{the affine $I_{K^{n+1} }(V_{K^{n+1} }(I))$ and projective $I_{\mathbb{P}^n}(V_{\mathbb{P}^n}(I))$ operations}@} {@{agree on $R_+$}@}, allowing {@{the usual Nullstellensatz to give $I_{\mathbb{P}^n}(V_{\mathbb{P}^n}(I)) = \sqrt{I}$}@} (and {@{$I$ itself when $I$ is radical}@}).) and so, like in {@{the affine case}@}, we have:<sup>[\[15\]](#^ref-15)</sup>

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; There exists {@{an order-reversing one-to-one correspondence}@} between {@{proper homogeneous radical ideals of $R$ and subsets of $\mathbb{P}^n$ of the form $\operatorname{V}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(I)$}@}. {@{The correspondence}@} is given by {@{$\operatorname{I}_{\mathbb{P}^n}$ and $\operatorname{V}_{\mathbb{P}^n}$}@}.

## analytic Nullstellensatz \(Rückert’s Nullstellensatz\)

{@{The Nullstellensatz also holds}@} for {@{the [germs](germ%20(mathematics).md) of holomorphic functions at a point of complex _n_-space $\Complex^n$}@}. (annotation: {@{A germ $f = [(U, \tilde{f})] \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x}$}@} is {@{an equivalence class of pairs $(U, \tilde{f})$ ($x \in U \subseteq \mathbb{C}^n$ open, $\tilde{f} : U \to \mathbb{C}$ holomorphic)}@} with {@{$(U, \tilde{f}) \sim (V, \tilde{g})$ when $\tilde{f}\vert{}_W = \tilde{g}\vert{}_W$}@} on some {@{open $x \in W \subseteq U \cap V$}@}. Intuitively, $f$ retains {@{local behavior arbitrarily close to $x$ (via convergent power series at $x$)}@} while {@{discarding behavior away from $x$}@}.) Precisely, for each {@{open subset $U \subseteq \Complex^n$}@}, let {@{$\mathcal{O}_{\Complex^n}(U)$}@} denote {@{the ring of holomorphic functions on _U_}@}; then {@{$\mathcal{O}_{\Complex^n}$ is a _[sheaf](sheaf%20(mathematics).md)_ on $\Complex^n$}@}. {@{The [stalk](stalk%20(sheaf).md) $\mathcal{O}_{\Complex^n, 0}$ at, say, the origin}@} can be shown to be {@{a [Noetherian](Noetherian%20ring.md) [local ring](local%20ring.md) that is a [unique factorization domain](unique%20factorization%20domain.md)}@}. (annotation: {@{Germs}@} form {@{the elements of the Noetherian local ring $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x}$}@} (acting like {@{a polynomial ring}@}), {@{ideals $I = \langle f_1, \dots, f_m \rangle \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x}$}@} act like {@{polynomial ideals}@}, and {@{$\mathfrak{m}_x = \{[(U, \tilde{f})] \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x} : \tilde{f}(x) = 0\}$}@} is its {@{unique maximal ideal}@} because for any {@{$g = [(V, \tilde{g})] \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x} \setminus \mathfrak{m}_x$, $\tilde{g}(x) \neq 0$}@} implies {@{$\tilde{g} \neq 0$ on some open neighborhood $x \in W \subseteq V$ by continuity}@}, so {@{$[(W, 1/\tilde{g})] \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x}$}@} satisfies {@{$g \cdot [(W, 1/\tilde{g})] = 1$}@}. This makes $g$ {@{a unit and $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x}/\mathfrak{m}_x \cong \mathbb{C}$}@}, so any {@{ideal strictly containing $\mathfrak{m}_x$ contains $1$ and equals $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, x}$}@}.)

If {@{$f \in \mathcal{O}_{\Complex ^n, 0}$}@} is {@{a germ represented by a [holomorphic function](holomorphic%20function.md) $\widetilde{f}: U \to \Complex$}@}, then let {@{$V_0(f)$ be the [equivalence class](equivalence%20class.md)}@} of {@{the set $$\left \{ z \in U \mid \widetilde{f}(z) = 0 \right\},$$}@} where {@{two subsets $X, Y \subseteq \Complex^n$ are considered equivalent}@} if {@{$X \cap U = Y \cap U$ for some neighborhood _U_ of 0}@}. Note $V_0(f)$ is {@{independent of a choice of the representative $\widetilde{f}$}@}. (annotation: {@{The set $Z(U, \tilde{f}) = \{z \in U \mid \tilde{f}(z) = 0\}$}@} is {@{the zero locus of the representative $\tilde{f}$ on a neighborhood $U$ of $0$}@}, and taking its {@{equivalence class $V_0(f)$ under $X \cap U' = Y \cap U'$ for some neighborhood $U'$ of $0$}@} identifies {@{subsets agreeing arbitrarily close to $0$ while discarding behavior farther out}@}, forming {@{the local zero set germ at $0$}@}. This is {@{well-defined}@} because if {@{$(U, \tilde{f}) \sim (V, \tilde{g})$ as germs at $0$}@}, then {@{$\tilde{f}\vert{}_W = \tilde{g}\vert{}_W$ on some open neighborhood $W \subseteq U \cap V$ of $0$}@}, so {@{$Z(U, \tilde{f}) \cap W = \{z \in W \mid \tilde{f}(z) = 0\} = \{z \in W \mid \tilde{g}(z) = 0\} = Z(V, \tilde{g}) \cap W$}@}, meaning {@{$Z(U, \tilde{f})$ and $Z(V, \tilde{g})$ lie in the same equivalence class}@}.) For each {@{ideal $I \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{\Complex^n,0}$}@}, let {@{$V_0(I)$}@} denote {@{$V_0(f_1) \cap \dots \cap V_0(f_r)$ for some generators $f_1, \ldots, f_r$ of $I$}@}. It is {@{well-defined; i.e., is independent of a choice of the generators}@}. (annotation: Because {@{$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$ is Noetherian}@}, every {@{ideal $I \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$ admits a finite generating set $\{f_1, \dots, f_r\} \subseteq I$}@}. Given two {@{generating sets $\{f_1, \dots, f_r\}$ and $\{g_1, \dots, g_s\}$ of $I$}@}, each {@{$g_j = \sum_{i=1}^r a_{ji} f_i$ ($a_{ji} \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$)}@} is {@{an $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$-linear combination of the $f_i$}@}; choosing {@{a neighborhood $W$ of $0$ where all representatives $\tilde{f}_i$, $\tilde{g}_j$, and $\tilde{a}_{ji}$}@} are defined with {@{$\tilde{g}_j = \sum_{i=1}^r \tilde{a}_{ji} \tilde{f}_i$}@}, any {@{$z \in W$ satisfying $\tilde{f}_i(z) = 0$ for all $i$}@} also satisfies {@{$\tilde{g}_j(z) = \sum_{i=1}^r \tilde{a}_{ji}(z)\tilde{f}_i(z) = 0$ for all $j$}@}, and symmetry yields {@{$V_0(f_1) \cap \dots \cap V_0(f_r) = V_0(g_1) \cap \dots \cap V_0(g_s)$}@}.)

For each {@{subset $X \subseteq \Complex ^n$}@}, let {@{$$I_0(X) = \left \{ f \in \mathcal{O}_{\Complex^n,0} \mid V_0(f) \supset X \right \}.$$}@} (annotation: $I_0(X)$ is {@{the set of holomorphic function germs at $0$}@} whose {@{local zero locus germ contains $X$ (in the germ sense around $0$)}@}.) It is easy to see that {@{$I_0(X)$ is an ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{\Complex ^n, 0}$}@} and that {@{$I_0(X) = I_0(Y)$ if $X \sim Y$}@} in the sense discussed above. (annotation: For {@{$f, g \in I_0(X)$ and $h \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$}@}, representatives {@{$\tilde{f}, \tilde{g}$ vanishing on a neighborhood of $X$ near $0$}@} also make {@{$\tilde{f} + \tilde{g}$ and $\tilde{h}\tilde{f}$ vanish there}@}, so {@{$I_0(X)$ is an ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$}@}. Moreover, if {@{$X \sim Y$}@}, then {@{$X \cap U' = Y \cap U'$ on some neighborhood $U'$ of $0$}@}, so {@{containment of local zero locus germs near $0$}@} is {@{identical for $X$ and $Y$, giving $I_0(X) = I_0(Y)$}@}.)

{@{The __analytic Nullstellensatz__}@} then states:<sup>[\[16\]](#^ref-16)</sup> for each {@{ideal $I \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{\Complex ^n, 0}$}@}, {@{$$\sqrt{I} = I_0(V_0(I))$$}@} where the left-hand side is {@{the [radical](radical%20of%20an%20ideal.md) of $I$}@}. (annotation: Paralleling {@{the algebraic Strong Nullstellensatz $I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}$}@} for {@{$J \subseteq \mathbb{C}[z_1, \dots, z_n]$}@}, {@{$\sqrt{I} = I_0(V_0(I))$}@} equates {@{local vanishing on the analytic germ $V_0(I)$}@} with {@{nilpotence modulo $I$ in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^n, 0}$}@}, but requires {@{Weierstrass preparation and division rather than algebraic Nullstellensatz}@} since {@{convergent power series germs replace polynomials}@}.)

## formal Nullstellensatz

(annotation: {@{"Formal"}@} means {@{the correspondence between radical ideals and Zariski-closed sets}@} is {@{purely symbolic and ring-theoretic}@}, holding for any {@{commutative ring $R$ without coordinates or field-extension arguments}@}.)

In {@{classical algebraic geometry}@}, {@{the zero locus \('variety'\) operation \($V$\)}@} is applied to {@{subsets of the ring of polynomials over an algebraically closed field}@}, while {@{the vanishing ideal operation \($I$\)}@} is applied to {@{subsets of affine $n$-space}@}, whose points are {@{in one-to-one correspondence with the maximal ideals of the polynomial ring}@} by {@{the weak Nullstellensatz \(see above\)}@}. In {@{[scheme theory](scheme%20(mathematics).md)}@}, {@{$V$ and $I$ are generalized and redefined}@} so that they could be applied to {@{subsets of arbitrary commutative \(unital\) rings and their _prime_ spectra}@}, respectively. (annotation: Under {@{this correspondence}@}, {@{$R$}@} matches {@{a coordinate ring like $\mathbb{C}[x, y]$ or $\mathbb{Z}$}@}, {@{a prime $\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec}(R)$}@} matches {@{an irreducible subvariety or point such as $(x - a, y - b)$ in $\mathbb{C}[x, y]$ or $(p)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$}@}, {@{evaluating $f$ at $\mathfrak{p}$}@} is {@{passing to $R/\mathfrak{p}$, where $f(\mathfrak{p}) = 0$ means $f \in \mathfrak{p}$}@}.) In particular, for any {@{[commutative ring](commutative%20ring.md) _A_}@}, its {@{set of prime ideals \([prime spectrum](spectrum%20of%20a%20ring.md)\) $\mathrm{Spec}\ A$}@}, and subsets {@{$S\subset A$ and $X\subset \mathrm{Spec}\ A$}@}, we set <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$\mathbb{V}(S) = \{\mathfrak{p}\in \mathrm{Spec}\ A \mid \mathfrak{p}\supset S\}$ and $\mathbb{I}(X)=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in X} \mathfrak{p}$}@}. (annotation: Here {@{$\mathbb{V}(S)$}@} collects all {@{points or irreducible subvarieties $\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec} A$}@} where every {@{function $f \in S$ vanishes ($f \in \mathfrak{p}$)}@}, while {@{$\mathbb{I}(X) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \in X} \mathfrak{p}$}@} collects all {@{elements of $A$ that vanish at every prime $\mathfrak{p}$ in the subset $X$}@}.) <p> Then for any {@{ideal $J\triangleleft A$}@}, {@{a formal analogue of Hilbert's Nullstellensatz}@} holds: {@{$$\mathbb{I}(\mathbb{V}(J)) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{V}(J)}\mathfrak{p}= \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}\ A,\ \mathfrak{p}\supset J}\mathfrak{p}=\sqrt{J},$$}@} where {@{the last equality}@} follows from {@{a standard property of prime ideals from commutative algebra}@}.<sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup> In {@{analogy to zero loci in the classical theory}@}, {@{the $\mathbb{V}(S)$ are used}@} to define {@{the closed sets in the [Zariski topology](Zariski%20topology.md) of $\mathrm{Spec}\ A$}@}.<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup>
