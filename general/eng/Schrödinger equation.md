{@{The __Schrödinger equation__}@} is {@{a [partial differential equation](partial%20differential%20equation.md) that governs the [wave function](wave%20function.md) of a non-relativistic quantum-mechanical system}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;1–2&hairsp;</sup> Its discovery was {@{a significant landmark in the development of [quantum mechanics](quantum%20mechanics.md)}@}. Conceptually, {@{the Schrödinger equation}@} is {@{the quantum counterpart of [Newton's second law](Newton's%20second%20law.md#second%20law) in [classical mechanics](classical%20mechanics.md)}@}. Given {@{a set of known [initial conditions](initial%20conditions.md)}@}, {@{Newton's second law}@} makes {@{a mathematical prediction as to what path a given [physical system](physical%20system.md) will take over time}@}. {@{The Schrödinger equation}@} gives {@{the evolution over time of the [wave function](wave%20function.md)}@}, {@{the quantum-mechanical characterization}@} of {@{an [isolated](isolated%20system.md) physical system}@}. The equation was {@{postulated by Schrödinger based on a postulate of [Louis de Broglie](Louis%20de%20Broglie.md)}@} that all {@{matter has an associated [matter wave](matter%20wave.md)}@}. {@{The equation given by Schrödinger}@} is {@{non-relativistic}@} because it contains {@{a first derivative in time and a second derivative in space}@}, and therefore {@{space and time are not on equal footing}@}. {@{[Paul Dirac](Paul%20Dirac.md) incorporated [special relativity](special%20relativity.md) and quantum mechanics}@} into {@{a single formulation that simplifies to the Schrödinger equation in the non-relativistic limit}@}: {@{the [Dirac equation](Dirac%20equation.md)}@}, which contains {@{a single derivative in both space and time}@}. Another {@{[partial differential equation](partial%20differential%20equation.md), the [Klein–Gordon equation](Klein–Gordon%20equation.md)}@}, led to {@{a problem with probability density even though it was a [relativistic wave equation](relativistic%20wave%20equations.md)}@}. {@{The probability density}@} could be {@{negative, which is physically unviable}@}. This was fixed by {@{Dirac by introducing [Dirac matrices](gamma%20matrices.md)}@}. ### linearity

{@{The Schrödinger equation}@} is {@{a [linear differential equation](linear%20differential%20equation.md)}@}, meaning that if {@{two state vectors $|\psi _{1}\rangle$ and $|\psi _{2}\rangle$ are solutions}@}, then so is {@{any [linear combination](linear%20combination.md) $$|\psi \rangle =a|\psi _{1}\rangle +b|\psi _{2}\rangle$$ of the two state vectors}@} where {@{_a_ and _b_ are any complex numbers}@}.<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;25&hairsp;</sup> Moreover, {@{the sum can be extended}@} for {@{any number of state vectors}@}. This property allows {@{[superpositions of quantum states](quantum%20superposition.md) to be solutions of the Schrödinger equation}@}. Even more generally, it holds that {@{a general solution to the Schrödinger equation}@} can be found by taking {@{a weighted sum over a basis of states}@}. {@{A choice often employed}@} is {@{the basis of [energy](energy%20operator.md) eigenstates}@}, which are {@{solutions of the time-independent Schrödinger equation}@}. Holding {@{the Hamiltonian ${\hat {H} }$ constant}@}, {@{the Schrödinger equation has the solution}@}<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup> {@{$$|\Psi (t)\rangle =e^{-i{\hat {H} }t/\hbar }|\Psi (0)\rangle .$$}@} {@{The operator ${\hat {U} }(t)=e^{-i{\hat {H} }t/\hbar }$}@} is known as {@{the time-evolution operator}@}, and it is {@{[unitary](unitarity%20(physics).md)}@}: it {@{preserves the inner product between vectors in the Hilbert space}@}.<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup> {@{Unitarity}@} is {@{a general feature of time evolution under the Schrödinger equation}@}. If {@{the initial state is $|\Psi (0)\rangle$}@}, then {@{the state at a later time $t$}@} will be given by {@{$$|\Psi (t)\rangle ={\hat {U} }(t)|\Psi (0)\rangle$$}@} for {@{some unitary operator ${\hat {U} }(t)$}@}. Then ${\hat {U} }(t)$ depends upon {@{the parameter $t$ in such a way that $${\hat {U} }(t)=e^{-i{\hat {G} }t}$$}@} for some {@{self-adjoint operator ${\hat {G} }$, called the _generator_ of the family ${\hat {U} }(t)$}@}. {@{A Hamiltonian}@} is {@{just such a generator}@} \(up to the factor of {@{the Planck constant that would be set to 1 in [natural units](natural%20units.md)}@}\). ### Klein–Gordon and Dirac equations

Attempts to combine {@{quantum physics with special relativity}@} began with building {@{[relativistic wave equations](relativistic%20wave%20equations.md) from the relativistic [energy–momentum relation](energy–momentum%20relation.md)}@} {@{$$E^{2}=(pc)^{2}+\left(m_{0}c^{2}\right)^{2},$$}@} instead of {@{nonrelativistic energy equations}@}. {@{The [Klein–Gordon equation](Klein–Gordon%20equation.md) and the [Dirac equation](Dirac%20equation.md)}@} are {@{two such equations}@}. {@{The Klein–Gordon equation}@}, {@{$$-{\frac {1}{c^{2} } }{\frac {\partial ^{2} }{\partial t^{2} } }\psi +\nabla ^{2}\psi ={\frac {m^{2}c^{2} }{\hbar ^{2} } }\psi ,$$}@} was {@{the first such equation to be obtained}@}, even before {@{the nonrelativistic one-particle Schrödinger equation}@}, and applies to {@{massive spinless particles}@}. Historically, {@{Dirac obtained the Dirac equation}@} by seeking {@{a differential equation that would be first-order in both time and space}@}, {@{a desirable property for a relativistic theory}@}. However this required {@{a different form for the energy momentum relation}@}: {@{$$E=\beta mc^{2}+c\left(\sum _{n\mathop {=} 1}^{3}\alpha _{n}p_{n}\right)$$}@} where {@{the $\beta$ and $\alpha _{i}$ quantities are 4 × 4 matrices}@}. Following {@{[Max Planck](Max%20Planck.md)'s quantization of light \(see [black-body radiation](black-body%20radiation.md)\)}@}, {@{[Albert Einstein](Albert%20Einstein.md) interpreted Planck's [quanta](quantum.md)}@} to be {@{[photons](photon.md), [particles of light](corpuscular%20theory%20of%20light.md)}@}, and proposed that {@{the [energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency](Planck%20relation.md)}@}, one of {@{the first signs of [wave–particle duality](wave–particle%20duality.md)}@}. Since {@{energy and [momentum](momentum.md)}@} are related in {@{the same way as [frequency](frequency.md) and [wave number](wavenumber.md) in [special relativity](special%20relativity.md)}@}, it followed that {@{the momentum $p$ of a photon}@} is {@{inversely proportional to its [wavelength](wavelength.md) $\lambda$}@}, or proportional to its {@{wave number $k$: $$p={\frac {h}{\lambda } }=\hbar k,$$}@} where {@{$h$ is the [Planck constant](Planck%20constant.md) and $\hbar ={h}/{2\pi }$ is the reduced Planck constant}@}. {@{[Louis de Broglie](Louis%20de%20Broglie.md) hypothesized that this is true}@} for {@{all particles, even particles which have mass such as electrons}@}. He showed that, assuming that {@{the [matter waves](matter%20wave.md) propagate along with their particle counterparts}@}, {@{electrons form [standing waves](standing%20wave.md)}@}, meaning that only {@{certain discrete rotational frequencies about the nucleus of an atom}@} are allowed.<sup>[\[38\]](#^ref-38)</sup> {@{These quantized orbits}@} correspond to {@{discrete [energy levels](energy%20level.md)}@}, and de Broglie reproduced {@{the [Bohr model](Bohr%20model.md) formula for the energy levels}@}. {@{The Bohr model}@} was based on {@{the assumed quantization of angular momentum $L$}@} according to {@{$$L=n{\frac {h}{2\pi } }=n\hbar .$$}@} According to {@{de Broglie, the electron is described by a wave}@}, and {@{a whole number of wavelengths}@} must {@{fit along the circumference of the electron's orbit}@}: {@{$$n\lambda =2\pi r.$$}@} This approach essentially confined {@{the electron wave in one dimension}@}, along {@{a circular orbit of radius $r$}@}.
