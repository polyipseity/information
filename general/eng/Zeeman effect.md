{@{The __Zeeman effect__ \(Dutch: [\[ˈzeːmɑn\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch)\)}@} is {@{the splitting of a [spectral line](spectral%20line.md) into several components in the presence of a static [magnetic field](magnetic%20field.md)}@}. It is caused by {@{the interaction of the magnetic field with the [magnetic moments](magnetic%20moment.md) of the atomic [electrons](electron.md)}@} associated with {@{their [orbital motion](angular%20momentum.md) and [spin](spin%20(physics).md)}@}; {@{this interaction}@} shifts {@{some orbital energies more than others, resulting in the split spectrum}@}.

The effect is {@{named after the [Dutch](Netherlands.md) physicist [Pieter Zeeman](Pieter%20Zeeman.md)}@}, who {@{discovered it in 1896 and received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](Nobel%20Prize%20in%20Physics.md) for it in 1902}@}. It is {@{analogous to the [Stark effect](Stark%20effect.md)}@}, {@{the splitting of a spectral line into several components in the presence of an [electric field](electric%20field.md)}@}. Also, similar to {@{the Stark effect}@}, {@{transitions between different components}@} have, {@{in general, different intensities}@}, with {@{some being entirely forbidden \(in the [dipole](dipole.md) approximation\)}@}, as {@{governed by the [selection rules](selection%20rule.md)}@}.

Since {@{the distance between the Zeeman sub-levels}@} is {@{a function of magnetic field strength}@}, this effect can be {@{used to measure magnetic field strength}@}, e.g. that of {@{the [Sun](Sun.md) and other [stars](star.md) or in laboratory [plasmas](plasma%20(physics).md)}@}.

## discovery

In {@{1896, Zeeman learned that his laboratory}@} had {@{one of [Henry Augustus Rowland](Henry%20Augustus%20Rowland.md)'s highest resolving [diffraction gratings](diffraction%20grating.md)}@}. Zeeman had read {@{[James Clerk Maxwell](James%20Clerk%20Maxwell.md)'s article in _[Encyclopædia Britannica](Encyclopædia%20Britannica.md)_}@} describing {@{[Michael Faraday](Michael%20Faraday.md)'s failed attempts to influence light with magnetism}@}. Zeeman wondered if {@{the new spectrographic techniques could succeed where early efforts had not}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;75&hairsp;</sup>

When {@{illuminated by a slit-shaped source}@}, {@{the grating}@} produces {@{a long array of slit images corresponding to different wavelengths}@}. Zeeman placed {@{a piece of [asbestos](asbestos.md) soaked in salt water}@} into {@{a [Bunsen burner](Bunsen%20burner.md) flame at the source of the grating}@}: he could easily see {@{two lines in the [sodium](sodium.md) light [emission spectrum](emission%20spectrum.md)}@}. Switching on {@{a strong \(about one [tesla](tesla%20(unit).md)\) [electromagnet](electromagnet.md) around the flame}@}, he observed {@{a slight broadening of the lines}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;76&hairsp;</sup>

When Zeeman {@{switched to [cadmium](cadmium.md) as the source}@}, he observed {@{the spectral lines split when the magnetic field was applied}@}. {@{These splittings}@} could be {@{analyzed with [Hendrik Lorentz](Hendrik%20Lorentz.md)'s then-new [electron theory](Lorentz%20oscillator%20model.md)}@}. It is now known that {@{the magnetic effects on sodium require quantum-mechanical treatment}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;77&hairsp;</sup> {@{Zeeman and Lorentz}@} were {@{awarded the 1902 Nobel Prize}@}; in his {@{acceptance speech Zeeman explained his apparatus and showed slides of the spectrographic images}@}.<sup>[\[2\]](#^ref-2)</sup>

## nomenclature

<a id="anomalous"></a> Historically, one {@{distinguishes between the __normal__ and an __anomalous Zeeman effect__}@} \(discovered by {@{[Thomas Preston](Thomas%20Preston%20(scientist).md) in Dublin, Ireland}@}<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup>\). {@{The anomalous effect}@} appears on {@{transitions where the net [spin](spin%20(physics).md) of the [electrons](electron.md) is non-zero}@}. It was called {@{"anomalous" because the electron spin had not yet been discovered}@}, and so there was {@{no good explanation for it at the time that Zeeman observed the effect}@}. {@{[Wolfgang Pauli](Wolfgang%20Pauli.md) recalled}@} that when {@{asked by a colleague as to why he looked unhappy}@}, he replied: "How can one {@{look happy when he is thinking about the anomalous Zeeman effect}@}?"<sup>[\[4\]](#^ref-4)</sup>

At {@{higher magnetic field strength}@} {@{the effect ceases to be linear}@}. At {@{even higher field strengths, comparable to the strength of the atom's internal field}@}, {@{the electron coupling is disturbed and the spectral lines rearrange}@}. This is called {@{the [__Paschen–Back effect__](#strong%20field%20(Paschen–Back%20effect))}@}.

In {@{modern [scientific literature](scientific%20literature.md)}@}, these terms are {@{rarely used, with a tendency to use just the "Zeeman effect"}@}. {@{Another rarely used obscure term}@} is {@{__inverse Zeeman effect__}@},<sup>[\[5\]](#^ref-5)</sup> referring to {@{the Zeeman effect in an absorption spectral line}@}.

{@{A similar effect}@}, {@{splitting of the nuclear energy levels in the presence of a magnetic field}@}, is referred to as {@{the __nuclear Zeeman effect__}@}.<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup>

## theoretical presentation

{@{The total [Hamiltonian](Hamiltonian%20(quantum%20mechanics).md)}@} of {@{an atom in a magnetic field}@} is {@{$$H=H_{0}+V_{\text{M} },$$}@} where $H_{0}$ is {@{the unperturbed Hamiltonian of the atom}@}, and $V_{\text{M} }$ is {@{the [perturbation](perturbation%20theory.md) due to the magnetic field}@}: {@{$$V_{\text{M} }=-{\vec {\mu } }\cdot {\vec {B} },$$}@} where ${\vec {\mu } }$ is {@{the [magnetic moment](magnetic%20moment.md) of the atom}@}. {@{The magnetic moment}@} consists of {@{the electronic and nuclear parts}@}; however, {@{the latter is many orders of magnitude smaller and will be neglected here}@}. Therefore, {@{$${\vec {\mu } }\approx -{\frac {\mu _{\text{B} }g{\vec {J} } }{\hbar } },$$}@} where $\mu _{\text{B} }$ is {@{the [Bohr magneton](Bohr%20magneton.md)}@}, ${\vec {J} }$ is {@{the total electronic [angular momentum](angular%20momentum.md)}@}, and $g$ is {@{the [Landé g-factor](Landé%20g-factor.md)}@}.

{@{A more accurate approach}@} is to take into account that {@{the operator of the magnetic moment of an electron}@} is {@{a sum of the contributions of the [orbital angular momentum](angular%20momentum%20operator.md) ${\vec {L} }$ and the [spin angular momentum](angular%20momentum%20operator.md) ${\vec {S} }$}@}, with each {@{multiplied by the appropriate [gyromagnetic ratio](gyromagnetic%20ratio.md)}@}: {@{$${\vec {\mu } }=-{\frac {\mu _{\text{B} }(g_{l}{\vec {L} }+g_{s}{\vec {S} })}{\hbar } },$$}@} where {@{$g_{l}=1$, and $g_{s}\approx 2.0023193$}@} \({@{the [anomalous gyromagnetic ratio](anomalous%20magnetic%20dipole%20moment.md), deviating from 2}@} due to {@{the effects of [quantum electrodynamics](quantum%20electrodynamics.md)}@}\). In the case of {@{the [LS coupling](LS%20coupling.md#LS%20coupling)}@}, one can {@{sum over all electrons in the atom}@}: {@{$$g{\vec {J} }={\Big \langle }\sum _{i}(g_{l}{\vec {l} }_{i}+g_{s}{\vec {s} }_{i}){\Big \rangle }={\big \langle }(g_{l}{\vec {L} }+g_{s}{\vec {S} }){\big \rangle },$$}@} where {@{${\vec {L} }$ and ${\vec {S} }$}@} are {@{the total spin momentum and spin of the atom}@}, and averaging is done over {@{a state with a given value of the total angular momentum}@}.

If {@{the interaction term $V_{\text{M} }$ is small \(less than the [fine structure](fine%20structure.md)\)}@}, it can be {@{treated as a perturbation}@}; this is {@{the Zeeman effect proper}@}. In {@{the Paschen–Back effect, described below}@}, $V_{\text{M} }$ {@{exceeds the [LS coupling](LS%20coupling.md#LS%20coupling) significantly \(but is still small compared to $H_{0}$\)}@}. In {@{ultra-strong magnetic fields}@}, {@{the magnetic-field interaction may exceed $H_{0}$}@}, in which case {@{the atom can no longer exist in its normal meaning}@}, and one talks about {@{[Landau levels](Landau%20level.md#Landau%20levels) instead}@}. There are {@{intermediate cases that are more complex than these limit cases}@}.

## weak field \(Zeeman effect\)

If {@{the [spin–orbit interaction](spin–orbit%20interaction.md)}@} dominates {@{over the effect of the external magnetic field}@}, {@{${\vec {L} }$ and ${\vec {S} }$ are not separately conserved}@}, only {@{the total angular momentum ${\vec {J} }={\vec {L} }+{\vec {S} }$ is}@}. {@{The spin and orbital angular momentum vectors}@} can be thought of as {@{[precessing](precession.md) about the \(fixed\) total angular momentum vector ${\vec {J} }$}@}. {@{The \(time-\)"averaged" spin vector}@} is then {@{the projection of the spin onto the direction of ${\vec {J} }$}@}: $${\vec {S} }_{\text{avg} }={\frac {({\vec {S} }\cdot {\vec {J} })}{J^{2} } }{\vec {J} },$$ and for {@{the \(time-\)"averaged" orbital vector}@}: {@{$${\vec {L} }_{\text{avg} }={\frac {({\vec {L} }\cdot {\vec {J} })}{J^{2} } }{\vec {J} }.$$}@} (annotation: Because both {@{$\vec{L}$ and $\vec{S}$ precess rapidly around the fixed total angular momentum vector $\vec{J}$}@}, their {@{perpendicular components average to zero ($\langle\vec{L}_{\perp}\rangle = 0$, $\langle\vec{S}_{\perp}\rangle = 0$)}@}, leaving {@{only their parallel components along $\vec{J}$ as the vector projections}@} {@{$\vec{L}_{\text{avg} } = \frac{\vec{L} \cdot \vec{J} }{J^2}\vec{J}$ and $\vec{S}_{\text{avg} } = \frac{\vec{S} \cdot \vec{J} }{J^2}\vec{J}$}@}.) Thus {@{$$\langle V_{\text{M} }\rangle ={\frac {\mu _{\text{B} } }{\hbar } }{\vec {J} }\left(g_{L}{\frac { {\vec {L} }\cdot {\vec {J} } }{J^{2} } }+g_{S}{\frac { {\vec {S} }\cdot {\vec {J} } }{J^{2} } }\right)\cdot {\vec {B} }.$$}@} Using {@{${\vec {L} }={\vec {J} }-{\vec {S} }$ and squaring both sides}@}, (annotation: Geometrically, viewing {@{the vector relation $\vec{L} = \vec{J} - \vec{S}$ as a closed triangle}@} allows {@{the law of cosines $L^2 = J^2 + S^2 - 2JS\cos\theta$ to be written directly in dot product form}@} as {@{$L^2 = (\vec{J} - \vec{S}) \cdot (\vec{J} - \vec{S}) = J^2 + S^2 - 2\vec{J} \cdot \vec{S}$}@}, expressing {@{the relative orientation of the angular momenta}@} through {@{scalar products of the side lengths rather than complex matrix expansions}@}.) we get {@{$${\vec {S} }\cdot {\vec {J} }={\frac {1}{2} }(J^{2}+S^{2}-L^{2})={\frac {\hbar ^{2} }{2} }[j(j+1)-l(l+1)+s(s+1)],$$}@} and using {@{${\vec {S} }={\vec {J} }-{\vec {L} }$ and squaring both sides}@}, we get {@{$${\vec {L} }\cdot {\vec {J} }={\frac {1}{2} }(J^{2}-S^{2}+L^{2})={\frac {\hbar ^{2} }{2} }[j(j+1)+l(l+1)-s(s+1)].$$}@} (annotation: The appearance of {@{$\hbar^2 x(x+1)$ instead of a simple squared value $x^2$}@} for {@{$J^2$, $L^2$, and $S^2$}@} happens because {@{quantum angular momentum components do not commute}@}, meaning {@{these squared operators yield discrete quantized values}@} of {@{$\hbar^2 j(j+1)$, $\hbar^2 l(l+1)$, and $\hbar^2 s(s+1)$}@} derived from {@{quantum step rules rather than simple classical vector squares}@}.)

Combining {@{everything and taking $J_{z}=\hbar m_{j}$}@}, (annotation: This aligns {@{the coordinate $z$-axis with the external magnetic field vector $\vec{B}$}@} to project {@{the angular momentum onto the field direction}@}.) we obtain {@{the magnetic [potential energy](potential%20energy.md) of the atom in the applied external magnetic field}@}: {@{$${\begin{aligned}V_{\text{M} }&=\mu _{\text{B} }Bm_{j}\left[g_{L}{\frac {j(j+1)+l(l+1)-s(s+1)}{2j(j+1)} }+g_{S}{\frac {j(j+1)-l(l+1)+s(s+1)}{2j(j+1)} }\right]\\&=\mu _{\text{B} }Bm_{j}\left[1+(g_{S}-1){\frac {j(j+1)-l(l+1)+s(s+1)}{2j(j+1)} }\right]\\&=\mu _{\text{B} }Bm_{j}g_{J},\end{aligned} }$$}@} where {@{the quantity in square brackets}@} is {@{the [Landé g-factor](Landé%20g-factor.md) $g_{J}$ of the atom}@} \({@{$g_{L}=1$, $g_{S}\approx 2$}@}\), and $m_{j}$ is {@{the _z_ component of the total angular momentum}@}.

(annotation: Recall $g_J$: {@{$g_J = 1+(g_{S}-1){\frac {j(j+1)-l(l+1)+s(s+1)}{2j(j+1)} }$}@}.) For {@{a single electron above filled shells}@}, with {@{$s=1/2$ and $j=l\pm s$}@}, {@{the Landé g-factor}@} can be simplified to {@{$$g_{J}=1\pm {\frac {g_{S}-1}{2l+1} }.$$}@}

Taking {@{$V_{\text{M} }$ to be the perturbation}@}, {@{the Zeeman correction to the energy}@} is {@{$$E_{\text{Z} }^{(1)}=\langle nljm_{j}|H_{\text{Z} }^{'}|nljm_{j}\rangle =\langle V_{\text{M} }\rangle _{\Psi }=\mu _{\text{B} }g_{J}B_{\text{ext} }m_{j}.$$}@} (annotation: {@{The principal quantum number $n$}@} determines {@{the energy shell and size of the orbital}@}, {@{the orbital quantum number $l$}@} specifies {@{its angular momentum magnitude and shape}@}, {@{the total angular momentum quantum number $j$}@} defines {@{the combined vector sum of spin and orbital motion}@}, and {@{the magnetic projection quantum number $m_j$}@} specifies {@{the orientation of this total angular momentum along the external magnetic field axis}@}.)

### example: Lyman-alpha transition in hydrogen

{@{The [Lyman-alpha transition](Lyman%20alpha.md) in [hydrogen](hydrogen.md)}@} in {@{the presence of the [spin–orbit interaction](spin–orbit%20interaction.md)}@} involves {@{the transitions $2{\,}^{2}\!P_{1/2}\to 1{\,}^{2}\!S_{1/2}$}@} (annotation: Using {@{the spectroscopic notation $n^{2S+1}L_j$}@} (where {@{$n$ is the principal quantum number}@}, {@{$2S+1$ is the spin multiplicity}@}, {@{$L$ is the orbital letter}@}, and {@{$j$ is the total angular momentum}@}), this represents {@{a radiative decay from the $2P_{1/2}$ fine-structure energy eigenstate}@} ({@{$n=2, \ell=1, j=1/2$}@}) to {@{the ground $1S_{1/2}$ eigenstate}@} ({@{$n=1, \ell=0, j=1/2$}@}).) and {@{$2{\,}^{2}\!P_{3/2}\to 1{\,}^{2}\!S_{1/2}$}@}. (annotation: In {@{the same $n^{2S+1}L_j$ framework}@}, this transition denotes {@{a decay from the higher-energy $2P_{3/2}$ fine-structure eigenstate}@} ({@{$n=2, \ell=1, j=3/2$}@}) to {@{the ground $1S_{1/2}$ state}@} ({@{$n=1, \ell=0, j=1/2$}@}).)

In {@{the presence of an external magnetic field}@}, {@{the weak-field Zeeman effect}@} splits {@{the $1{\,}^{2}\!S_{1/2}$ and $2{\,}^{2}\!P_{1/2}$ levels}@} into {@{2 states each \($m_{j}=+1/2,-1/2$\)}@} and {@{the $2{\,}^{2}\!P_{3/2}$ level}@} into {@{4 states \($m_{j}=+3/2,+1/2,-1/2,-3/2$\)}@}. {@{The Landé g-factors for the three levels}@} are {@{$${\begin{aligned}g_{J}&=2&&{\text{for} }\ 1{\,}^{2}\!S_{1/2}\ (j=1/2,l=0),\\g_{J}&=2/3&&{\text{for} }\ 2{\,}^{2}\!P_{1/2}\ (j=1/2,l=1),\\g_{J}&=4/3&&{\text{for} }\ 2{\,}^{2}\!P_{3/2}\ (j=3/2,l=1).\end{aligned} }$$}@} (annotation: Recall {@{the simplified Landé g-factor formula for a single electron above filled shells}@}: {@{$g_{J}=1\pm {\frac {g_{S}-1}{2l+1} }$}@}.)

> {@{![Zeeman splitting of a P-S doublet transition.](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Zeeman%20p%20s%20doublet.svg)}@}

Note in particular that {@{the size of the energy splitting}@} is {@{different for the different orbitals}@} because {@{the _g<sub>J</sub>_ values are different}@}. {@{Fine-structure splitting occurs}@} even in {@{the absence of a magnetic field, as it is due to spin–orbit coupling}@}. {@{Depicted on the right}@} is {@{the additional Zeeman splitting}@}, which occurs in {@{the presence of magnetic fields}@}.

## strong field \(Paschen–Back effect\)

{@{The Paschen–Back effect}@} is {@{the splitting of atomic energy levels in the presence of a strong magnetic field}@}. This occurs when {@{an external magnetic field is sufficiently strong}@} to {@{disrupt the coupling between orbital \(${\vec {L} }$\) and spin \(${\vec {S} }$\) angular momenta}@}. This effect is {@{the strong-field limit of the Zeeman effect}@}. When {@{$s=0$}@}, {@{the two effects are equivalent}@}. The effect was named after {@{the German physicists [Friedrich Paschen](Friedrich%20Paschen.md) and [Ernst E. A. Back](Ernst%20Emil%20Alexander%20Back.md)}@}.<sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup>

When {@{the magnetic-field perturbation significantly exceeds the spin–orbit interaction}@}, one can {@{safely assume $[H_{0},S]=0$}@}. (annotation: {@{Physical interpretation}@} is that {@{the spin angular momentum}@} is {@{no longer coupled to the orbital angular momentum}@}, and thus {@{the spin is a conserved quantity}@} in {@{the presence of the strong magnetic field}@}.)  This allows {@{the expectation values of $L_{z}$ and $S_{z}$}@} to be {@{easily evaluated for a state $|\psi \rangle$}@}. {@{The energies}@} are simply {@{$$E_{z}=\left\langle \psi \left|H_{0}+{\frac {B_{z}\mu _{\rm {B} } }{\hbar } }(L_{z}+g_{s}S_{z})\right|\psi \right\rangle =E_{0}+B_{z}\mu _{\rm {B} }(m_{l}+g_{s}m_{s}).$$}@} The above may be read as {@{implying that the LS-coupling is completely broken by the external field}@}. However, {@{$m_{l}$ and $m_{s}$}@} are {@{still "good" quantum numbers}@}. (annotation: They respectively represent {@{the magnetic quantum numbers}@} for {@{the orbital and spin angular momenta}@}.) Together with {@{the [selection rules](selection%20rule.md) for an [electric dipole transition](electric%20dipole%20transition.md)}@}, i.e., {@{$\Delta s=0,\Delta m_{s}=0,\Delta l=\pm 1,\Delta m_{l}=0,\pm 1$}@} this allows to {@{ignore the spin degree of freedom altogether}@}. As a result, only {@{three spectral lines will be visible}@}, corresponding to {@{the $\Delta m_{l}=0,\pm 1$ selection rule}@}. {@{The splitting $\Delta E=B\mu _{\rm {B} }\Delta m_{l}$}@} is {@{_independent_ of the unperturbed energies and electronic configurations of the levels being considered}@}.

More precisely, if {@{$s\neq 0$}@}, {@{each of these three components}@} is {@{actually a group of several transitions}@} due to {@{the residual spin–orbit coupling and relativistic corrections}@} \(which are {@{of the same order, known as 'fine structure'}@}\). {@{The first-order perturbation theory with these corrections}@} yields {@{the following formula for the hydrogen atom in the Paschen–Back limit}@}:<sup>[\[8\]](#^ref-8)</sup> {@{$$E_{z+fs}=E_{z}+{\frac {m_{e}c^{2}\alpha ^{4} }{2n^{3} } }\left\{ {\frac {3}{4n} }-\left[{\frac {l(l+1)-m_{l}m_{s} }{l(l+1/2)(l+1)} }\right]\right\}.$$}@} (annotation: In {@{the strong-field Paschen-Back limit}@}, the magnetic field {@{decouples orbital and spin angular momenta}@}, reducing {@{the spectrum to three primary lines}@}. This paragraph adds {@{residual spin-orbit and relativistic fine-structure corrections to this strong-field model}@}, showing that {@{each primary line}@} actually {@{splits into a tight cluster of sub-lines determined by $E_{z+fs}$}@}.)

## intermediate field for j = 1/2

In {@{the magnetic dipole approximation}@}, {@{the Hamiltonian which includes both the [hyperfine](hyperfine%20structure.md) and Zeeman interactions}@} is<sup>\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation%20needed)_\]</sup> <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$$H=hA{\vec {I} }\cdot {\vec {J} }-{\vec {\mu } }\cdot {\vec {B} }$$}@} <br/> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$$H=hA{\vec {I} }\cdot {\vec {J} }+(\mu _{\rm {B} }g_{J}{\vec {J} }+\mu _{\rm {N} }g_{I}{\vec {I} })\cdot {\vec {\rm {B} } }$$}@} (annotation: {@{The first term}@} represents {@{the hyperfine interaction}@} while {@{the second term}@} represents {@{the Zeeman interaction}@}.) <p> where $A$ is {@{the hyperfine splitting at zero applied magnetic field}@}, {@{$\mu _{\rm {B} }$ and $\mu _{\rm {N} }$}@} are {@{the [Bohr magneton](Bohr%20magneton.md) and [nuclear magneton](nuclear%20magneton.md)}@}, respectively \(note that {@{the last term in the expression}@} above describes {@{the _nuclear_ Zeeman effect}@}\), {@{${\vec {J} }$ and ${\vec {I} }$}@} are {@{the electron and nuclear angular momentum operators}@} and {@{$g_{J}$}@} is {@{the [Landé g-factor](Landé%20g-factor.md)}@}: {@{$$g_{J}=g_{L}{\frac {J(J+1)+L(L+1)-S(S+1)}{2J(J+1)} }+g_{S}{\frac {J(J+1)-L(L+1)+S(S+1)}{2J(J+1)} }.$$}@} In {@{the case of weak magnetic fields}@}, {@{the Zeeman interaction}@} can be treated as {@{a perturbation to the $|F,m_{f}\rangle$ basis}@}. (annotation: This basis is described by {@{the total angular momentum $F = \vec{J} + \vec{I}$}@} formed by {@{combining the electron angular momentum $\vec{J}$ and the nuclear spin $\vec{I}$}@}, with {@{$m_f$}@} representing {@{the magnetic quantum number for its z-axis projection}@}.) In {@{the high field regime}@}, {@{the magnetic field}@} becomes {@{so strong that the Zeeman effect will dominate}@}, and one must use {@{a more complete basis of $|I,J,m_{I},m_{J}\rangle$}@} or just {@{$|m_{I},m_{J}\rangle$ since $I$ and $J$ will be constant within a given level}@}. (annotation: {@{The nuclear spin $I$}@} is {@{an invariant property of the nucleus}@} and {@{the electronic angular momentum $J$}@} is {@{fixed by the specific energy level}@}.)

To get {@{the complete picture, including intermediate field strengths}@}, we must consider {@{eigenstates which are superpositions of the $|F,m_{F}\rangle$ and $|m_{I},m_{J}\rangle$ basis states}@}. For {@{$J=1/2$}@}, {@{the Hamiltonian can be solved analytically}@}, resulting in {@{the __Breit–Rabi formula__ \(named after [Gregory Breit](Gregory%20Breit.md) and [Isidor Isaac Rabi](Isidor%20Isaac%20Rabi.md)\)}@}. Notably, {@{the electric quadrupole interaction is zero}@} for {@{$L=0$ \($J=1/2$\)}@}, so {@{this formula is fairly accurate}@}.

We now utilize {@{quantum mechanical [ladder operators](ladder%20operator.md)}@}, which are defined for {@{a general angular [momentum operator](momentum%20operator.md) $L$}@} as {@{$$L_{\pm }\equiv L_{x}\pm iL_{y}$$}@} {@{These ladder operators}@} have the property {@{$$L_{\pm }|L_{,}m_{L}\rangle ={\sqrt {(L\mp m_{L})(L\pm m_{L}+1)} }|L,m_{L}\pm 1\rangle$$}@} as long as {@{$m_{L}$ lies in the range ${-L,\dots ...,L}$ \(otherwise, they return zero\)}@}. Using {@{ladder operators $J_{\pm }$ and $I_{\pm }$}@} We can {@{rewrite the Hamiltonian}@} as {@{$$H=hAI_{z}J_{z}+{\frac {hA}{2} }(J_{+}I_{-}+J_{-}I_{+})+\mu _{\rm {B} }Bg_{J}J_{z}+\mu _{\rm {N} }Bg_{I}I_{z}$$}@} (annotation: Expanding {@{the dot product as $\vec{I} \cdot \vec{J} = I_x J_x + I_y J_y + I_z J_z$}@} and expressing {@{the transverse operators via ladder definitions $J_\pm = J_x \pm i J_y$ and $I_\pm = I_x \pm i I_y$}@}, we evaluate {@{$J_+ I_- + J_- I_+ = (J_x + i J_y)(I_x - i I_y) + (J_x - i J_y)(I_x + i I_y) = 2(J_x I_x + J_y I_y)$}@} because {@{electron ($\vec{J}$) and nuclear ($\vec{I}$) spin operators commute with each other}@}. Substituting {@{$J_x I_x + J_y I_y = \frac{1}{2}(J_+ I_- + J_- I_+)$ into $\vec{I} \cdot \vec{J}$}@} and adding {@{the $z$-axis Zeeman interactions $\mu_{\mathrm{B} } B g_J J_z + \mu_{\mathrm{N} } B g_I I_z$}@} directly yields {@{$H = hA I_z J_z + \frac{hA}{2}(J_+ I_- + J_- I_+) + \mu_{\mathrm{B} } B g_J J_z + \mu_{\mathrm{N} } B g_I I_z$}@}.) We can now see that {@{at all times, the total angular momentum projection $m_{F}=m_{J}+m_{I}$ will be conserved}@}. This is because {@{both $J_{z}$ and $I_{z}$}@} leave {@{states with definite $m_{J}$ and $m_{I}$ unchanged}@}, while {@{$J_{+}I_{-}$ and $J_{-}I_{+}$}@} {@{either increase $m_{J}$ and decrease $m_{I}$ or vice versa}@}, so {@{the sum is always unaffected}@}. (annotation: Because {@{the Schrödinger equation}@} dictates that {@{the Hamiltonian governs the time evolution of the system}@}, {@{inspecting the Hamiltonian}@} gives the above conclusions.) Furthermore, since {@{$J=1/2$}@} there are {@{only two possible values of $m_{J}$ which are $\pm 1/2$}@}. Therefore, for every {@{value of $m_{F}$ there are only two possible states}@}, and we can {@{define them as the basis}@}: {@{$$|\pm \rangle \equiv |m_{J}=\pm 1/2,m_{I}=m_{F}\mp 1/2\rangle$$}@} {@{This pair of states}@} is {@{a [two-level quantum mechanical system](two-level%20quantum%20mechanical%20system.md)}@}. Now we can determine {@{the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian}@}: <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$$\langle \pm |H|\pm \rangle =-{\frac {1}{4} }hA+\mu _{\rm {N} }Bg_{I}m_{F}\pm {\frac {1}{2} }(hAm_{F}+\mu _{\rm {B} }Bg_{J}-\mu _{\rm {N} }Bg_{I}))$$}@} <br/> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$$\langle \pm |H|\mp \rangle ={\frac {1}{2} }hA{\sqrt {(I+1/2)^{2}-m_{F}^{2} } }$$}@} <p> Solving for {@{the eigenvalues of this matrix}@} – as can be done {@{by hand \(see [two-level quantum mechanical system](two-level%20quantum%20mechanical%20system.md)\), or more easily, with a computer algebra system}@} – we arrive at {@{the energy shifts}@}: <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$$\Delta E_{F=I\pm 1/2}=-{\frac {h\Delta W}{2(2I+1)} }+\mu _{\rm {N} }g_{I}m_{F}B\pm {\frac {h\Delta W}{2} }{\sqrt {1+{\frac {2m_{F}x}{I+1/2} }+x^{2} } }$$}@} <br/> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{$$x\equiv {\frac {B(\mu _{\rm {B} }g_{J}-\mu _{\rm {N} }g_{I})}{h\Delta W} }\quad \quad \Delta W=A\left(I+{\frac {1}{2} }\right)$$}@} <p> where {@{$\Delta W$}@} is {@{the splitting \(in units of Hz\) between two hyperfine sublevels in the absence of magnetic field $B$}@}, $x$ is referred to as {@{the 'field strength parameter'}@} \(Note: for {@{$m_{F}=\pm (I+1/2)$}@} {@{the expression under the square root}@} is {@{an exact square}@}, and so {@{the last term should be replaced}@} by {@{$+{\frac {h\Delta W}{2} }(1\pm x)$}@}\). This equation is known as {@{the __Breit–Rabi formula__}@} and is useful for {@{systems with one [valence electron](valence%20electron.md) in an $s$ \($J=1/2$\) level}@}.<sup>[\[9\]](#^ref-9)</sup><sup>[\[10\]](#^ref-10)</sup>

Note that {@{index $F$ in $\Delta E_{F=I\pm 1/2}$}@} should be considered {@{not as total angular momentum of the atom but as _asymptotic total angular momentum_}@}. It is {@{equal to total angular momentum only if $B=0$}@} otherwise {@{eigenvectors corresponding different eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian}@} are {@{the superpositions of states with different $F$ but equal $m_{F}$}@} \({@{the only exceptions}@} are {@{$|F=I+1/2,m_{F}=\pm F\rangle$}@}\). (annotation: When {@{$B = 0$}@}, the Hamiltonian contains {@{only the hyperfine term $\vec{I} \cdot \vec{J} = \frac{1}{2}(\vec{F}^2 - \vec{I}^2 - \vec{J}^2)$}@}, which {@{commutes with $\vec{F}^2$ and makes $F$ a strictly conserved quantum number}@}. When {@{$B \neq 0$}@}, {@{the added Zeeman interaction ($\mu_{\mathrm{B} } g_J J_z B + \mu_{\mathrm{N} } g_I I_z B$)}@} {@{does not commute with $\vec{F}^2$}@}, so $F$ is {@{no longer conserved and serves only as an asymptotic label}@}. Consequently, eigenstates are {@{quantum superpositions of different $F$ values sharing the same magnetic projection $m_F$}@} (such as {@{$\vert{}\psi\rangle = c_1 \vert{}I + 1/2, m_F\rangle + c_2 \vert{}I - 1/2, m_F\rangle$}@}), except for {@{pure stretched states like $\vert{}I + 1/2, m_F = \pm(I + 1/2)\rangle$}@}, which remain {@{unmixed because no other states share their maximum $m_F$ projection}@}.)

## applications

### astrophysics

> {@{![Magnetic field of sunspot \(left\) splits absorption line according to Zeeman effect \(right\).](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Sunzeeman1919.png)}@}
>
> {@{Zeeman effect}@} on {@{a sunspot spectral line}@}

{@{[George Ellery Hale](George%20Ellery%20Hale.md) was the first}@} to notice {@{the Zeeman effect in the solar spectra}@}, indicating {@{the existence of strong magnetic fields in sunspots}@}. Such fields can be {@{quite high, on the order of 0.1 [tesla](tesla%20(unit).md) or higher}@}. Today, {@{the Zeeman effect is used}@} to produce {@{[magnetograms](solar%20magnetogram.md) showing the variation of magnetic field on the Sun}@},<sup>[\[11\]](#^ref-11)</sup> and to analyze {@{the magnetic field geometries in other stars}@}.<sup>[\[12\]](#^ref-12)</sup>

### laser cooling

{@{The Zeeman effect is utilized}@} in {@{many [laser cooling](laser%20cooling.md) applications}@} such as {@{a [magneto-optical trap](magneto-optical%20trap.md) and the [Zeeman slower](Zeeman%20slower.md)}@}.<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup>

### spintronics

{@{Zeeman-energy mediated coupling of spin and orbital motions}@} is used in {@{[spintronics](spintronics.md) for controlling electron spins}@} in {@{quantum dots through [electric dipole spin resonance](electric%20dipole%20spin%20resonance.md)}@}.<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup>

### metrology

{@{Old high-precision frequency standards}@}, i.e. {@{hyperfine structure transition-based atomic clocks}@}, may require {@{periodic fine-tuning due to exposure to magnetic fields}@}. This is carried out by measuring {@{the Zeeman effect on specific hyperfine structure transition levels of the source element \(cesium\)}@} and applying {@{a uniformly precise, low-strength magnetic field to said source}@}, in a process known as {@{[degaussing](degaussing.md)}@}.<sup>[\[15\]](#^ref-15)</sup>

{@{The Zeeman effect may also be utilized}@} to improve {@{accuracy in [atomic absorption spectroscopy](atomic%20absorption%20spectroscopy.md)}@}.<sup>\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation%20needed)_\]</sup>

### biology

{@{A theory about the [magnetic sense](magnetic%20sense.md) of birds}@} assumes that {@{a protein in the retina}@} is {@{changed due to the Zeeman effect}@}.<sup>[\[16\]](#^ref-16)</sup>

### nuclear spectroscopy

{@{The nuclear Zeeman effect}@} is important in such applications as {@{[nuclear magnetic resonance](nuclear%20magnetic%20resonance.md) spectroscopy, [magnetic resonance imaging](magnetic%20resonance%20imaging.md) \(MRI\), and [Mössbauer spectroscopy](Mössbauer%20spectroscopy.md)}@}.<sup>\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation%20needed)_\]</sup>

### other

{@{The [electron spin resonance](electron%20spin%20resonance.md) spectroscopy}@} is based on {@{the Zeeman effect}@}.<sup>\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation%20needed)_\]</sup>

## demonstrations

> {@{![A sodium vapor lamp viewed through a sodium vapor will appear dark unless a powerful electromagnet around the sodium vapor is energized. The change is caused by the Zeeman effect on the sodium atoms.](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Zeeman%20effect%20demo.svg)}@}
>
> Diagram of {@{a Zeeman effect demonstration}@}

{@{The Zeeman effect can be demonstrated}@} by placing {@{a sodium vapor source in a powerful [electromagnet](electromagnet.md)}@} and viewing {@{a sodium vapor lamp through the magnet opening}@} \(see diagram\). With {@{magnet off}@}, {@{the sodium vapor source will block the lamp light}@}; when {@{the magnet is turned on}@} {@{the lamp light will be visible through the vapor}@}.

{@{The sodium vapor}@} can be created by {@{sealing sodium metal in an evacuated glass tube}@} and {@{heating it while the tube is in the magnet}@}.<sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup>

Alternatively, {@{salt \([sodium chloride](sodium%20chloride.md)\) on a ceramic stick}@} can be {@{placed in the flame of [Bunsen burner](Bunsen%20burner.md) as the sodium vapor source}@}. When {@{the magnetic field is energized}@}, {@{the lamp image will be brighter}@}.<sup>[\[18\]](#^ref-18)</sup> However, {@{the magnetic field also affects the flame}@}, making {@{the observation depend upon more than just the Zeeman effect}@}.<sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup> These issues also {@{plagued Zeeman's original work}@}; he devoted {@{considerable effort to ensure his observations were truly an effect of magnetism on light emission}@}.<sup>[\[19\]](#^ref-19)</sup>

When {@{salt is added to the Bunsen burner}@}, it {@{[dissociates](dissociation%20(chemistry).md) to give [sodium](sodium.md) and [chloride](chloride.md)}@}. {@{The sodium atoms get excited}@} due to {@{[photons](photon.md) from the sodium vapour lamp}@}, with electrons {@{excited from 3s to 3p states, absorbing light in the process}@}. {@{The sodium vapour lamp}@} emits {@{light at 589nm, which has precisely the energy to excite an electron of a sodium atom}@}. If it was {@{an atom of another element, like chlorine}@}, {@{shadow will not be formed}@}.<sup>[\[20\]](#^ref-20)</sup><sup>\[_[failed verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability)_\]</sup> When {@{a magnetic field is applied}@}, due to {@{the Zeeman effect the [spectral line](spectral%20line.md) of sodium}@} gets {@{split into several components}@}. This means {@{the energy difference between the 3s and 3p [atomic orbitals](atomic%20orbital.md) will change}@}. As {@{the sodium vapour lamp don't precisely deliver the right frequency anymore}@}, light {@{doesn't get absorbed and passes through}@}, resulting in {@{the shadow dimming}@}. As {@{the magnetic field strength is increased}@}, {@{the shift in the spectral lines increases}@} and {@{lamp light is transmitted}@}.<sup>\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation%20needed)_\]</sup>
