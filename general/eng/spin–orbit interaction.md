In {@{[quantum mechanics](quantum%20mechanics.md)}@}, {@{the __spin–orbit interaction__ \(also called __spin–orbit effect__ or __spin–orbit coupling__\)}@} is {@{a [relativistic](special%20relativity.md) interaction of a particle's [spin](spin%20(physics).md) with its motion inside a [potential](potential%20well.md)}@}. {@{A key example of this phenomenon}@} is {@{the spin–orbit interaction}@} leading to {@{shifts in an [electron](electron.md)'s [atomic energy levels](energy%20level.md)}@}, due to {@{electromagnetic interaction between the electron's [magnetic dipole](magnetic%20dipole.md), its orbital motion}@}, and {@{the electrostatic field of the positively charged [nucleus](atomic%20nucleus.md)}@}. {@{This phenomenon is detectable}@} as {@{a splitting of [spectral lines](spectral%20line.md)}@}, which can be {@{thought of as a [Zeeman effect](Zeeman%20effect.md) product of two effects}@}: {@{the apparent magnetic field}@} seen from {@{the electron perspective due to special relativity}@} and {@{the magnetic moment of the electron}@} associated with {@{its intrinsic spin due to quantum mechanics}@}.

For {@{atoms}@}, {@{energy level splitting produced by the spin–orbit interaction}@} is usually of {@{the same order in size as the relativistic corrections to the [kinetic energy](kinetic%20energy.md#relativistic%20kinetic%20energy%20of%20rigid%20bodies) and the [zitterbewegung](Zitterbewegung.md) effect}@}. {@{The addition of these three corrections}@} is known as {@{the [fine structure](fine%20structure.md)}@}. {@{The interaction between the magnetic field created by the electron}@} and {@{the magnetic moment of the nucleus}@} is {@{a slighter correction to the energy levels known as the [hyperfine structure](hyperfine%20structure.md)}@}.

{@{A similar effect}@}, due to {@{the relationship between [angular momentum](angular%20momentum.md) and the [strong nuclear force](strong%20nuclear%20force.md)}@}, occurs for {@{[protons](proton.md) and [neutrons](neutron.md) moving inside the nucleus}@}, leading to {@{a shift in their energy levels in the [nuclear shell model](nuclear%20shell%20model.md)}@}. In {@{the field of [spintronics](spintronics.md)}@}, {@{spin–orbit effects for electrons}@} in {@{[semiconductors](semiconductor.md) and other materials}@} are {@{explored for technological applications}@}. {@{The spin–orbit interaction}@} is at the origin of {@{[magnetocrystalline anisotropy](magnetocrystalline%20anisotropy.md) and the [spin Hall effect](spin%20Hall%20effect.md)}@}.

{@{The interaction}@} was first {@{introduced by [Llewellyn Thomas](Llewellyn%20Thomas.md) in 1926}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup>

## in atomic energy levels

> {@{![diagram of atomic energy levels](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Fine%20hyperfine%20levels.svg)}@}
>
> {@{Fine and hyperfine structure}@} in {@{hydrogen}@} \(not to scale\).

This section presents {@{a relatively simple and quantitative description of the spin–orbit interaction}@} for {@{an electron bound to a [hydrogen-like atom](hydrogen-like%20atom.md)}@}, up to {@{first order in [perturbation theory](perturbation%20theory%20(quantum%20mechanics).md)}@}, using some {@{[semiclassical](semiclassical%20physics.md) [electrodynamics](electrodynamics.md) and non-relativistic quantum mechanics}@}. This gives {@{results that agree reasonably well with observations}@}.

{@{A rigorous calculation of the same result}@} would use {@{[relativistic quantum mechanics](relativistic%20quantum%20mechanics.md), using the [Dirac equation](Dirac%20equation.md)}@}, and would include {@{[many-body interactions](many-body%20problem.md)}@}. {@{Achieving an even more precise result}@} would involve calculating {@{small corrections from [quantum electrodynamics](quantum%20electrodynamics.md)}@}.

### energy of a magnetic moment

{@{The energy of a magnetic moment in a magnetic field}@} is given by {@{$$\Delta H=-{\boldsymbol {\mu } }\cdot \mathbf {B} ,$$}@} where ___μ___ is {@{the [magnetic moment](magnetic%20moment.md) of the particle}@}, and __B__ is {@{the [magnetic field](magnetic%20field.md) it experiences}@}.

### magnetic field

We shall deal with {@{the [magnetic field](magnetic%20field.md)}@} first. Although in {@{the rest frame of the nucleus}@}, there is {@{no magnetic field acting on the electron}@}, there _is_ {@{one in the rest frame of the electron}@} \(see {@{[classical electromagnetism and special relativity](classical%20electromagnetism%20and%20special%20relativity.md)}@}\). Ignoring for now that {@{this frame is not [inertial](inertial.md)}@}, we end up with the equation {@{$$\mathbf {B} =-{\frac {\mathbf {v} \times \mathbf {E} }{c^{2} } },$$}@} where __v__ is {@{the velocity of the electron}@}, and __E__ is {@{the electric field it travels through}@}.<sup>[\[a\]](#^ref-a)</sup> (annotation: Using {@{the Biot-Savart law for a circular current loop}@}, {@{an orbiting charge with period $T = 2\pi r / v$}@} yields {@{an effective current $I = q v / (2\pi r)$}@}. Evaluating the line integral gives {@{$\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \oint_C \frac{I \, d\mathbf{l} \times \hat{\mathbf{r} } }{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \left(\frac{q v}{2\pi r}\right) \frac{2\pi r \, \hat{\mathbf{z} } }{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0 q v}{4\pi r^2} \, \hat{\mathbf{z} }$}@}. {@{Multiplying and regrouping by $\varepsilon_0$}@} then gives {@{$\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q v}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{z} } = \frac{1}{c^2} \left(\frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r^2}\right) v \hat{\mathbf{z} } = -\frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{E} }{c^2}$}@}. Alternatively, applying {@{the low-velocity ($\gamma \approx 1$) electromagnetic Lorentz transformation}@} {@{$\mathbf{B}' = \gamma(\mathbf{B} - \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{E} }{c^2})$ with $\mathbf{B} = 0$}@} directly yields {@{$\mathbf{B} = -\frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{E} }{c^2}$}@}.) Here, in {@{the non-relativistic limit}@}, we assume that {@{the Lorentz factor $\gamma \backsimeq 1$}@}. Now we know that {@{__E__ is radial}@}, so we can rewrite {@{$\mathbf {E} =\left|E\right|{\frac {\mathbf {r} }{r} }$}@}. Also we know that {@{the momentum of the electron}@} {@{$\mathbf {p} =m_{\text{e} }\mathbf {v}$}@}. Substituting {@{these and changing the order of the cross product}@} \(using the identity {@{$\mathbf {A} \times \mathbf {B} =-\mathbf {B} \times \mathbf {A}$}@}\) gives {@{$$\mathbf {B} ={\frac {\mathbf {r} \times \mathbf {p} }{m_{\text{e} }c^{2} } }\left|{\frac {E}{r} }\right|.$$}@} Next, we express {@{the electric field as the gradient of the [electric potential](electric%20potential.md) $\mathbf {E} =-\nabla V$}@}. Here we make {@{the [central field approximation](central%20field%20approximation.md)}@}, that is, that {@{the electrostatic potential is spherically symmetric}@}, so is {@{only a function of radius}@}. This approximation is {@{exact for hydrogen and hydrogen-like systems}@}. Now we can say that {@{$$|E|=\left|{\frac {\partial V}{\partial r} }\right|={\frac {1}{e} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} },$$}@} where {@{$U=-eV$}@} is {@{the [potential energy](potential%20energy.md) of the electron in the central field}@}, and _e_ is {@{the [elementary charge](elementary%20charge.md)}@}. Now we remember from {@{classical mechanics that the [angular momentum](angular%20momentum.md) of a particle}@} {@{$\mathbf {L} =\mathbf {r} \times \mathbf {p}$}@}. Putting it all together, we get {@{$$\mathbf {B} ={\frac {1}{m_{\text{e} }ec^{2} } }{\frac {1}{r} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} }\mathbf {L} .$$}@} At this point, __B__ is {@{a positive number multiplied by __L__}@}, meaning that {@{the [magnetic field](magnetic%20field.md) is parallel to the [orbital](orbit.md) [angular momentum](angular%20momentum.md) of the particle}@}, which is itself {@{perpendicular to the particle's velocity}@}.

### spin magnetic moment of the electron

{@{The [spin magnetic moment](spin%20magnetic%20moment.md#spin%20magnetic%20moment) of the electron}@} is {@{$${\boldsymbol {\mu } }_{S}=-g_{\text{s} }\mu _{\text{B} }{\frac {\mathbf {S} }{\hbar } },$$}@} where $\mathbf {S}$ is {@{the spin \(or intrinsic angular-momentum\) vector}@}, $\mu _{\text{B} }$ is {@{the [Bohr magneton](Bohr%20magneton.md) (annotation: $\mu_{\text B} = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e}$)}@}, and {@{$g_{\text{s} }=2.0023...\approx 2$}@} is {@{the electron-spin [g-factor](g-factor%20(physics).md)}@}. Here ${\boldsymbol {\mu } }$ is {@{a negative constant multiplied by the [spin](spin%20(physics).md)}@}, so {@{the [spin magnetic moment](spin%20magnetic%20moment.md#spin%20magnetic%20moment)}@} is {@{antiparallel to the spin}@}.

{@{The spin–orbit potential}@} consists of {@{two parts}@}. {@{The Larmor part}@} is connected to {@{the interaction of the spin magnetic moment of the electron}@} with {@{the magnetic field of the nucleus in the co-moving frame of the electron}@}. {@{The second contribution}@} is related to {@{[Thomas precession](Thomas%20precession.md)}@}.

### Larmor interaction energy

- Main article: ::@:: [Larmor precession](Larmor%20precession.md)

{@{The Larmor interaction energy}@} is {@{$$\Delta H_{\text{L} }=-{\boldsymbol {\mu } }\cdot \mathbf {B} .$$}@} {@{Substituting in this equation expressions}@} for {@{the spin magnetic moment and the magnetic field}@}, one gets {@{$$\Delta H_{\text{L} }={\frac {g_{\text{s} }\mu _{\text{B} } }{\hbar m_{\text{e} }ec^{2} } }{\frac {1}{r} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} }\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} \approx {\frac {2\mu _{\text{B} } }{\hbar m_{\text{e} }ec^{2} } }{\frac {1}{r} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} }\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} .$$}@} Now we have to take into account {@{[Thomas precession](Thomas%20precession.md) correction for the electron's curved trajectory}@}.

### Thomas interaction energy

- Main article: ::@:: [Thomas precession](Thomas%20precession.md)

In {@{1926 [Llewellyn Thomas](Llewellyn%20Thomas.md) relativistically recomputed the doublet separation}@} in {@{the fine structure of the atom}@}.<sup>[\[2\]](#^ref-2)</sup> {@{Thomas precession rate ${\boldsymbol {\Omega } }_{\text{T} }$}@} is related to {@{the angular frequency of the orbital motion ${\boldsymbol {\omega } }$ of a spinning particle}@} as follows:<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup><sup>[\[4\]](#^ref-4)</sup> {@{$${\boldsymbol {\Omega } }_{\text{T} }=-{\boldsymbol {\omega } }(\gamma -1),$$}@} where $\gamma$ is {@{the [Lorentz factor](Lorentz%20factor.md) of the moving particle}@}. {@{The Hamiltonian producing the spin precession ${\boldsymbol {\Omega } }_{\text{T} }$}@} is given by {@{$$\Delta H_{\text{T} }={\boldsymbol {\Omega } }_{\text{T} }\cdot \mathbf {S} .$$}@} To {@{the first order in $(v/c)^{2}$}@}, we obtain {@{$$\Delta H_{\text{T} }=-{\frac {\mu _{\text{B} } }{\hbar m_{\text{e} }ec^{2} } }{\frac {1}{r} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} }\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} .$$}@} (annotation: Expanding {@{the Lorentz factor via $\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2} \approx 1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2}$}@} so that {@{$\gamma - 1 \approx \frac{v^2}{2c^2}$ to first order in $(v/c)^2$}@}, substituting {@{the orbital angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{\mathbf{L} }{m_e r^2}$}@}, applying {@{the circular motion centripetal relation $m_e v^2 / r = \frac{\partial U(r)}{\partial r}$}@}, and expressing {@{the coefficients using the Bohr magneton $\mu_{\text{B} }$}@} into {@{$\Delta H_{\text{T} } = -\boldsymbol{\omega}(\gamma - 1) \cdot \mathbf{S}$}@} directly yields {@{$\Delta H_{\text{T} } = -\frac{\mu_{\text{B} } }{\hbar m_e c^2} \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial U(r)}{\partial r} \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$}@}.)

### total interaction energy

{@{The total spin–orbit potential}@} in {@{an external electrostatic potential}@} takes the form {@{$$\Delta H\equiv \Delta H_{\text{L} }+\Delta H_{\text{T} }={\frac {(g_{\text{s} }-1)\mu _{\text{B} } }{\hbar m_{\text{e} }ec^{2} } }{\frac {1}{r} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} }\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} \approx {\frac {\mu _{\text{B} } }{\hbar m_{\text{e} }ec^{2} } }{\frac {1}{r} }{\frac {\partial U(r)}{\partial r} }\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} .$$}@} {@{The net effect of Thomas precession}@} is {@{the reduction of the Larmor interaction energy by factor of about 1/2}@}, which came to be known as {@{the _Thomas half_}@}.

### evaluating the energy shift

Thanks to {@{all the above approximations}@}, we can now evaluate {@{the detailed energy shift in this model}@}. Note that {@{_L_<sub>_z_</sub> and _S_<sub>_z_</sub>}@} are {@{no longer conserved quantities}@}. In particular, we wish to find {@{a new basis that diagonalizes both _H_<sub>0</sub> \(the non-perturbed Hamiltonian\) and Δ<!-- markdown separator -->_H_}@}. To {@{find out what basis this is}@}, we first define {@{the [total angular momentum](azimuthal%20quantum%20number.md#total%20angular%20momentum%20of%20an%20electron%20in%20the%20atom) [operator](operator%20(physics).md) $$\mathbf {J} =\mathbf {L} +\mathbf {S} .$$}@} Taking {@{the [dot product](dot%20product.md) of this with itself}@}, we get {@{$$\mathbf {J} ^{2}=\mathbf {L} ^{2}+\mathbf {S} ^{2}+2\,\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S}$$ \(since __L__ and __S__ commute\)}@}, and therefore {@{$$\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} ={\frac {1}{2} }\left(\mathbf {J} ^{2}-\mathbf {L} ^{2}-\mathbf {S} ^{2}\right)$$}@}

It can be shown that {@{the five operators _H_<sub>0</sub>, __J__<sup>2</sup>, __L__<sup>2</sup>, __S__<sup>2</sup>, and _J_<sub>z</sub>}@} all {@{commute with each other and with Δ<!-- markdown separator -->_H_}@}. Therefore, {@{the basis we were looking for}@} is {@{the simultaneous [eigenbasis](eigenbasis.md#eigenbasis) of these five operators}@} \(i.e., {@{the basis where all five are diagonal}@}\). {@{Elements of this basis}@} have {@{the five [quantum numbers](quantum%20number.md)}@}: {@{$n$ \(the "principal quantum number"\)}@}, {@{$j$ \(the "total angular momentum quantum number"\)}@}, {@{$\ell$ \(the "orbital angular momentum quantum number"\)}@}, {@{$s$ \(the "spin quantum number"\)}@}, and {@{$j_{z}$ \(the "_z_ component of total angular momentum"\)}@}.

To {@{evaluate the energies}@}, we note that {@{$$\left\langle {\frac {1}{r^{3} } }\right\rangle ={\frac {2}{a^{3}n^{3}\;\ell (\ell +1)(2\ell +1)} }$$}@} for {@{hydrogenic wavefunctions}@} \(here {@{$a=\hbar /(Z\alpha m_{\text{e} }c)$}@} is {@{the [Bohr radius](Bohr%20radius.md) divided by the nuclear charge _Z_}@}\); and {@{$$\left\langle \mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S} \right\rangle ={\frac {1}{2} }{\big (}\langle \mathbf {J} ^{2}\rangle -\langle \mathbf {L} ^{2}\rangle -\langle \mathbf {S} ^{2}\rangle {\big )}={\frac {\hbar ^{2} }{2} }{\big (}j(j+1)-\ell (\ell +1)-s(s+1){\big )}.$$}@}

### final energy shift

We can now say that {@{$$\Delta E={\frac {\beta }{2} }{\big (}j(j+1)-\ell (\ell +1)-s(s+1){\big )},$$}@} where {@{the spin-orbit coupling constant}@} is {@{$$\beta =\beta (n,l)=Z^{4}{\frac {\mu _{0} }{4\pi } }g_{\text{s} }\mu _{\text{B} }^{2}{\frac {1}{n^{3}a_{0}^{3}\;\ell (\ell +1/2)(\ell +1)} }.$$}@} For {@{the exact relativistic result}@}, see {@{the [solutions to the Dirac equation for a hydrogen-like atom](hydrogen-like%20atom.md#solution%20to%20Dirac%20equation)}@}.

{@{The derivation above}@} calculates {@{the interaction energy in the \(momentaneous\) rest frame of the electron}@} and in {@{this reference frame}@} there's {@{a magnetic field that's absent in the rest frame of the nucleus}@}.

{@{Another approach}@} is to calculate {@{it in the rest frame of the nucleus}@}, see for example {@{George P. Fisher: _Electric Dipole Moment of a Moving Magnetic Dipole_ \(1971\)}@}.<sup>[\[5\]](#^ref-5)</sup> However {@{the rest frame calculation is sometimes avoided}@}, because one has to {@{account for [hidden momentum](hidden%20momentum.md)}@}.<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup>

## scattering

In {@{solid state physics and particle physics}@}, {@{[Mott scattering](Mott%20scattering.md)}@} describes {@{the scattering of electrons out of an impurity}@} which includes {@{the spin-orbit effects}@}.<sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup><sup>[\[8\]](#^ref-8)</sup> It is analogous to {@{the [Coulomb scattering](Coulomb%20scattering.md) \(Rutherford scattering\) with the addition of spin-orbit coupling}@}. In {@{particle physics}@}, it is due to {@{relativistic corrections}@}.<sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup>

{@{A crystalline solid \(semiconductor, metal etc.\)}@} is characterized by {@{its [band structure](band%20structure.md)}@}. While on {@{the overall scale \(including the core levels\)}@} {@{the spin–orbit interaction is still a small perturbation}@}, it may play {@{a relatively more important role if we zoom in to bands close to the [Fermi level](Fermi%20level.md) \($E_{\text{F} }$\)}@}. {@{The atomic $\mathbf {L} \cdot \mathbf {S}$ \(spin–orbit\) interaction}@}, for example, splits {@{bands that would be otherwise degenerate}@}, and {@{the particular form of this spin–orbit splitting}@} \(typically of {@{the order of few to few hundred millielectronvolts}@}\) depends on {@{the particular system}@}. {@{The bands of interest}@} can be then described by {@{various effective models, usually based on some perturbative approach}@}. An example of {@{how the atomic spin–orbit interaction influences the band structure of a crystal}@} is explained in {@{the article about [Rashba](Rashba%20effect.md) and [Dresselhaus](Dresselhaus%20effect.md) interactions}@}.

In {@{crystalline solid contained paramagnetic ions}@}, e.g. {@{ions with unclosed d or f atomic subshell}@}, {@{localized electronic states}@} exist.<sup>[\[9\]](#^ref-9)</sup><sup>[\[10\]](#^ref-10)</sup> In this case, {@{atomic-like electronic levels structure}@} is shaped by {@{intrinsic magnetic spin–orbit interactions and interactions with [crystalline electric fields](crystal%20field%20theory.md)}@}.<sup>[\[11\]](#^ref-11)</sup> Such structure is named {@{[the fine electronic structure](fine%20electronic%20structure.md)}@}. For {@{[rare-earth](rare-earth.md) ions}@} {@{the spin–orbit interactions}@} are {@{much stronger than the [crystal electric field](crystal%20field%20theory.md) \(CEF\) interactions}@}.<sup>[\[12\]](#^ref-12)</sup> {@{The strong spin–orbit coupling}@} makes {@{_J_ a relatively good quantum number}@}, because {@{the first excited multiplet}@} is {@{at least ~130 meV \(1500 K\) above the primary multiplet}@}. The result is that {@{filling it at room temperature \(300 K\)}@} is {@{negligibly small}@}. (annotation: {@{Thermal energy at room temperature}@} is {@{too low to populate the excited multiplet}@}.) In this case, {@{a \(2<!-- markdown separator -->_J_ + 1\)-fold degenerated primary multiplet}@} split by {@{an external CEF}@} can be treated as {@{the basic contribution to the analysis of such systems' properties}@}. (annotation: {@{System properties can be modeled}@} by considering {@{only the $(2J + 1)$-fold degenerate ground multiplet}@} split by {@{the crystal electric field}@}.) In the case of {@{approximate calculations for basis $|J,J_{z}\rangle$}@}, to determine {@{which is the primary multiplet}@}, {@{the [Hund](Friedrich%20Hund.md) principles, known from atomic physics}@}, are applied: (annotation: 3 items: {@{maximal _S_, maximal _L_, _J_ = \|_L_ − _S_\| or _J_ = _L_ + _S_}@})

- (annotation: Hund principles for _S_) ::@:: The ground state of the terms' structure has the maximal value _S_ allowed by the [Pauli exclusion principle](Pauli%20exclusion%20principle.md).
- (annotation: Hund principles for _L_) ::@:: The ground state has a maximal allowed _L_ value, with maximal _S_.
- (annotation: Hund principles for the primary multiplet) ::@:: The primary multiplet has a corresponding _J_ = \|_L_ − _S_\| when the shell is less than half full, and _J_ = _L_ + _S_, where the fill is greater.

{@{The _S_, _L_ and _J_ of the ground multiplet}@} are {@{determined by [Hund's rules](Hund's%20rules.md)}@}. {@{The ground multiplet}@} is {@{2<!-- markdown separator -->_J_ + 1 degenerated}@} – its {@{degeneracy is removed by CEF interactions and magnetic interactions}@}. {@{CEF interactions and magnetic interactions}@} resemble, somehow, {@{the [Stark](Stark%20effect.md) and the [Zeeman effect](Zeeman%20effect.md) known from [atomic physics](atomic%20physics.md)}@}. {@{The energies and eigenfunctions}@} of {@{the discrete fine electronic structure}@} are obtained by {@{diagonalization of the \(2<!-- markdown separator -->_J_ + 1\)-dimensional matrix}@}. {@{The fine electronic structure}@} can be {@{directly detected by many different spectroscopic methods}@}, including {@{the [inelastic neutron scattering](inelastic%20neutron%20scattering.md#inelastic%20neutron%20scattering) \(INS\) experiments}@}. {@{The case of strong cubic CEF}@}<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup> \(for {@{3<!-- markdown separator -->_d_ transition-metal ions}@}\) interactions form {@{group of levels \(e.g. _T_<sub>2<!-- markdown separator -->_g_</sub>, _A_<sub>2<!-- markdown separator -->_g_</sub>\)}@}, which are {@{partially split by spin–orbit interactions and \(if occur\) lower-symmetry CEF interactions}@}. {@{The energies and eigenfunctions}@} of {@{the discrete fine electronic structure \(for the lowest term\)}@} are obtained by {@{diagonalization of the \(2<!-- markdown separator -->_L_ + 1\)\(2<!-- markdown separator -->_S_ + 1\)-dimensional matrix}@}. At {@{zero temperature \(_T_ = 0 K\)}@} only {@{the lowest state is occupied}@}. {@{The magnetic moment at _T_ = 0 K}@} is equal to {@{the moment of the ground state}@}. It allows the evaluation of {@{the total, spin and orbital moments}@}. {@{The eigenstates and corresponding eigenfunctions $|\Gamma _{n}\rangle$}@} can be found from {@{direct diagonalization of Hamiltonian matrix}@} containing {@{crystal field and spin–orbit interactions}@}. Taking into consideration {@{the thermal population of states}@}, {@{the thermal evolution of the single-ion properties of the compound}@} is established. This technique is based on {@{the equivalent operator theory}@}<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup> defined as {@{the CEF widened by thermodynamic and analytical calculations}@} defined as {@{the supplement of the CEF theory}@} by including {@{thermodynamic and analytical calculations}@}.

### examples of effective Hamiltonians

{@{Hole bands}@} of {@{a bulk \(3D\) zinc-blende semiconductor}@} will be split by {@{$\Delta _{0}$ into heavy and light holes}@} \(which form {@{a $\Gamma _{8}$ quadruplet in the $\Gamma$-point}@} of {@{the Brillouin zone}@}\) and {@{a split-off band}@} \({@{$\Gamma _{7}$ doublet}@}\). Including {@{two conduction bands}@} \({@{$\Gamma _{6}$ doublet}@} in {@{the $\Gamma$-point}@}\), the system is described by {@{the effective eight-band [model of Kohn and Luttinger](Luttinger–Kohn%20model.md)}@}. If only {@{top of the valence band is of interest}@} \(for example when {@{$E_{\text{F} }\ll \Delta _{0}$}@}, {@{Fermi level measured from the top of the valence band}@}\), {@{the proper four-band effective model}@} is {@{$$H_{\text{KL} }(k_{\text{x} },k_{\text{y} },k_{\text{z} })=-{\frac {\hbar ^{2} }{2m} }\left[\left(\gamma _{1}+{ {\tfrac {5}{2} }\gamma _{2} }\right)k^{2}-2\gamma _{2}\left(J_{\text{x} }^{2}k_{\text{x} }^{2}+J_{\text{y} }^{2}k_{\text{y} }^{2}+J_{\text{z} }^{2}k_{\text{z} }^{2}\right)-2\gamma _{3}\sum _{m\neq n}J_{m}J_{n}k_{m}k_{n}\right]$$}@} where $\gamma _{1,2,3}$ are {@{the Luttinger parameters}@} \(analogous to {@{the single effective mass}@} of {@{a one-band model of electrons}@}\) and $J_{ {\text{x} },{\text{y} },{\text{z} } }$ are {@{angular momentum 3/2 matrices}@} \($m$ is {@{the free electron mass}@}\). In {@{combination with magnetization}@}, {@{this type of spin–orbit interaction}@} will distort {@{the electronic bands depending on the magnetization direction}@}, thereby causing {@{[magnetocrystalline anisotropy](magnetocrystalline%20anisotropy.md) \(a special type of [magnetic anisotropy](magnetic%20anisotropy.md)\)}@}. If {@{the semiconductor moreover lacks the inversion symmetry}@}, {@{the hole bands}@} will exhibit {@{cubic Dresselhaus splitting}@}. Within {@{the four bands \(light and heavy holes\)}@}, {@{the dominant term}@} is {@{$$H_{ {\text{D} }3}=b_{41}^{8{\text{v} }8{\text{v} } }[(k_{\text{x} }k_{\text{y} }^{2}-k_{\text{x} }k_{\text{z} }^{2})J_{\text{x} }+(k_{\text{y} }k_{\text{z} }^{2}-k_{\text{y} }k_{\text{x} }^{2})J_{\text{y} }+(k_{\text{z} }k_{\text{x} }^{2}-k_{\text{z} }k_{\text{y} }^{2})J_{\text{z} }]$$}@} where {@{the material parameter $b_{41}^{8{\text{v} }8{\text{v} } }=-81.93\,{\text{meV} }\cdot {\text{nm} }^{3}$}@} for {@{GaAs}@} \(see {@{pp. 72 in Winkler's book}@}, according to {@{more recent data the Dresselhaus constant in GaAs}@} is {@{9 eVÅ<sup>3</sup>}@};<sup>[\[15\]](#^ref-15)</sup> {@{the total Hamiltonian}@} will be {@{$H_{\text{KL} }+H_{ {\text{D} }3}$}@}\). {@{[Two-dimensional electron gas](2DEG.md)}@} in {@{an asymmetric quantum well \(or heterostructure\)}@} will feel {@{the Rashba interaction}@}. {@{The appropriate two-band effective Hamiltonian}@} is {@{$$H_{0}+H_{\text{R} }={\frac {\hbar ^{2}k^{2} }{2m^{*} } }\sigma _{0}+\alpha (k_{\text{y} }\sigma _{\text{x} }-k_{\text{x} }\sigma _{\text{y} })$$}@} where $\sigma _{0}$ is {@{the 2 × 2 identity matrix}@}, {@{$\sigma _{ {\text{x} },{\text{y} } }$}@} {@{the Pauli matrices}@} and $m^{*}$ {@{the electron effective mass}@}. {@{The spin–orbit part of the Hamiltonian, $H_{\text{R} }$}@} is parametrized by {@{$\alpha$, sometimes called the Rashba parameter \(its definition somewhat varies\)}@}, which is related to {@{the structure asymmetry}@}.

{@{Above expressions for spin–orbit interaction}@} couple {@{spin matrices $\mathbf {J}$ and ${\boldsymbol {\sigma } }$}@} to {@{the quasi-momentum $\mathbf {k}$}@}, and to {@{the vector potential $\mathbf {A}$ of an AC electric field}@} through {@{the [Peierls substitution](Peierls%20substitution.md)}@} {@{$\mathbf {k} =-i\nabla -{\frac {e}{\hbar c} }\mathbf {A}$}@}. They are {@{lower order terms}@} of {@{the Luttinger–Kohn [__k·p__ perturbation theory](k·p%20perturbation%20theory.md) in powers of $k$}@}. {@{Next terms of this expansion}@} also produce {@{terms that couple spin operators of the electron coordinate $\mathbf {r}$}@}. Indeed, {@{a cross product $({\boldsymbol {\sigma } }\times {\mathbf {k} })$}@} is {@{[invariant](invariant%20(mathematics).md) with respect to time inversion}@}. In {@{cubic crystals}@}, it has {@{a symmetry of a vector}@} and acquires {@{a meaning of a spin–orbit contribution ${\boldsymbol {r} }_{\text{SO} }$}@} to {@{the operator of coordinate}@}. For {@{electrons in semiconductors with a narrow gap $E_{\rm {G} }$}@} between {@{the conduction and heavy hole bands, Yafet}@} derived the equation<sup>[\[16\]](#^ref-16)</sup><sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup> {@{$${\mathbf {r} }_{\text{SO} }={\frac {\hbar ^{2}g}{4m_{0} } }\left({\frac {1}{E_{\rm {G} } } }+{\frac {1}{E_{\rm {G} }+\Delta _{0} } }\right)({\boldsymbol {\sigma } }\times {\mathbf {k} })$$}@} where $m_{0}$ is {@{a free electron mass}@}, and $g$ is {@{a $g$-factor properly renormalized for spin–orbit interaction}@}. This operator {@{couples electron spin $\mathbf {S} ={\tfrac {1}{2} }{\boldsymbol {\sigma } }$}@} directly to {@{the electric field $\mathbf {E}$}@} through {@{the interaction energy $-e(\mathbf {r} _{\text{SO} }\cdot \mathbf {E} )$}@}.

### oscillating electromagnetic field

{@{[Electric dipole spin resonance](electric%20dipole%20spin%20resonance.md) \(EDSR\)}@} is {@{the coupling of the electron spin with an oscillating electric field}@}. Similar to {@{the [electron spin resonance](electron%20paramagnetic%20resonance.md) \(ESR\)}@} in which {@{electrons can be excited with an electromagnetic wave}@} with {@{the energy given by the [Zeeman effect](Zeeman%20effect.md)}@}, in {@{EDSR the resonance can be achieved}@} if the frequency is {@{related to the energy band splitting given by the spin–orbit coupling in solids}@}. While in {@{ESR the coupling}@} is obtained via {@{the magnetic part of the EM wave with the electron magnetic moment}@}, {@{the ESDR is the coupling}@} of {@{the electric part with the spin and motion of the electrons}@}. {@{This mechanism has been proposed}@} for controlling {@{the spin of electrons in [quantum dots](quantum%20dot.md) and other [mesoscopic systems](mesoscopic%20physics.md)}@}.<sup>[\[18\]](#^ref-18)</sup>
