# Kőnig's theorem

- For {@{the theorem about infinite graphs}@}, see {@{[Kőnig's lemma](Kőnig's%20lemma.md)}@}. For other uses, see {@{[König's theorem \(disambiguation\)](König's%20theorem%20(disambiguation).md)}@}.

> {@{![Graph illustrating König's theorem](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Koenigs-theorem-graph.svg)}@}
>
> An example of {@{a bipartite graph}@}, with {@{a maximum matching \(blue\) and minimum vertex cover \(red\) both of size six}@}.

In {@{the [mathematical](mathematics.md) area of [graph theory](graph%20theory.md)}@}, {@{__Kőnig's theorem__, proved by [Dénes Kőnig](Dénes%20Kőnig.md) \([1931](#CITEREFKőnig1931)\)}@}, describes {@{an equivalence between the [maximum matching](maximum%20matching.md) problem and the minimum [vertex cover problem](vertex%20cover%20problem.md) in [bipartite graphs](bipartite%20graph.md)}@}. It was discovered {@{independently, also in 1931, by [Jenő Egerváry](Jenő%20Egerváry.md)}@} in {@{the more general case of [weighted graphs](weighted%20graph.md#weighted%20graph)}@}.

## setting

{@{A [vertex cover](vertex%20cover.md) in a graph}@} is {@{a set of vertices that includes at least one endpoint of every edge}@}, and {@{a vertex cover is _minimum_}@} if {@{no other vertex cover has fewer vertices}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup> {@{A [matching](matching%20(graph%20theory).md) in a graph}@} is {@{a set of edges no two of which share an endpoint}@}, and {@{a matching is _maximum_}@} if {@{no other matching has more edges}@}.<sup>[\[2\]](#^ref-2)</sup>

It is {@{obvious from the definition}@} that any {@{vertex-cover set must be at least as large as any matching set}@} \(since for every {@{edge in the matching, at least one vertex is needed in the cover}@}\). In particular, {@{the [minimum vertex cover](minimum%20vertex%20cover.md) set}@} is {@{at least as large as the [maximum matching](maximum%20matching.md) set}@}. {@{Kőnig's theorem}@} states that, in {@{any [bipartite graph](bipartite%20graph.md)}@}, {@{the minimum vertex cover set and the maximum matching set have in fact the same size}@}.<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup>

## statement of the theorem

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; _In {@{any [bipartite graph](bipartite%20graph.md)}@}, {@{the number of edges in a [maximum matching](maximum%20matching.md)}@} {@{equals the number of vertices in a [minimum vertex cover](minimum%20vertex%20cover.md)}@}.<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup>_

### example

(annotation: {@{![Graph illustrating König's theorem](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Koenigs-theorem-graph.svg)}@}) {@{The bipartite graph shown in the above illustration}@} has {@{14 vertices}@}; {@{a matching with six edges}@} is {@{shown in blue}@}, and {@{a vertex cover with six vertices is shown in red}@}. There can be {@{no smaller vertex cover}@}, because any {@{vertex cover has to include at least one endpoint of each matched edge \(as well as of every other edge\)}@}, so this is {@{a minimum vertex cover}@}. Similarly, there can be {@{no larger matching}@}, because any {@{matched edge has to include at least one endpoint in the vertex cover}@}, so this is {@{a maximum matching}@}. {@{Kőnig's theorem}@} states that {@{the equality between the sizes of the matching and the cover}@} \(in this {@{example, both numbers are six}@}\) applies {@{more generally to any bipartite graph}@}.

## proofs

### constructive proof

> {@{![Illustration to how maximum flow/minimum cut looks in bipartite graphs](../../archives/Wikimedia%20Commons/Minimum%20cut%20in%20a%20bipartite%20graph.svg)}@}
>
> {@{Minimum cut $(S, T)$}@} in {@{the flow network $G'_\infty$}@}

{@{The following proof}@} provides {@{a way of constructing a minimum vertex cover from a maximum matching}@}. Let {@{$G=(V,E)$}@} be {@{a bipartite graph}@} and let $A,B$ be {@{the two parts of the vertex set $V$}@}. Suppose that $M$ is {@{a maximum matching for $G$}@}.

Construct {@{the [flow network](flow%20network.md) $G'_\infty$ derived from $G$}@} in such way that there are {@{edges of capacity $1$ from the source $s$ to every vertex $a \in A$}@} and from {@{every vertex $b \in B$ to the sink $t$}@}, and of {@{capacity $+\infty$ from $a$ to $b$ for any $(a, b) \in E$}@}. (annotation: Because {@{flow constrains edges}@} whereas {@{a matching requires vertex exclusivity}@}, adding {@{$s \to a$ and $b \to t$ edges of capacity $1$}@} uses {@{the bipartition $V = A \sqcup B$}@} so {@{a flow unit on $(s, a)$ or $(b, t)$}@} models {@{choosing $a \in A$ or $b \in B$ at most once for a matching}@}. Assigning {@{capacity $1$ to each $(a, b) \in E$}@} fits {@{maximum matching at first glance but gives a weaker cut characterization}@}; raising {@{every $(a, b) \in E$ capacity to $+\infty$}@} keeps {@{the maximum flow unchanged}@} while preventing {@{any finite-capacity cut $(S, T)$ from crossing an edge from $S \cap A$ to $T \cap B$}@}.)

{@{The size $|M|$ of the maximum matching in $G$}@} is {@{the size of a [maximum flow](maximum%20flow%20problem.md) in $G'_\infty$}@}, which, in turn, is {@{the size of a [minimum cut](minimum%20cut.md) in the network $G'_\infty$}@}, as follows from {@{the [max-flow min-cut theorem](max-flow%20min-cut%20theorem.md)}@}.

Let {@{$(S, T)$ be a minimum cut}@}. Let {@{$A = A_S \cup A_T$ and $B = B_S \cup B_T$}@}, such that {@{$A_S, B_S \subset S$ and $A_T, B_T \subset T$}@}. Then {@{the minimum cut is composed}@} only of {@{edges going from $s$ to $A_T$ or from $B_S$ to $t$}@}, as any {@{edge from $A_S$ to $B_T$ would make the size of the cut infinite}@}. (annotation: This also implies that there are {@{no edges between $A_S$ and $B_T$}@}, because {@{any such edge would be crossed by the cut $(S, T)$}@}.)

Therefore, {@{the size of the minimum cut}@} is equal to {@{$|A_T|+|B_S|$}@}. On the other hand, {@{$A_T \cup B_S$ is a vertex cover}@}, as any {@{edge that is not incident to vertices from $A_T$ and $B_S$}@} must be {@{incident to a pair of vertices from $A_S$ and $B_T$}@}, which would {@{contradict the fact that there are no edges between $A_S$ and $B_T$}@}.

Thus, {@{$A_T \cup B_S$}@} is {@{a minimum vertex cover of $G$}@}.<sup>[\[4\]](#^ref-4)</sup>

### constructive proof without flow concepts

> __annotation__
>
> {@{An alternating path}@} is {@{a simple path whose consecutive edges alternate between $E \setminus M$ and $M$}@} ({@{alternative definitions}@} may require {@{an unmatched start vertex or allowing non-simple walks}@}), and {@{an augmenting path}@} is {@{an alternating path whose distinct endpoints are both unmatched by $M$}@}. By {@{Berge's lemma}@}, {@{a matching $M$ is maximum if and only if $G$ has no augmenting path}@}; because $M$ is {@{maximum rather than merely maximal}@}, {@{no augmenting path exists in $G$}@}.
>
> In {@{the bipartite graph $G$ with parts $A$ and $B$}@}, {@{alternating structures}@} classify {@{exhaustively into six types}@}: {@{even-length alternating paths starting and ending in $A$; even-length alternating paths starting and ending in $B$}@}; {@{odd-length alternating paths starting in $A$ and ending in $B$; odd-length alternating paths starting in $B$ and ending in $A$}@}; {@{alternating cycles (even-length simple cycles alternating between $E \setminus M$ and $M$)}@}; and {@{length-zero or single-edge alternating structures (isolated vertices or isolated matched edges in $M$)}@}. {@{Classifying these types}@} first shows, when picking {@{one endpoint per $e \in M$ for a vertex cover $K$ of size $\vert{}M\vert{}$}@}, which {@{types force left endpoints in $A$, which force right endpoints in $B$}@}, and which {@{permit any consistent endpoint choice}@}.
>
> Picking {@{only left endpoints $a \in A$ for every $(a, b) \in M$}@} fails on {@{an even-length alternating path starting and ending in $A$}@} when its {@{start vertex is unmatched and its first edge $(u, b_1) \in E \setminus M$ is unmatched}@}, forcing {@{right endpoints in $B$ along that path to cover unmatched edges}@}. Conversely, {@{an even-length alternating path starting and ending in $B$}@} with {@{an unmatched start vertex and an unmatched first edge in $E \setminus M$}@} forces {@{left endpoints in $A$ by symmetry}@}. {@{The remaining four types}@} allow {@{any consistent left-or-right choice per matched edge}@}.
>
> Examining each {@{matched edge $e \in M$ across the alternating structures containing it}@}, no {@{edge $e \in M$ can lie in both a structure}@} forcing {@{its left endpoint in $A$ and a structure forcing its right endpoint in $B$}@} without creating {@{an augmenting path that contradicts maximality of $M$ by Berge's lemma}@}. Therefore, choosing {@{left endpoints when forced left, right endpoints when forced right, and any consistent endpoint otherwise}@} is {@{well-defined and conflict-free}@}.
>
> While earlier we used {@{the general alternating path (any path alternating between $E \setminus M$ and $M$ regardless of start vertex)}@}, here an alternating path means {@{one starting at an unmatched vertex $u \in U \subseteq A$ while alternating between $E \setminus M$ and $M$ with no extra restrictions}@}; under this definition, {@{paths forcing right endpoints in $B$}@} are {@{precisely these alternating paths from $U$}@}, so $U$ picks {@{unmatched vertices in $A$, extending to $Z$ gathers their vertices}@}, and {@{$K = (A \setminus Z) \cup (B \cap Z)$}@} takes {@{default left choices in $A \setminus Z$ and forced right choices in $B \cap Z$}@}.

Setting $U$ as the unmatched vertices in $A$ and $Z$ as $U$ together with vertices reachable from $U$ via alternating paths isolates the forced right-endpoint choices in $B$, so $K = (A \setminus Z) \cup (B \cap Z)$ keeps left endpoints in $A \setminus Z$ and takes right endpoints in $B \cap Z$.

{@{No vertex in a vertex cover}@} can {@{cover more than one edge of $M$ \(because the edge half-overlap would prevent $M$ from being a matching in the first place\)}@}, so if {@{a vertex cover with $|M|$ vertices can be constructed}@}, it {@{must be a minimum cover}@}.<sup>[\[5\]](#^ref-5)</sup>

To {@{construct such a cover (annotation: of size $|M|$)}@}, let $U$ be {@{the set of unmatched vertices in $A$ \(possibly empty\)}@}, and let $Z$ be {@{the set of vertices that are either in $U$ or are connected to $U$ by alternating paths}@} \(paths that {@{alternate between edges that are in the matching and edges that are not in the matching}@}\). (annotation: While earlier in the annotation above we used {@{the general alternating path (any path alternating between $E \setminus M$ and $M$ regardless of start vertex)}@}, here an alternating path means {@{one starting at an unmatched vertex $u \in U \subseteq A$ while alternating between $E \setminus M$ and $M$ with no extra restrictions}@}.) Let {@{$$K=(A\setminus Z)\cup (B\cap Z).$$}@} (annotation: Under the above new definition, {@{paths forcing right endpoints in $B$}@} are {@{precisely these alternating paths from $U$}@}, so $U$ picks {@{unmatched vertices in $A$, extending to $Z$ gathers their vertices}@}, and {@{$K = (A \setminus Z) \cup (B \cap Z)$}@} takes {@{default left choices in $A \setminus Z$ and forced right choices in $B \cap Z$}@}.) {@{Every edge $e$ in $E$}@} either {@{belongs to an alternating path \(and has a right endpoint in $K$\)}@}, or it {@{has a left endpoint in $K$}@}. (annotation: Here {@{"alternating path"}@} means {@{an alternating path starting at an unmatched vertex in $U \subseteq A$}@}, so its {@{vertices lie in $Z$}@}; since its {@{first edge is unmatched}@}, {@{matched edges in $M$}@} occur at {@{every even step (traversed from right in $B$ to left in $A$)}@}, placing {@{the right endpoint of any edge on such a path}@} in {@{$B \cap Z \subseteq K$}@}.) For (annotation: i.e. because), if $e$ is {@{matched but not in an alternating path}@}, then its {@{left endpoint cannot be in an alternating path \(because two matched edges can not share a vertex\)}@} and thus {@{belongs to $A\setminus Z$}@}. (annotation: For {@{$e \in M$ not on such a path}@}, its {@{left endpoint is matched rather than in $U$}@} and cannot be {@{reached as the left end of an even-step matched edge from $U$}@}, so it {@{stays outside $Z$, lies in $A \setminus Z \subseteq K$, and covers $e$}@}.) Alternatively, if $e$ is {@{unmatched but not in an alternating path}@}, then its {@{left endpoint cannot be in an alternating path}@}, for {@{such a path could be extended by adding $e$ to it}@}. (annotation: For {@{$e \in E \setminus M$ not on such a path}@}, its {@{left endpoint cannot lie in $Z$}@} because {@{appending $e$ would put $e$ on an alternating path from $U$}@} (with its {@{right endpoint in $B \cap Z \subseteq K$ if $e$ matched}@}, or creating {@{an augmenting path that contradicts Berge's lemma if its right endpoint in $B$ is unmatched}@}), so its {@{left endpoint stays in $A \setminus Z \subseteq K$ and covers $e$}@}.) Thus, {@{$K$ forms a vertex cover}@}.<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup>

Additionally, every {@{vertex in $K$ is an endpoint of a matched edge}@}. For, every {@{vertex in $A\setminus Z$ is matched}@} because {@{$Z$ is a superset of $U$, the set of unmatched left vertices}@}. And every {@{vertex in $B\cap Z$ must also be matched}@}, for if there {@{existed an alternating path to an unmatched vertex}@} then changing {@{the matching by removing the matched edges from this path and adding the unmatched edges in their place}@} would {@{increase the size of the matching}@}. (annotation: Reaching {@{an unmatched vertex along an alternating path from $U$}@} would yield {@{an augmenting path that flips matched and unmatched edges to increase $\vert{}M\vert{}$}@}, contradicting {@{maximality of $M$ by Berge's lemma}@}.) However, no {@{matched edge can have both of its endpoints in $K$}@}. Thus, $K$ is {@{a vertex cover of [cardinality](cardinality.md) equal to $M$, and must be a minimum vertex cover}@}.<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup>

### proof using linear programming duality

To {@{explain this proof}@}, we first have to {@{extend the notion of a matching to that of a [fractional matching](fractional%20matching.md)}@} - {@{an assignment of a weight in \[0,1\] to each edge}@}, such that {@{the sum of weights near each vertex is at most 1}@} \({@{an integral matching}@} is {@{a special case of a fractional matching in which the weights are in {0,1}<!-- flashcard separator -->}@}\). Similarly we define {@{a fractional vertex-cover}@} - {@{an assignment of a non-negative weight to each vertex}@}, such that {@{the sum of weights in each edge is at least 1}@} \({@{an integral vertex-cover}@} is {@{a special case of a fractional vertex-cover in which the weights are in {0,1}<!-- flashcard separator -->}@}\).

{@{The maximum fractional matching size}@} in {@{a graph $G=(V,E)$}@} is {@{the solution of the following [linear program](linear%20program.md)}@}:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (annotation: {@{maximum fractional matching}@}) <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Maximize __1__<sub>_E_</sub> _·_ __x__}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Subject to}@}: {@{__x__ ≥ __0__<!-- markdown separator -->_<sub>E</sub>_}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ {@{__A__<sub>_G_</sub> · __x__ _≤ __1__<sub>V</sub>._}@}

where __x__ is {@{a vector of size \|_E_\|}@} in which each {@{element represents the weight of an edge in the fractional matching}@}. {@{__1__<sub>E</sub>}@} is {@{a vector of \|_E_\| ones}@}, so the first line indicates {@{the size of the matching}@}. {@{__0__<!-- markdown separator -->_<sub>E</sub>_}@} is {@{a vector of \|_E_\| zeros}@}, so the second line indicates {@{the constraint that the weights are non-negative}@}. {@{__1__<sub>V</sub>}@} is {@{a vector of \|_V_\| ones}@} and {@{__A__<sub>_G_</sub>}@} is {@{the [incidence matrix](incidence%20matrix.md) (annotation: between vertices and edges) of _G,_}@} so the third line indicates {@{the constraint that the sum of weights near each vertex is at most 1}@}. Similarly, {@{the minimum fractional vertex-cover size in $G=(V,E)$}@} is {@{the solution of the following LP}@}:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (annotation: {@{minimum fractional vertex-cover}@}) <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Minimize __1__<sub>_V_</sub> _·_ __y__}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Subject to}@}: {@{__y__ ≥ __0__<!-- markdown separator -->_<sub>V</sub>_}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ {@{__A__<sub>_G_</sub><sup>T</sup> · __y__ ≥ ___1__<sub>E</sub>._}@}

where __y__ is {@{a vector of size \|V\|}@} in which each {@{element represents the weight of a vertex in the fractional cover}@}. Here, the first line is {@{the size of the cover}@}, the second line represents {@{the non-negativity of the weights}@}, and the third line represents {@{the requirement that the sum of weights near each edge must be at least 1}@}. Now, {@{the minimum fractional cover LP}@} is {@{exactly the [dual linear program](dual%20linear%20program.md) of the maximum fractional matching LP}@}. (annotation: Converting {@{the primal fractional matching LP}@} ({@{$\max 1_E \cdot x$}@} subject to {@{$A_G x \le 1_V$ and $x \ge 0_E$}@}) into {@{the dual fractional vertex-cover LP}@} ({@{$\min 1_V \cdot y$}@} subject to {@{$A_G^T y \ge 1_E$ and $y \ge 0_V$}@}) pairs {@{each component across the two programs}@}: first, {@{the $\vert{}V\vert{}$ primal vertex constraints $A_G x \le 1_V$}@} correspond to {@{the $\vert{}V\vert{}$ dual variables $y \ge 0_V$}@}, where {@{$\le$ constraints in a maximization primal}@} induce {@{non-negative dual variables $y_v \ge 0$}@} (whereas {@{$\ge$ constraints in a maximization primal would induce $y_v \le 0$}@}); second, {@{the $\vert{}V\vert{}$ bound entries $1_V$ in $A_G x \le 1_V$}@} become {@{the objective coefficients in $1_V \cdot y = \sum_{v \in V} y_v$}@}, turning {@{vertex capacity bounds into dual objective weights}@}; third, {@{the $\vert{}E\vert{}$ non-negative primal variables $x \ge 0_E$}@} correspond to {@{the $\vert{}E\vert{}$ dual edge constraints $A_G^T y \ge 1_E$}@}, where {@{non-negative variables $x_e \ge 0$ in a maximization primal}@} induce {@{$\ge$ inequalities in the minimization dual}@} (whereas {@{$x_e \le 0$ in a maximization primal would induce $\le$ dual inequalities}@}); fourth, {@{the $\vert{}E\vert{}$ objective entries $1_E$ in $1_E \cdot x = \sum_{e \in E} x_e$}@} become {@{the lower-bound entries $1_E$ in $A_G^T y \ge 1_E$}@}, turning {@{primal edge weights into minimum cover requirements}@}; fifth, {@{the $\vert{}V\vert{} \times \vert{}E\vert{}$ incidence matrix $A_G$}@} {@{transposes into the $\vert{}E\vert{} \times \vert{}V\vert{}$ matrix $A_G^T$}@}, converting {@{vertex-indexed rows $(A_G x)_v \le 1$}@} into {@{edge-indexed rows $(A_G^T y)_e = y_u + y_v \ge 1$ for $e = \{u, v\}$}@}; and finally, {@{primal maximization $\max 1_E \cdot x$}@} converts into {@{dual minimization $\min 1_V \cdot y$}@}, aligning {@{upper bounds on edge sums with lower bounds on vertex sums}@}.) Therefore, by {@{the (annotation: strong) LP duality theorem}@}, both {@{programs have the same solution}@}. This fact is {@{true not only in bipartite graphs but in arbitrary graphs}@}:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; _In {@{any graph}@}, {@{the largest size of a fractional matching}@} equals {@{the smallest size of a fractional vertex cover}@}._

What makes {@{bipartite graphs special}@} is that, in {@{bipartite graphs}@}, {@{both these linear programs}@} have {@{optimal solutions in which all variable values are integers}@}. This follows from the fact that in {@{the [fractional matching polytope](matching%20polytope.md) of a bipartite graph}@}, {@{all extreme points}@} have {@{only [integer](integer.md) coordinates}@}, and {@{the same is true for the fractional vertex-cover polytope}@}. Therefore, {@{the above theorem}@} implies:<sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup>

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; _In {@{any bipartite graph}@}, {@{the largest size of a matching}@} equals {@{the smallest size of a vertex cover}@}._

## algorithm

{@{The [constructive proof](constructive%20proof.md)}@} described above provides {@{an algorithm for producing a minimum vertex cover given a maximum matching}@}. Thus, {@{the [Hopcroft–Karp algorithm](Hopcroft–Karp%20algorithm.md)}@} for {@{finding maximum matchings in bipartite graphs}@} may also be {@{used to solve the vertex cover problem efficiently in these graphs}@}.<sup>[\[8\]](#^ref-8)</sup>

Despite {@{the equivalence of the two problems from the point of view of exact solutions}@}, they are not {@{equivalent for [approximation algorithms](approximation%20algorithm.md)}@}. {@{Bipartite maximum matchings}@} can be {@{approximated arbitrarily accurately in constant time by [distributed algorithms](distributed%20algorithm.md)}@}; in contrast, {@{approximating the minimum vertex cover of a bipartite graph}@} requires {@{at least logarithmic time}@}.<sup>[\[9\]](#^ref-9)</sup>

## non-bipartite graphs

For {@{graphs that are not bipartite}@}, {@{the minimum vertex cover}@} may be {@{larger than the maximum matching}@}. Moreover, {@{the two problems are very different in complexity}@}: {@{maximum matchings}@} can be {@{found in [polynomial time](polynomial%20time.md#polynomial%20time) for any graph}@}, while {@{minimum vertex cover is [NP-complete](NP-complete.md)}@}.

{@{The complement of a vertex cover in any graph}@} is {@{an [independent set](independent%20set%20(graph%20theory).md)}@}, so {@{a minimum vertex cover is complementary to a maximum independent set}@}; {@{finding maximum independent sets}@} is {@{another NP-complete problem}@}. {@{The equivalence between matching and covering articulated in Kőnig's theorem}@} allows {@{minimum vertex covers and maximum independent sets to be computed in polynomial time for bipartite graphs}@}, despite {@{the NP-completeness of these problems for more general graph families}@}.<sup>[\[10\]](#^ref-10)</sup>

## history

{@{Kőnig's theorem is named}@} after {@{the Hungarian mathematician [Dénes Kőnig](Dénes%20Kőnig.md)}@}. Kőnig had {@{announced in 1914 and published in 1916}@} the results that every {@{[regular](regular%20graph.md) bipartite graph has a [perfect matching](perfect%20matching.md)}@},<sup>[\[11\]](#^ref-11)</sup> and more generally that {@{the [chromatic index](chromatic%20index.md#definitions) (annotation: i.e. edge chromatic number) of any bipartite graph}@} \(that is, {@{the minimum number of matchings into which it can be partitioned}@}\) equals its {@{[maximum degree](degree%20(graph%20theory).md)}@}<sup>[\[12\]](#^ref-12)</sup> – the latter statement is known as {@{__Kőnig's line coloring theorem__}@}.<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup> However, {@{[Bondy & Murty \(1976\)](#CITEREFBondyMurty1976) attribute Kőnig's theorem}@} itself to {@{a later paper of Kőnig \(1931\)}@}.

According to {@{[Biggs, Lloyd & Wilson \(1976\)](#CITEREFBiggsLloydWilson1976), Kőnig}@} attributed {@{the idea of studying matchings in bipartite graphs to his father, mathematician [Gyula Kőnig](Gyula%20Kőnig.md)}@}. In {@{Hungarian, Kőnig's name}@} has {@{a [double acute accent](double%20acute%20accent.md)}@}, but his theorem is sometimes {@{spelled \(incorrectly\) in German characters, with an [umlaut](umlaut%20(diacritic).md)}@}.

## related theorems

{@{Kőnig's theorem is equivalent}@} to {@{many other min-max theorems in graph theory and [combinatorics](combinatorics.md)}@}, such as {@{[Hall's marriage theorem](Hall's%20marriage%20theorem.md) and [Dilworth's theorem](Dilworth's%20theorem.md)}@}. Since bipartite matching is {@{a special case of [maximum flow](maximum%20flow%20problem.md)}@}, the theorem also results from {@{the [max-flow min-cut theorem](max-flow%20min-cut%20theorem.md)}@}.<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup>

## connections with perfect graphs

{@{A graph is said to be [perfect](perfect%20graph.md)}@} if, in {@{every [induced subgraph](induced%20subgraph.md)}@}, {@{the (annotation: vertex) [chromatic number](chromatic%20number.md#chromatic%20number) equals the size of the largest [clique](clique%20(graph%20theory).md) (annotation: the size of a largest subset of vertices of an undirected graph such that every two distinct vertices are adjacent)}@}. Any {@{bipartite graph is perfect}@},<sup>[\[15\]](#^ref-15)</sup> because {@{each of its subgraphs is either bipartite or independent (annotation: no edges between any two vertices)}@}; in {@{a bipartite graph that is not independent}@} {@{the chromatic number and the size of the largest clique are both two}@} while in {@{an independent set the chromatic number and clique number are both one}@}.

A graph is {@{perfect if and only if its complement (annotation: invert the presence of edges) is perfect}@},<sup>[\[16\]](#^ref-16)</sup> (annotation: Note the previous statement is {@{the perfect graph theorem, not Kőnig's theorem}@}.) and {@{Kőnig's theorem}@} can be seen as equivalent to the statement that {@{the complement of a bipartite graph is perfect}@}. For (annotation: i.e. because), each {@{color class in a coloring of the complement of a bipartite graph}@} is {@{of size at most 2}@} (annotation: Each {@{color class in $\overline{G}$ is an independent set, thus a clique in the bipartite graph $G$}@}, which contains {@{no triangles and therefore has size at most $2$}@}.) and {@{the classes of size 2 form a matching}@}, {@{a clique in the complement of a graph _G_}@} is {@{an independent set in _G_}@}, and as we have already described {@{an independent set in a bipartite graph _G_}@} is {@{a complement of a vertex cover in _G_}@}. Thus, any {@{matching _M_ in a bipartite graph _G_ with _n_ vertices}@} corresponds to {@{a coloring of the complement of _G_ with _n_&hairsp;−\|_M_\| colors}@}, which by {@{the perfection of complements of bipartite graphs (annotation: this is assumed, to imply Kőnig's theorem)}@} corresponds to {@{an independent set in _G_ with _n_&hairsp;−\|_M_\| vertices}@}, which corresponds to {@{a vertex cover of _G_ with _M_ vertices (annotation: thus the above assumed statement implies Kőnig's theorem)}@}. Conversely, {@{Kőnig's theorem}@} proves {@{the perfection of the complements of bipartite graphs (annotation: the reverse direction)}@}, a result proven in {@{a more explicit form by [Gallai \(1958\)](#CITEREFGallai1958)}@}.

One can also connect {@{Kőnig's line coloring theorem}@} to {@{a different class of perfect graphs, the [line graphs](line%20graph.md) of bipartite graphs}@}. If {@{_G_ is a graph}@}, {@{the line graph _L_\(_G_\)}@} has {@{a vertex for each edge of _G_, and an edge for each pair of adjacent edges in _G_}@}. Thus, {@{the chromatic number of _L_\(_G_\)}@} equals {@{the chromatic index (annotation: i.e. edge chromatic number) of _G_}@}. If {@{_G_ is bipartite}@}, {@{the cliques in _L_\(_G_\)}@} are {@{exactly the sets of edges in _G_ sharing a common endpoint}@}. Now {@{Kőnig's line coloring theorem}@}, stating that {@{the chromatic index (annotation: i.e. edge chromatic number) equals the maximum vertex degree (annotation: corresponding to the maximum clique size) in any bipartite graph}@}, can be interpreted as stating that {@{the line graph of a bipartite graph is perfect}@}.<sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup> (annotation: Note that {@{induced subgraphs of a bipartite graph are either bipartite or independent}@}.)

Since {@{line graphs of bipartite graphs are perfect}@}, {@{the complements of line graphs of bipartite graphs are also perfect}@}. {@{A clique in the complement of the line graph of _G_}@} is {@{just a matching in _G_}@}. (annotation: {@{A clique in the complement $\overline{L(G)}$}@} is {@{a set of vertices that are all pairwise adjacent in $\overline{L(G)}$}@}, so each {@{pair is non-adjacent in the line graph $L(G)$}@}; since {@{two vertices of $L(G)$ are adjacent}@} when their {@{corresponding edges of $G$ share an endpoint}@}, {@{pairwise non-adjacency in $L(G)$}@} means those {@{edges in $G$ are pairwise vertex-disjoint and therefore form a matching in $G$}@}.) And {@{a coloring in the complement of the line graph of _G_, when _G_ is bipartite}@}, is {@{a partition of the edges of _G_ into subsets of edges sharing a common endpoint}@}; (annotation: {@{A proper coloring of $\overline{L(G)}$}@} partitions {@{$V(\overline{L(G)}) = E(G)$ into independent sets in $\overline{L(G)}$}@}, each forming {@{a clique in $L(G)$ where any two edges $e_1, e_2$ share an endpoint in $G$}@}; if any {@{three edges in the class did not all share a single vertex}@}, two edges could {@{meet at $u$ while a third edge $e_3$ avoids $u$ yet meets $e_1$ at $v \neq u$ and $e_2$ at $w \neq u$}@}, forcing {@{three edges between $u, v, w$ that form a triangle ($K_3$) in $G$}@}, which is {@{impossible since a bipartite graph $G$ has no odd cycles}@}, so every {@{color class consists of edges in $G$ meeting at one common vertex $v \in V(G)$}@} and partitions {@{$E(G)$ into endpoint-sharing subsets}@}.) {@{the endpoints shared by each of these subsets}@} form {@{a vertex cover for _G_}@}. Therefore, {@{Kőnig's theorem}@} itself (annotation: It equates {@{maximum matching size with minimum vertex cover size in a bipartite graph $G$}@}, which means {@{maximum clique size equals minimum coloring number (perfection) in $\overline{L(G)}$}@}.) can also be interpreted as stating that {@{the complements of line graphs of bipartite graphs are perfect}@}.<sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup>

## weighted variants

{@{Konig's theorem}@} can be {@{extended to [weighted graphs](weighted%20graph.md#weighted%20graph)}@}.

### [Egerváry](Jenő%20Egerváry.md)'s theorem for edge-weighted graphs

{@{[Jenő Egerváry](Jenő%20Egerváry.md) \(1931\) considered graphs}@} in which each {@{edge _e_ has a non-negative integer weight _w<sub>e</sub>_}@}. {@{The weight vector}@} is {@{denoted by __w__}@}. {@{The __w-__<!-- markdown separator -->_weight of a matching_}@} is {@{the sum of weights of the edges participating in the matching}@}. {@{A ___w-__<!-- markdown separator -->vertex-cover_}@} is {@{a multiset of vertices \("multiset" means that each vertex may appear several times\)}@}, in which each {@{edge _e_ is adjacent to at least _w<sub>e</sub>_ vertices}@}. {@{[Egerváry](Jenő%20Egerváry.md)'s theorem}@} says:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; _In any {@{edge-weighted bipartite graph}@}, {@{the maximum __w-__<!-- markdown separator -->weight of a matching}@} equals {@{the smallest number of vertices in a __w-__<!-- markdown separator -->vertex-cover}@}._ (annotation: {@{Kőnig's theorem}@} is simply {@{a special case where every edge has the weight 1}@}.)

{@{The maximum ___w-___<!-- markdown separator -->weight of a fractional matching}@} is given by {@{the LP}@}:<sup>[\[18\]](#^ref-18)</sup>

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (annotation: {@{maximum _w_-weight fractional matching}@}) <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Maximize ___w___ _·_ __x__}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Subject to}@}: {@{__x__ ≥ __0__<!-- markdown separator -->_<sub>E</sub>_}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ {@{__A__<sub>_G_</sub> · __x__ _≤ __1__<sub>V</sub>._}@} (annotation: Observe that compared to {@{the LP for Kőnig's theorem}@}, {@{___1__<sub>E</sub>_ is replaced by ___w___}@}.)

And {@{the minimum number of vertices in a fractional ___w-___<!-- markdown separator -->vertex-cover}@} is given by {@{the dual LP}@}:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (annotation: {@{minimum fractional _w_-vertex-cover}@}) <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Minimize __1__<sub>_V_</sub> _·_ __y__}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; {@{Subject to}@}: {@{__y__ ≥ __0__<!-- markdown separator -->_<sub>V</sub>_}@} <p> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ {@{__A__<sub>_G_</sub><sup>T</sup> · __y__ ≥ ___w__._}@} (annotation: Observe that compared to {@{the dual LP for Kőnig's theorem}@}, {@{___1__<sub>E</sub>_ is replaced by ___w___}@}.)

As in {@{the proof of Konig's theorem}@}, {@{the (annotatino: strong) LP duality theorem}@} implies that {@{the optimal values are equal \(for any graph\)}@}, and the fact that {@{the graph is bipartite}@} implies that these {@{programs have optimal solutions in which all values are integers}@}.

### theorem for vertex-weighted graphs

One can consider {@{a graph in which each vertex _v_ has a non-negative integer weight _b<sub>v</sub>_}@}. {@{The weight vector}@} is denoted by {@{__b__}@}. {@{The ___b__-weight_ of a vertex-cover}@} is {@{the sum of _b<sub>v</sub>_ for all _v_ in the cover}@}. (annotation: Because {@{the earlier $w$-vertex-cover}@} is already {@{defined as a vertex multiset with multiplicity $y_v$ copies of vertex $v$}@}, here {@{the $b$-weight summation is already over the multiset with multiplicity considered}@}.) {@{A ___b__-matching_}@} is {@{an assignment of a non-negative integral weight to each edge}@}, such that {@{the sum of weights of edges adjacent to any vertex _v_ is at most _b<sub>v</sub>_}@}. (annotation: Here, {@{a $b$-matching}@} treats {@{the chosen edge weights as an edge multiset}@} with {@{multiplicity $x_e \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ copies of each edge $e$}@}, bounded at {@{every vertex $v$ by $\sum_{e \ni v} x_e \le b_v$}@}, which is {@{_different_ from the fixed per-edge weight $w_e$}@} summed as {@{$\sum_{e \in E} w_e x_e = w \cdot x$ in the matching objective}@}.) {@{Egerváry's theorem}@} can be {@{extended, using a similar argument}@}, (annotation: by {@{strong LP duality theorem and that the graph is bipartite}@}) to {@{graphs that have both edge-weights __w__ and vertex-weights __b__}@}:<sup>[\[18\]](#^ref-18)</sup>

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; _In any {@{edge-weighted vertex-weighted bipartite graph}@}, {@{the maximum __w-__<!-- markdown separator -->weight of a __b__-matching}@} equals {@{the minimum __b__-weight of vertices in a __w-__<!-- markdown separator -->vertex-cover}@}._ (annotation: {@{The theorem for edge-weighted graphs}@} is simply {@{a special case where every vertex has the weight 1}@}. In {@{its LP formulation}@}, {@{___1__<sub>V</sub>_ is replaced by ___b___}@}.)
