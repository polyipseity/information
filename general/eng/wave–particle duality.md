## history

### wave–particle duality of light

In {@{the late 17th century, Sir [Isaac Newton](Isaac%20Newton.md)}@} had advocated that {@{light was [corpuscular](corpuscular%20theory%20of%20light.md) \(particulate\)}@}, but {@{[Christiaan Huygens](Christiaan%20Huygens.md) took an opposing wave description}@}. While Newton had {@{favored a particle approach}@}, he was the first to {@{attempt to reconcile both wave and particle theories of light}@}, and {@{the only one in his time to consider both}@}, thereby anticipating {@{modern wave–particle duality}@}.<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup><sup>[\[4\]](#^ref-4)</sup><sup>[\[5\]](#^ref-5)</sup> {@{[Thomas Young](Thomas%20Young%20(scientist).md)'s [interference experiments](Young's%20interference%20experiment.md) in 1801}@}, and {@{[François Arago](François%20Arago.md)'s detection of the [Poisson spot](Poisson%20spot.md) in 1819}@}, validated {@{Huygens' wave models}@}. However, {@{the wave model}@} was challenged in {@{1901 by [Planck's law](Planck's%20law.md) for [black-body radiation](black-body%20radiation.md)}@}.<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup> {@{[Max Planck](Max%20Planck.md) heuristically derived a formula}@} for {@{the observed spectrum}@} by assuming that {@{a hypothetical electrically charged [oscillator](oscillation.md) in a cavity}@} that contained {@{black-body radiation}@} could only {@{change its [energy](energy.md) in a minimal increment, _E_}@}, that was {@{proportional to the frequency of its associated [electromagnetic wave](electromagnetic%20wave.md)}@}. In {@{1905 [Albert Einstein](Albert%20Einstein.md) interpreted the [photoelectric effect](photoelectric%20effect.md)}@} also with {@{discrete energies for photons}@}.<sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup> These both indicate {@{particle behavior}@}. Despite {@{confirmation by various experimental observations}@}, {@{the [photon](photon.md) theory \(as it came to be called\) remained controversial}@} until {@{[Arthur Compton](Arthur%20Compton.md) performed a [series of experiments](Compton%20effect.md) from 1922 to 1924 demonstrating the momentum of light}@}.<sup>[\[8\]](#^ref-8)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;211&hairsp;</sup> {@{The experimental evidence of particle-like momentum and energy}@} seemingly contradicted {@{the earlier work demonstrating wave-like interference of light}@}. In {@{1927, the wave nature of electrons}@} was {@{empirically confirmed by two experiments}@}. {@{The [Davisson–Germer experiment](Davisson–Germer%20experiment.md) at Bell Labs}@} measured {@{electrons scattered from [Ni metal](nickel.md) surfaces}@}.<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup><sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup><sup>[\[15\]](#^ref-15)</sup><sup>[\[16\]](#^ref-16)</sup><sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup> {@{[George Paget Thomson](George%20Paget%20Thomson.md) and Alexander Reid at Cambridge University}@} scattered {@{electrons through thin [nickel](nickel.md) films and observed concentric diffraction rings}@}.<sup>[\[18\]](#^ref-18)</sup> {@{Alexander Reid, who was Thomson's graduate student}@}, performed {@{the first experiments}@},<sup>[\[19\]](#^ref-19)</sup> but he {@{died soon after in a motorcycle accident<sup>[\[20\]](#^ref-20)</sup> and is rarely mentioned}@}. These experiments were rapidly followed by {@{the first non-relativistic diffraction model for electrons}@} by {@{[Hans Bethe](Hans%20Bethe.md)<sup>[\[21\]](#^ref-21)</sup> based upon the [Schrödinger equation](Schrödinger%20equation.md)}@}, which is very {@{close to how electron diffraction is now described}@}. Significantly, {@{Davisson and Germer noticed<sup>[\[16\]](#^ref-16)</sup><sup>[\[17\]](#^ref-17)</sup> that their results}@} could not be {@{interpreted using a [Bragg's law](Bragg's%20law.md) approach}@} as {@{the positions were systematically different}@}; the approach of {@{Bethe,<sup>[\[21\]](#^ref-21)</sup> which includes the refraction due to the average potential}@}, yielded {@{more accurate results}@}. {@{Davisson and Thomson were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1937}@} for {@{the experimental verification of wave properties of electrons by diffraction
