In {@{[engineering](engineering.md), [applied mathematics](applied%20mathematics.md), and [physics](physics.md)}@}, {@{the __Buckingham _π_ theorem__}@} is {@{a key [theorem](theorem.md) in [dimensional analysis](dimensional%20analysis.md)}@}. It is {@{a formalisation of [Rayleigh's method of dimensional analysis](Rayleigh's%20method%20of%20dimensional%20analysis.md#Rayleigh's%20method)}@}. Loosely, {@{the theorem states that}@} if there is {@{a physically meaningful equation involving a certain number _n_ of physical variables}@}, then {@{the original equation can be rewritten}@} in terms of a set of {@{_p_ = _n_ − _k_ dimensionless parameters _π_<sub>1</sub>, _π_<sub>2</sub>, ..., _π_<sub>_p_</sub> constructed from the original variables}@}, where _k_ is {@{the number of physical dimensions involved}@}; (annotation: Intuitively and roughly speaking, {@{each independent dimension}@} reduces {@{the degree of freedom by 1}@}.) it is obtained as {@{the [rank](rank%20(matrix%20theory).md) of a particular [matrix](matrix%20(mathematics).md)}@}.

{@{The theorem provides a method}@} for computing {@{sets of dimensionless parameters from the given variables, or [nondimensionalization](nondimensionalization.md)}@}, even if {@{the form of the equation is still unknown}@}.

{@{The Buckingham _π_ theorem}@} indicates that {@{validity of the [laws of physics](scientific%20law.md#laws%20of%20physics)}@} does not {@{depend on a specific [unit](unit%20of%20measurement.md) system}@}. {@{A statement of this theorem}@} is that {@{any physical law can be expressed as an [identity](identity%20(mathematics).md)}@} involving {@{only dimensionless combinations \(ratios or products\) of the variables linked by the law}@} \(for example, {@{pressure and volume}@} are {@{linked by [Boyle's law](Boyle's%20law.md)}@} – they are {@{[inversely proportional](inversely%20proportional.md#inverse%20proportionality)}@}\). If {@{the dimensionless combinations' values changed with the systems of units}@}, then {@{the equation would not be an identity}@}, and {@{the theorem would not hold}@}.

## history

{@{[Leonhard Euler](Leonhard%20Euler.md) was the first}@} to write {@{extensively about dimensional analysis for physical relations in 1765}@}. {@{[Joseph Fourier](Joseph%20Fourier.md) also wrote}@} about {@{the [principle of dimensional homogeneity](principle%20of%20dimensional%20homogeneity.md#dimensional%20homogeneity) in 1822}@}, and {@{[Lord Rayleigh](Lord%20Rayleigh.md) proposed, in 1877}@}, {@{a "method of dimensions" for analyzing physical systems}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup> {@{[Edgar Buckingham](Edgar%20Buckingham.md), a physicist at the American [National Bureau of Standards](National%20Bureau%20of%20Standards.md)}@},<sup>[\[2\]](#^ref-2)</sup> formally outlined {@{the _π_ theorem for an arbitrary number of quantities}@}. {@{Buckingham's article}@} introduced the use of {@{the symbol "$\pi _{i}$" for the dimensionless variables \(or parameters\)}@}, and this is {@{the source of the theorem's name}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup> However, {@{a French scientist, [A. Vaschy](A.%20Vaschy.md) \[[fr](..%5Ffra%5FAimé%20Vaschy.md)\]}@}, had {@{previously outlined a similar theorem in 1878}@}, as had {@{[Dimitri Riabouchinsky](Dimitri%20Riabouchinsky.md) in 1911}@}.<sup>[\[1\]](#^ref-1)</sup>

Although {@{named for [Edgar Buckingham](Edgar%20Buckingham.md)}@}, {@{the _π_ theorem was first proved}@} by {@{the French mathematician [Joseph Bertrand](Joseph%20Louis%20François%20Bertrand.md) in 1878}@}.<sup>[\[3\]](#^ref-3)</sup> Bertrand considered only {@{special cases of problems from electrodynamics and heat conduction}@}, but his article contains, {@{in distinct terms, all the basic ideas of the modern proof of the theorem}@} and clearly indicates {@{the theorem's utility for modelling physical phenomena}@}. {@{The technique of using the theorem \("the method of dimensions"\)}@} became {@{widely known due to the works of [Rayleigh](John%20Strutt,%203rd%20Baron%20Rayleigh.md)}@}. {@{The first application of the _π_ theorem _in the general case_}@}<sup>[\[note 1\]](#^note-1)</sup> to {@{the dependence of pressure drop in a pipe upon governing parameters}@} probably dates back to {@{1892,<sup>[\[4\]](#^ref-4)</sup> a heuristic proof with the use of series expansions, to 1894}@}.<sup>[\[5\]](#^ref-5)</sup><sup>\[_[improper synthesis?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No%20original%20research#Synthesis_of_published_material)_\]</sup>

{@{Formal generalization of the _π_ theorem}@} for {@{the case of arbitrarily many quantities}@} was given first by {@{[A. Vaschy](A.%20Vaschy.md) \[[fr](..%5Ffra%5FAimé%20Vaschy.md)\] in 1892}@},<sup>[\[6\]](#^ref-6)</sup><sup>[\[7\]](#^ref-7)</sup> then in {@{1911—apparently independently—by both A. Federman<sup>[\[8\]](#^ref-8)</sup> and [D. Riabouchinsky](Dimitri%20Riabouchinsky.md)}@},<sup>[\[9\]](#^ref-9)</sup> and again in {@{1914 by Buckingham}@}.<sup>[\[10\]](#^ref-10)</sup><sup>\[_[improper synthesis?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No%20original%20research#Synthesis_of_published_material)_\]</sup>

## statement

More formally, {@{the number $p$ of dimensionless terms that can be formed}@} is equal to {@{the [nullity](kernel%20(linear%20algebra).md#nullity) of the [dimensional matrix](#formal%20proof)}@}, and $k$ is {@{the [rank](rank%20(linear%20algebra).md)}@}. For {@{experimental purposes}@}, {@{different systems that share the same description in terms of these [dimensionless numbers](dimensionless%20number.md)}@} are {@{equivalent}@}.

In {@{mathematical terms}@}, if we have {@{a physically meaningful equation}@} such as {@{$$f(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots ,q_{n})=0,$$}@} where {@{$q_{1},\ldots ,q_{n}$ are any $n$ physical variables}@}, and there is {@{a maximal dimensionally independent subset of size $k$}@},<sup>[\[note 2\]](#^note-2)</sup> then {@{the above equation can be restated}@} as {@{$$F(\pi _{1},\pi _{2},\ldots ,\pi _{p})=0,$$}@} where {@{$\pi _{1},\ldots ,\pi _{p}$ are dimensionless parameters constructed from the $q_{i}$}@} by {@{$p=n-k$ dimensionless equations — the so-called _Pi groups_}@} — of the form {@{$$\pi _{i}=q_{1}^{a_{1} }\,q_{2}^{a_{2} }\cdots q_{n}^{a_{n} },$$}@} where {@{the exponents $a_{i}$ are rational numbers}@}. \(They can always be {@{taken to be integers}@} by redefining {@{$\pi _{i}$ as being raised to a power that clears all denominators}@}.\) If there are {@{$\ell$ fundamental units in play}@}, then {@{$p\geq n-\ell$}@}.

## significance

{@{The Buckingham _π_ theorem}@} provides {@{a method for computing sets of dimensionless parameters from given variables}@}, even if {@{the form of the equation remains unknown}@}. However, {@{the choice of dimensionless parameters}@} is {@{not unique}@}; {@{Buckingham's theorem}@} only provides {@{a way of generating sets of dimensionless parameters}@} and does not {@{indicate the most "physically meaningful"}@}.

{@{Two systems for which these parameters coincide}@} are called {@{_similar_ \(as with [similar triangles](similar%20triangles.md#similar%20triangles), they differ only in scale\)}@}; they are {@{equivalent for the purposes of the equation}@}, and {@{the experimentalist who wants to determine the form of the equation}@} can choose {@{the most convenient one}@}. Most importantly, {@{Buckingham's theorem}@} describes {@{the relation between the number of variables and fundamental dimensions}@}.

## similarity theory

{@{The Buckingham pi theorem}@} is {@{fundamental to __similarity theory__}@}, {@{an empirical procedure for establishing}@} {@{relationships between variables in a physical system that reflect characteristics observed in related systems}@}. For example, {@{experiments measuring buoyancy flux in an atmosphere model}@} may show {@{a series of similar curves}@}, but {@{the physical origin of the similarity may not be known}@}. {@{The procedure of similarity theory}@} guides {@{the selection of a formula for reproducing the curves even absent the physical model}@}.<sup>[\[11\]](#^ref-11)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;347&hairsp;</sup>

{@{Similarity theory}@} is used {@{[fluid dynamics](fluid%20dynamics.md), with specific classes of similarity}@} such as {@{[Monin-Obukhov similarity theory](Monin-Obukhov%20similarity%20theory.md) for surface layers}@} or {@{[Rossby number](Rossby%20number.md) similarity for analyzing atmospheric phenomena like tornadoes}@}.<sup>[\[11\]](#^ref-11)</sup><sup>:&hairsp;358&hairsp;</sup>

## proof

For {@{simplicity, it will be assumed}@} that {@{the space of fundamental and derived physical units}@} forms {@{a [vector space](vector%20space.md) over the [real numbers](real%20number.md)}@}, with {@{the fundamental units}@} as {@{basis vectors}@}, and with {@{multiplication of physical units}@} as {@{the "vector addition" operation}@}, and {@{raising to powers}@} as {@{the "[scalar multiplication](scalar%20multiplication.md)" operation}@}: represent {@{a dimensional variable}@} as {@{the set of exponents needed for the fundamental units}@} \(with {@{a power of zero if the particular fundamental unit is not present}@}\). For instance, {@{the [standard gravity](standard%20gravity.md) $g$}@} has units of {@{${\mathsf {L} }/{\mathsf {T} }^{2}={\mathsf {L} }^{1}{\mathsf {T} }^{-2}$ \(length over time squared\)}@}, so it is represented as {@{the vector $(1,-2)$ with respect to the basis of fundamental units \(length, time\)}@}. We could also require that {@{exponents of the fundamental units}@} be {@{rational numbers and modify the proof accordingly}@}, in which case {@{the exponents in the pi groups}@} can always be {@{taken as rational numbers or integers}@}.

### rescaling units

Suppose we have {@{quantities $q_{1},q_{2},\dots ,q_{n}$}@}, where {@{the units of $q_{i}$}@} contain {@{length raised to the power $c_{i}$}@}. If we originally {@{measure length in meters but later switch to centimeters}@}, then {@{the numerical value of $q_{i}$}@} would be {@{rescaled by a factor of $100^{c_{i} }$}@}. {@{Any physically meaningful law}@} should be {@{invariant under an arbitrary rescaling of every fundamental unit}@}; this is {@{the fact that the pi theorem hinges on}@}.

### formal proof

Given {@{a system of $n$ dimensional variables $q_{1},\ldots ,q_{n}$}@} in {@{$\ell$ fundamental \(basis\) dimensions}@}, {@{the _dimensional matrix_}@} is {@{the $\ell \times n$ matrix $M$}@} whose {@{$\ell$ rows correspond to the fundamental dimensions}@} and whose {@{$n$ columns are the dimensions of the variables}@}: {@{the $(i,j)$ th entry \(where $1\leq i\leq \ell$ and $1\leq j\leq n$\)}@} is {@{the power of the $i$<!-- markdown separator -->th fundamental dimension in the $j$<!-- markdown separator -->th variable}@}. {@{The matrix can be interpreted}@} as taking in {@{a combination of the variable quantities}@} and giving out {@{the dimensions of the combination in terms of the fundamental dimensions}@}. So {@{the $\ell \times 1$ \(column\) vector that results}@} from {@{the multiplication $$M{\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\\vdots \\a_{n}\end{bmatrix} }$$}@} consists of {@{the units of $$q_{1}^{a_{1} }\,q_{2}^{a_{2} }\cdots q_{n}^{a_{n} }$$}@} in terms of {@{the $\ell$ fundamental independent \(basis\) units}@}.<sup>[\[note 3\]](#^note-3)</sup>

If we rescale {@{the $i$<!-- markdown separator -->th fundamental unit by a factor of $\alpha _{i}$}@}, then {@{$q_{j}$ gets rescaled}@} by {@{$\alpha _{1}^{-m_{1j} }\,\alpha _{2}^{-m_{2j} }\cdots \alpha _{\ell }^{-m_{\ell j} }$}@}, where $m_{ij}$ is {@{the $(i,j)$ th entry of the dimensional matrix}@}. In order to {@{convert this into a [linear algebra](linear%20algebra.md) problem}@}, we take {@{[logarithms](logarithm.md) \(the base is irrelevant\) (annotation: to convert multiplications to additions)}@}, yielding {@{$${\begin{bmatrix}\log {q_{1} }\\\vdots \\\log {q_{n} }\end{bmatrix} }\mapsto {\begin{bmatrix}\log {q_{1} }\\\vdots \\\log {q_{n} }\end{bmatrix} }-M^{\operatorname {T} }{\begin{bmatrix}\log {\alpha _{1} }\\\vdots \\\log {\alpha _{\ell } }\end{bmatrix} },$$}@} which is {@{an [action](group%20action.md) of $\mathbb {R} ^{\ell }$ on $\mathbb {R} ^{n}$}@}. We define {@{a physical law to be an arbitrary function $f\colon (\mathbb {R} ^{+})^{n}\to \mathbb {R}$}@} such that {@{$(q_{1},q_{2},\dots ,q_{n})$ is a permissible set of values for the [physical system](physical%20system.md) when $f(q_{1},q_{2},\dots ,q_{n})=0$}@}. We further require {@{$f$ to be invariant under this action}@}. Hence it descends to {@{a function $F\colon \mathbb {R} ^{n}/\operatorname {im} {M^{\operatorname {T} } }\to \mathbb {R}$}@}. (annotation: Because {@{$f: (\mathbb{R}^+)^n \to \mathbb{R}$}@} is {@{invariant under the scaling action}@}, it {@{descends to the quotient space as $F([\log q_1, \dots, \log q_n]) = f(q_1, \dots, q_n)$}@} for any {@{equivalence class $[\log q_1, \dots, \log q_n] \in \mathbb{R}^n / \mathrm{im}\, M^{\mathrm{T} }$}@}.) {@{All that remains}@} is to exhibit {@{an isomorphism between $\mathbb {R} ^{n}/\operatorname {im} {M^{\operatorname {T} } }$ and $\mathbb {R} ^{p}$}@}, {@{the \(log\) space of pi groups}@} {@{$(\log {\pi _{1} },\log {\pi _{2} },\dots ,\log {\pi _{p} })$}@}.

We construct {@{an $n\times p$ matrix $K$ (annotation: $p = n - k$ where $k$ is the rank of $M$)}@} whose {@{columns are a basis for $\ker {M}$}@}. (annotation: Intuitively, {@{$\operatorname{im} M^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^n / \operatorname{im} M^\top \cong \mathbb{R}^p$}@} is {@{the most obvious thing that can give us a starting point for a proof}@}. It is harder to {@{directly work with $\operatorname{im} M^\top$}@}, so recall {@{the relationship $\operatorname{im} M^\top = (\operatorname{ker} M)^\perp$}@}. This hints at {@{working directly with $\operatorname{ker} M$ instead}@}.) It tells us how to {@{embed $\mathbb {R} ^{p}$ into $\mathbb {R} ^{n}$ as the kernel of $M$}@}. That is, we have {@{an [exact sequence](exact%20sequence.md)}@} {@{$$0\to \mathbb {R} ^{p}\xrightarrow {\ K\ } \mathbb {R} ^{n}\xrightarrow {\ M\ } \mathbb {R} ^{\ell }.$$}@} (annotation: It is exact because $K$ is {@{injective with $\operatorname{im} K = \ker M$}@}, and $M$ is {@{a linear map whose kernel matches the image of the preceding map}@}.) {@{Taking tranposes (annotation: which reverses the arrow directions)}@} yields {@{another exact sequence}@} {@{$$\mathbb {R} ^{\ell }\xrightarrow {\ M^{\operatorname {T} }\ } \mathbb {R} ^{n}\xrightarrow {\ K^{\operatorname {T} }\ } \mathbb {R} ^{p}\to 0.$$}@} (annotation: For {@{adjacent linear maps $A: V \to W$ and $B: W \to U$}@} with {@{$\operatorname{im} A = \ker B$}@}, passing to {@{the dual spaces gives $B^*: U^* \to W^*$ and $A^*: W^* \to V^*$}@}, where {@{the Riesz representation theorem for _real_ vector spaces}@} allows working {@{directly with $A$ and $B$ through their computational transposes $B^\top$ and $A^\top$}@}. Viewing {@{matrix rows as input detectors that detect via dot products}@}, {@{a vector $w \in W$ lies in $\ker B$}@} {@{if and only if it is orthogonal to every row detector}@}, which upon {@{transposition become columns spanning $\operatorname{im} B^\top$}@}, yielding {@{$\operatorname{im} B^\top = (\ker B)^\perp$}@}. Analogously, because {@{$\operatorname{im} A$ is spanned by the columns of $A$}@}, {@{a row functional lies in $\ker A^\top$}@} {@{if and only if it evaluates to zero on all columns}@}, giving {@{$\ker A^\top = (\operatorname{im} A)^\perp$}@}. Substituting {@{the exactness condition $\operatorname{im} A = \ker B$}@} results in {@{$\operatorname{im} B^\top = (\ker B)^\perp = (\operatorname{im} A)^\perp = \ker A^\top$}@}, confirming that {@{the transposed sequence is exact in the reverse direction}@}.) {@{The [first isomorphism theorem](isomorphism%20theorems.md#modules)}@} produces {@{the desired isomorphism}@}, which sends {@{the [coset](coset.md#vectors) $v+M^{\operatorname {T} }\mathbb {R} ^{\ell }$ to $K^{\operatorname {T} }v$}@}. (annotation: Applying {@{the first isomorphism theorem to the surjective map $K^\top: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p$}@} equates {@{the domain modulo its kernel, $\mathbb{R}^n / \ker K^\top$}@}, to its {@{image $\mathbb{R}^p$}@}. Substituting {@{the exactness condition $\ker K^\top = \operatorname{im} M^\top$}@} establishes {@{the quotient space isomorphism $\mathbb{R}^n / \operatorname{im} M^\top \cong \mathbb{R}^p$}@}.) This corresponds to rewriting {@{the tuple $(\log q_{1},\log q_{2},\dots ,\log q_{n})$}@} into {@{the pi groups $(\log \pi _{1},\log \pi _{2},\dots ,\log \pi _{p})$ coming from the columns of $K$}@}.

{@{The [International System of Units](International%20System%20of%20Units.md)}@} defines {@{seven base units}@}, which are {@{the [ampere](ampere.md), [kelvin](kelvin.md), [second](second.md)}@}, {@{[metre](metre.md), [kilogram](kilogram.md), [candela](candela.md) and [mole](mole%20(unit).md)}@}. It is sometimes {@{advantageous to introduce additional base units and techniques}@} to refine {@{the technique of dimensional analysis}@}. \(See {@{[orientational analysis](orientational%20analysis.md#Siano's%20extension%20orientational%20analysis)}@} and reference.<sup>[\[12\]](#^ref-12)</sup>\)

## examples

### speed

{@{This example (annotation: of speed)}@} is {@{elementary but serves to demonstrate the procedure}@}.

Suppose {@{a car}@} is {@{driving at 100 km/h}@}; how long does it {@{take to go 200 km}@}?

This question considers {@{$n=3$ dimensioned variables}@}: {@{distance $d$, time $t$, and speed $v$}@}, and we are seeking {@{some law of the form $t=\operatorname {Duration} (v,d)$}@}. {@{Any two of these variables}@} are {@{dimensionally independent}@}, but {@{the three taken together are not}@}. Thus there is {@{$p=n-k=3-2=1$ dimensionless quantity}@}.

{@{The dimensional matrix}@} is {@{$$M={\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\;\;\;1\\0&1&-1\end{bmatrix} }$$}@} in which the rows correspond to {@{the basis dimensions ${\mathsf {L} }$}@} and ${\mathsf {T} }$, and the columns to {@{the considered dimensions ${\mathsf {L} }$, ${\mathsf {T} }$, and ${\mathsf {V} }$}@}, where the latter {@{stands for the speed dimension}@}. {@{The elements of the matrix}@} correspond to {@{the powers to which the respective dimensions are to be raised}@}. For instance, {@{the third column $(1,-1)$}@}, states that {@{${\mathsf {V} }={\mathsf {L} }^{0}{\mathsf {T} }^{0}{\mathsf {V} }^{1}$}@}, represented by {@{the column vector $\mathbf {v} =[0,0,1]$}@}, is expressible {@{in terms of the basis dimensions}@} as {@{${\mathsf {V} }={\mathsf {L} }^{1}{\mathsf {T} }^{-1}={\mathsf {L} }/{\mathsf {T} }$}@}, since {@{$M\mathbf {v} =[1,-1]$}@}.

For {@{a dimensionless constant $\pi ={\mathsf {L} }^{a_{1} }{\mathsf {T} }^{a_{2} }{\mathsf {V} }^{a_{3} }$}@}, we are looking for {@{vectors $\mathbf {a} =[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}]$}@} such that {@{the matrix-vector product $M\mathbf {a}$ equals the zero vector $[0,0]$}@}. In {@{linear algebra, the set of vectors with this property}@} is known as {@{the [kernel](kernel%20(linear%20algebra).md) \(or nullspace\) of the dimensional matrix}@}. In this particular case its {@{kernel is one-dimensional}@}. {@{The dimensional matrix as written above}@} is in {@{[reduced row echelon form](reduced%20row%20echelon%20form.md#reduced%20row%20echelon%20form)}@}, so one can {@{read off a non-zero kernel vector to within a multiplicative constant}@}: {@{$$\mathbf {a} ={\begin{bmatrix}-1\\\;\;\;1\\\;\;\;1\\\end{bmatrix} }.$$}@} (annotation: From {@{the reduced row echelon form matrix}@}, {@{column 3}@} corresponds to {@{the free variable $x_3$}@} while {@{columns 1 and 2 contain the pivots}@}, meaning you {@{set $x_3 = 1$ and directly read off the dependent variables}@} by negating {@{the entries of that non-pivot column}@} to yield {@{the null space vector $\mathbf{a} = [-1, 1, 1]^\mathrm{T}$}@}.)

If {@{the dimensional matrix were not already reduced}@}, one could perform {@{[Gauss–Jordan elimination](Gauss–Jordan%20elimination.md#Gauss–Jordan%20elimination) on the dimensional matrix to more easily determine the kernel}@}. It follows that {@{the dimensionless constant}@}, replacing {@{the dimensions by the corresponding dimensioned variables}@}, may be written: {@{$$\pi =d^{-1}t^{1}v^{1}=tv/d.$$}@} Since {@{the kernel is only defined to within a multiplicative constant}@}, {@{the above dimensionless constant raised to any arbitrary power}@} yields {@{another \(equivalent\) dimensionless constant}@}.

{@{Dimensional analysis}@} has thus provided {@{a general equation relating the three physical variables}@}: {@{$$F(\pi )=0,$$}@} or, letting {@{$C$ denote a [zero](zero%20of%20a%20function.md) of function $F$}@}, {@{$$\pi =C,$$}@} which can be written in {@{the desired form \(which recall was $t=\operatorname {Duration} (v,d)$\)}@} as {@{$$t=C{\frac {d}{v} }.$$}@} {@{The actual relationship between the three variables}@} is {@{simply $d=vt$}@}. In other words, in this case $F$ has {@{one physically relevant root, and it is unity}@}. The fact that {@{only a single value of $C$ will do and that it is equal to 1}@} is not {@{revealed by the technique of dimensional analysis}@}.

### the simple pendulum

We wish to determine {@{the period $\tau$ of [small oscillations in a simple pendulum](pendulum%20(mechanics).md#small-angle%20approximation)}@}. It will be assumed that it is {@{a function of the length $\ell$, the mass $m$, and the [acceleration due to gravity](standard%20gravity.md) on the surface of the Earth $g$}@}, which has {@{dimensions of length divided by time squared}@}. {@{The model is of the form}@} {@{$$f(\tau ,m,\ell ,g)=0.$$}@} \(Note that it is {@{written as a relation, not as a function}@}: $\tau$ is not {@{written here as a function of $m$, $\ell$, and $g$}@}.\)

{@{Period, mass, and length}@} are {@{dimensionally independent}@}, but {@{acceleration}@} can be {@{expressed in terms of time and length}@}, which means {@{the four variables taken together are not dimensionally independent}@}. Thus we need only {@{$p=n-k=4-3=1$ dimensionless parameter, denoted by $\pi$}@}, and the model can be {@{re-expressed as $$F(\pi )=0,$$}@} where $\pi$ is given by {@{$$\pi =\tau ^{a_{1} }m^{a_{2} }\ell ^{a_{3} }g^{a_{4} }$$}@} for {@{some values of $a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}$}@}.

The dimensions of the dimensional quantities are: $${\begin{aligned}\tau &={\mathsf {T} },&m&={\mathsf {M} },\\\ell &={\mathsf {L} },&g&={\mathsf {L} }/{\mathsf {T} }^{2}.\end{aligned} }$$

{@{The dimensional matrix}@} is: {@{$$\mathbf {M} ={\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&-2\\0&1&0&0\\0&0&1&1\end{bmatrix} }.$$}@} \({@{The rows}@} correspond to {@{the dimensions ${\mathsf {T} }$, ${\mathsf {M} }$ and ${\mathsf {L} }$}@}, and {@{the columns}@} to {@{the dimensional variables $\tau$, $m$, $\ell$, and $g$}@}. For instance, {@{the 4th column, $(-2,0,1)$}@}, states that {@{the $g$ variable has dimensions of ${\mathsf {T} }^{-2}{\mathsf {M} }^{0}{\mathsf {L} }^{1}$}@}.\)

We are looking for {@{a kernel vector $a=\left[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\right]$}@} such that {@{the matrix product of $\mathbf {M}$ on $a$ yields the zero vector $[0,0,0]$}@}. {@{The dimensional matrix as written above}@} is {@{in reduced row echelon form}@}, so one can {@{read off a kernel vector within a multiplicative constant}@}: {@{$$a={\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix} }.$$}@} (annotation: Again, set {@{the free variable in the last coordinate to 1}@} and then {@{fill in the remaining coordinates}@}.) Were it not {@{already reduced, one could perform [Gauss–Jordan elimination](Gauss–Jordan%20elimination.md#Gauss–Jordan%20elimination)}@} on {@{the dimensional matrix to more easily determine the kernel}@}. It follows that {@{the dimensionless constant may be written}@}: {@{$${\begin{aligned}\pi &=\tau ^{2}m^{0}\ell ^{-1}g^{1}\\&=g\tau ^{2}/\ell \end{aligned} }.$$}@} In {@{fundamental terms}@}: {@{$$\pi =({\mathsf {T} })^{2}({\mathsf {M} })^{0}({\mathsf {L} })^{-1}\left({\mathsf {L} }/{\mathsf {T} }^{2}\right)^{1}=1,$$}@} which is {@{dimensionless}@}. Since {@{the kernel is only defined to within a multiplicative constant}@}, if {@{the above dimensionless constant is raised to any arbitrary power}@}, it will yield {@{another equivalent dimensionless constant}@}.

In this example, {@{three of the four dimensional quantities}@} are {@{fundamental units}@}, so {@{the last \(which is $g$\)}@} must be {@{a combination of the previous}@}. Note that if {@{$a_{2}$ \(the coefficient of $m$\) had been non-zero}@} then there would be {@{no way to cancel the $m$ value}@}; therefore {@{$a_{2}$ _must_ be zero}@}. {@{Dimensional analysis}@} has allowed us to conclude that {@{the period of the pendulum is not a function of its mass $m$}@}. \(In {@{the 3D space of powers of mass, time, and distance}@}, we can say that {@{the vector for mass}@} is {@{linearly independent from the vectors for the three other variables}@}. Up to {@{a scaling factor, ${\vec {g} }+2{\vec {\tau } }-{\vec {\ell } }$}@} is {@{the only nontrivial way to construct a vector of a dimensionless parameter}@}.\)

{@{The model}@} can now be expressed as: {@{$$F{\left(g\tau ^{2}/\ell \right)}=0.$$}@} Then this implies that {@{$g\tau ^{2}/\ell =C_{i}$ for some zero $C_{i}$ of the function $F$}@}. If {@{there is only one zero}@}, call it {@{$C$, then $g\tau ^{2}/\ell =C$}@}. It requires {@{more physical insight or an experiment}@} to show that there is {@{indeed only one zero and that the constant is in fact given by $C=4\pi ^{2}$}@}. (annotation: Recall {@{the natural frequency}@} is {@{$\omega = \sqrt{\frac {mg\ell} {m\ell^2} } = \sqrt{\frac g \ell}$}@}. Rearranging gives {@{$1 = \frac {g} {\ell \omega^2}$}@} and applying {@{$\tau = \frac {2\pi} \omega$}@} gives {@{$4\pi^2 = \frac {g\tau^2} \ell$}@}.)

For {@{large oscillations of a pendulum}@}, the analysis is {@{complicated by an additional dimensionless parameter, the maximum swing angle}@}. {@{The above analysis}@} is {@{a good approximation as the [angle approaches zero](small%20angle%20approximation.md)}@}.

### electric power

To demonstrate {@{the application of the _π_ theorem}@}, consider {@{the [power](electric%20power.md) consumption of a [stirrer](electric%20generator.md) with a given shape}@}. {@{The power, _P_}@}, in {@{dimensions \[M · L<sup>2</sup>/T<sup>3</sup>\]}@}, is {@{a function of the [density](density.md)}@}, {@{_ρ_ \[M/L<sup>3</sup>\]}@}, and {@{the [viscosity](viscosity.md) of the fluid to be stirred}@}, {@{_μ_ \[M/\(L · T\)\]}@}, as well as {@{the size of the stirrer given by its [diameter](diameter.md)}@}, {@{_D_ \[L\]}@}, and {@{the [angular speed](angular%20velocity.md) of the stirrer}@}, {@{_ω_ \[1/T\]}@}. Therefore, we have {@{a total of _n_ = 5 variables representing our example}@}. {@{Those _n_ = 5 variables}@} are {@{built up from _k_ = 3 independent dimensions}@}, e.g., {@{length: L \([SI](SI.md) units: [m](meters.md)\), time: T \([s](second.md)\), and mass: M \([kg](kilograms.md)\)}@}.

According to {@{the _π_-theorem}@}, {@{the _n_ = 5 variables can be reduced}@} by {@{the _k_ = 3 dimensions to form _p_ = _n_ − _k_ = 5 − 3 = 2 independent dimensionless numbers}@}. Usually, {@{these quantities}@} are chosen as {@{$\mathrm {Re} ={\frac {\rho \omega D^{2} }{\mu } }$}@}, commonly named {@{the [Reynolds number](Reynolds%20number.md) which describes the fluid flow regime}@}, and {@{$N_{\mathrm {p} }={\frac {P}{\rho \omega ^{3}D^{5} } }$}@}, {@{the [power number](power%20number.md), which is the dimensionless description of the stirrer}@}.

Note that {@{the two dimensionless quantities}@} are {@{not unique}@} and depend on {@{which of the _n_ = 5 variables are chosen as the _k_ = 3 dimensionally independent basis variables}@}, which, in this example, appear in {@{both dimensionless quantities}@}. {@{The Reynolds number and power number}@} fall from the above analysis if {@{$\rho$, $\omega$, and _D_ are chosen to be the basis variables}@}. If, instead, {@{$\mu$, $\omega$, and _D_ are selected}@}, {@{the Reynolds number is recovered}@} while {@{the second dimensionless quantity}@} becomes {@{$N_{\mathrm {Rep} }={\frac {P}{\mu D^{3}\omega ^{2} } }$}@}. We note that $N_{\mathrm {Rep} }$ is {@{the product of the Reynolds number and the power number}@}.

### other examples

{@{An example of dimensional analysis}@} can be found for {@{the case of the mechanics}@} of {@{a thin, solid and parallel-sided rotating disc}@}. There are {@{five variables involved}@} which {@{reduce to two non-dimensional groups}@}. {@{The relationship between these}@} can be determined by {@{numerical experiment using, for example, the [finite element method](finite%20element%20method.md)}@}.<sup>[\[13\]](#^ref-13)</sup>

{@{The theorem}@} has also been used in {@{fields other than physics}@}, for instance in {@{[sports science](sports%20science.md)}@}.<sup>[\[14\]](#^ref-14)</sup>

### notes

1. When in {@{applying the _π_–theorem}@} there arises {@{an _arbitrary function_ of dimensionless numbers}@}. <a id="^note-1"></a>^note-1
2. {@{A dimensionally independent set of variables}@} is one {@{for which the only exponents $q_{1}^{a_{1} }\,q_{2}^{a_{2} }\cdots q_{k}^{a_{k} }$}@} yielding {@{a dimensionless quantity are $a_{1}=a_{2}=\cdots =0$}@}. This is precisely {@{the notion of [linear independence](linear%20independence.md)}@}. <a id="^note-2"></a>^note-2
3. If {@{these basis units}@} are {@{$b_{1},\ldots ,b_{\ell }$}@} and if {@{the units of $q_{j}=m_{1j}b_{1}+\cdots +m_{\ell j}b_{\ell }$}@} for {@{every $1\leq j\leq n$}@}, then {@{$$M={\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}&\cdots &m_{1j}&\cdots &m_{1n}\\\vdots &&\vdots &&\vdots \\m_{\ell 1}&\cdots &m_{\ell j}&\cdots &m_{\ell n}\\\end{bmatrix} }$$}@} so that, for instance, {@{the units of $q_{1}$ in terms of these basis units}@} are {@{$$M\left(\left[1\ 0\ \cdots \ 0\right]^{\operatorname {T} }\right)={\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}\\\vdots \\m_{\ell 1}\\\end{bmatrix} }.$$}@} For {@{a concrete example}@}, suppose that {@{the $\ell =2$ fundamental units}@} are {@{[meters](meter.md) $b_{1}=m$ and [seconds](second.md) $b_{2}=s$}@}, and that there are {@{$n=3$ dimensional variables}@}: {@{$q_{1}=m/s^{2},q_{2}=1/m,q_{3}=s/m$}@}. By {@{definition of vector addition and scalar multiplication of units}@}, {@{$$q_{1}=ms^{-2}=1m+(-2)s,\quad q_{2}=m^{-1}=(-1)m+0s,\quad {\text{and} }\quad q_{3}=m^{-1}s=(-1)m+1s,$$}@} so that {@{$$M={\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}&m_{12}&m_{13}\\m_{21}&m_{22}&m_{23}\\\end{bmatrix} }={\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&-1\\-2&0&1\\\end{bmatrix} }.$$}@} By definition, {@{the dimensionless variables}@} are {@{those whose units are $m^{0}s^{0}$}@}, which are exactly {@{the vectors in $$\ker M=\operatorname {span} \left\{[1,-1,2]^{\operatorname {T} }\right\}=\left\{\left(q_{1}-q_{2}+2q_{3}\right)^{s}:s\in \mathbb {Q} \right\}.$$}@} This can be verified by {@{a direct computation}@}: {@{$$q_{1}-q_{2}+2q_{3}=\left(ms^{-2}\right)^{1}+\left(m^{-1}\right)^{-1}+\left(sm^{-1}\right)^{2}=m^{1}s^{-2}+m^{1}+m^{-2}s^{2}=m^{1+1+(-2)}s^{-2+0+2}=m^{0}s^{0},$$}@} which is {@{indeed dimensionless}@}. Consequently, if some {@{physical law states that $q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}$ are necessarily related}@} by {@{a \(presumably unknown\) equation of the form $f\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\right)=0$}@} for some {@{\(unknown\) function $f$ with $\operatorname {domain} (f)\subseteq \mathbb {R} ^{3}$}@} \(that is, {@{the tuple $\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\right)$}@} is {@{necessarily a zero of $f$}@}\), then there exists {@{some \(also unknown\) function $F:\mathbb {R} ^{1}\to \mathbb {R}$}@} that depends on {@{only $p=3-2=1$ variable}@}, {@{the dimensionless variable $\pi _{1}:=q_{1}-q_{2}+2q_{3}=q_{1}q_{3}^{2}/q_{2}$}@} \(or any {@{non-zero rational power ${\hat {\pi } }_{1}:=\pi _{1}^{s}$ of $\pi _{1}$, where $0\neq s\in \mathbb {Q}$}@}\), such that {@{$F\left(\pi _{1}\right)=0$ holds}@} \(if {@{${\hat {\pi } }_{1}:=\pi _{1}^{s}$ is used instead of $\pi _{1}$}@} then $F$ can be {@{replaced with ${\hat {F} }(x):=F\left(x^{1/s}\right)$ and once again ${\hat {F} }\left({\hat {\pi } }_{1}\right)=0$ holds}@}\). Thus in {@{terms of the original variables}@}, {@{$F\left(q_{1}q_{3}^{2}/q_{2}\right)=0$ must hold}@} \(alternatively, if using {@{${\hat {\pi } }_{1}:=\pi _{1}^{1/2}={\sqrt {\pi _{1} } }$}@} for instance, then {@{${\hat {F} }\left({\sqrt {q_{1}q_{3}^{2}/q_{2} } }\right)=0$ must hold}@}\). In other words, {@{the Buckingham _π_ theorem}@} implies that {@{$q_{1}q_{3}^{2}/q_{2}\in F^{-1}(0)$}@}, so that if it {@{happens to be the case that this $F$ has exactly one zero}@}, call it {@{$C$}@}, then the equation {@{$q_{1}q_{3}^{2}/q_{2}=C$ will necessarily hold}@} \({@{the theorem}@} does not give {@{information about what the exact value of the constant $C$ will be}@}, nor does it {@{guarantee that $F$ has exactly one zero}@}\). <a id="^note-3"></a>^note-3
